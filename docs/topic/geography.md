# Geography

_6 Fleming-tier papers._

## Synthesis

Together these Fleming-tier works define “Geography” less as descriptive place-cataloguing than as a set of spatial theories for pattern, perception, ecology, and conservation. The earliest lineage is ecological geography: John Curtis’s 1957 ordination of southern Wisconsin forest communities helped formalize gradient-based analysis of vegetation, replacing rigid community typologies with quantitative spatial-environmental continua. MacArthur and Wilson’s *The Theory of Island Biogeography* then supplied a general model linking species richness to area, isolation, colonization, and extinction, making geography central to ecological explanation. Holling’s 1973 work on resilience shifted the emphasis again, from equilibrium and stability to persistence under disturbance, a conceptual move that later shaped landscape ecology, sustainability science, and coupled human-natural systems.

The cluster’s second major lineage concerns spatial cognition, measurement, and intervention. Gibson’s *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* connects geography to psychology by treating perception as direct engagement with structured environments, not merely internal representation. Anselin’s LISA paper transformed spatial statistics by identifying local clusters and outliers rather than relying only on global autocorrelation, giving geography a rigorous toolkit for uneven spatial processes in epidemiology, economics, urban studies, and environmental science. Myers and colleagues’ biodiversity hotspots paper converted biogeographic knowledge into a global conservation prioritization framework, linking island biogeography, endemism, extinction risk, and policy. Collectively, these papers mark paradigm shifts from static regions to dynamic spatial systems, from averages to local heterogeneity, and from ecological theory to actionable global geography.
