# Geography

_6 Fleming-tier papers._

## Synthesis

Together these papers define “Geography” less as place-description than as a set of spatial theories for understanding pattern, perception, ecological organization, and conservation priority. A dominant lineage runs through ecological geography: Curtis and McIntosh’s 1957 ordination of Wisconsin upland forests helped formalize communities as gradients rather than fixed types; MacArthur and Wilson’s *Theory of Island Biogeography* made area, isolation, colonization, and extinction central to explaining species richness; Holling’s work on resilience shifted ecological systems away from equilibrium-only thinking toward persistence under disturbance; and Myers et al.’s biodiversity-hotspots paper translated spatial ecology into a global conservation triage framework.

The major paradigm shifts are therefore from classification to multivariate spatial gradients, from static distributions to dynamic biogeographic processes, from stability as constancy to resilience as adaptive persistence, and from general biodiversity description to priority-setting under scarcity. Anselin’s LISA paper adds the quantitative-geographic lineage: local spatial autocorrelation, clustering, and outlier detection made spatial heterogeneity analytically explicit rather than treating maps as visual backdrops. Gibson’s ecological approach to visual perception connects the cluster to psychology and environmental cognition, emphasizing perception as active attunement to affordances in situated surroundings. Collectively, the papers link geography to ecology, conservation biology, statistics, landscape science, and cognitive science, showing that spatial relations are not merely containers for phenomena but mechanisms that shape them.
