# Materials science

_51 Fleming-tier papers._

## Synthesis

Taken together, these Fleming-tier papers trace materials science from continuum descriptions of matter to atomically designed, device-ready solids. The early lineage begins with rupture, flow, elasticity, soil conductivity, and diffraction refinement: materials as structured continua whose mechanical, hydraulic, and crystallographic behavior could be measured, modeled, and predicted. A second lineage made structure visible and manipulable at finer scales, from monodisperse silica spheres and mesoporous molecular sieves to molecular dynamics, atomic force microscopy, surface-enhanced Raman scattering, and Raman fingerprints of graphite, amorphous carbon, graphene, and layered crystals. This changed the field’s center of gravity from bulk averages to microstructure, surfaces, interfaces, pores, defects, and single nanoscale objects.

The later papers mark a shift from characterization to deliberate materials platforms. Carbon nanotubes, graphene, MoS2, MXenes, phosphorene, boron nitride supports, and van der Waals heterostructures established two-dimensional materials as a new design language for electronics, mechanics, magnetism, and photonics. In parallel, dye-sensitized, organic, polymer, perovskite, photocatalytic, OLED, and triboelectric papers show materials science merging with energy conversion, optoelectronics, and electrochemistry. High-temperature superconductivity and high-entropy alloys expanded the compositional imagination, while mussel-inspired coatings linked materials design to biointerfaces. Several entries sit at disciplinary borders: patch clamp and optical coherence tomography connect materials and instrumentation to biology and medicine; photocatalysis links semiconductors to chemistry; plasmonics links nanostructure to optics. The cluster therefore represents the transformation of materials science into an integrative science of structure-property-processing-performance across length scales.
