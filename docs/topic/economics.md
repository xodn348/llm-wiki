# Economics

_37 Fleming-tier papers._

## Synthesis

Together these Fleming-tier economics papers trace the discipline’s shift from broad theoretical systems to formally modeled, empirically testable accounts of markets, institutions, and behavior. Keynes’s *General Theory* marks the macroeconomic break from classical self-correction toward aggregate demand, unemployment, and policy stabilization. Growth theory then develops from Solow’s exogenous technical change and aggregate production functions to Lewis’s development dualism, cross-country growth empirics, and endogenous innovation in Romer-style increasing returns. A second lineage reconceives markets as structured by information, incentives, and institutions: Coase on firms, Tiebout on local public goods, Becker on crime, Spence on signaling, Stiglitz-Weiss on credit rationing, Jensen-Meckling on agency, Williamson and North on institutional performance, and Granovetter on social embeddedness.

The cluster also records major paradigm shifts in finance, econometrics, and behavioral economics. Asset pricing moves from CAPM equilibrium risk to empirical anomalies, corporate debt pricing, multifactor models, momentum, and policy uncertainty. Econometrics becomes a central methodological engine through nonparametric trend tests, Granger causality, specification testing, VAR macroeconomics, ARCH volatility, and cointegration. Behavioral economics enters through prospect theory, cumulative prospect theory, and fairness models, challenging expected-utility and purely self-interested assumptions. Adjacent connections are unusually broad: political science through democracy and welfare regimes; sociology through embedded economic action; law and criminology through economic models of punishment; psychology through risk and fairness; and statistics through modern time-series inference. Collectively, these papers portray economics as a field repeatedly transformed by better accounts of uncertainty, information, institutions, and human behavior.
