# Classification and regression trees

## Why this mattered

The 1985 EJOR item attributed to J. Praagman appears to be a bibliographic review or notice of *Classification and Regression Trees*, not the original source of the CART method. The paradigm-shifting work was Breiman, Friedman, Olshen, and Stone’s 1984 monograph, which systematized tree-structured prediction as a general statistical methodology for both classification and regression. Its importance lay in making predictive modeling less dependent on globally specified parametric forms: a model could instead partition the feature space recursively, produce interpretable decision rules, and adapt naturally to nonlinearities and interactions.

CART made several ideas newly practical at once: automated split selection, regression trees as well as classification trees, cost-complexity pruning, cross-validation for choosing tree size, and explicit attention to prediction error rather than only model fit. That combination helped move statistical modeling toward algorithmic, data-adaptive procedures that could be applied across medicine, operations research, engineering, and later machine learning. It also made computers central to model construction, since the search over recursive partitions was not a pencil-and-paper extension of classical regression.

Its later influence is clearest in ensemble methods. Bagging, random forests, boosting with trees, gradient-boosted decision trees, and many modern tabular-learning systems all build on CART’s representation of prediction as recursive partitioning. In that sense, CART was not just another decision-tree method; it supplied the statistical and computational template from which much of post-1990s supervised learning on structured data developed.
