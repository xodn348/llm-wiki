# Cooling of gases by laser radiation

## Why this mattered

Hänsch and Schawlow’s paper made a conceptual shift: light was not only a tool for probing atoms, but a medium for removing their translational kinetic energy. The key idea was simple and powerful: because moving atoms see laser light Doppler-shifted into resonance, red-detuned radiation can preferentially scatter from atoms moving toward the beam. Repeated absorption and re-emission then produces a velocity-dependent damping force, transferring kinetic energy from the gas into slightly higher-frequency scattered photons. This reframed atomic motion as something that could be optically controlled, not merely measured.

What became newly possible was the prospect of cooling dilute gases far below ordinary cryogenic limits without physical contact with a cold surface. The paper identified the Doppler width and natural linewidth as the relevant scale for the achievable velocity reduction, giving the field a concrete target rather than just a qualitative proposal. In doing so it helped launch laser cooling as an experimental program: slowing atomic beams, optical molasses, magneto-optical trapping, and eventually the routine production of ultracold atomic samples.

Its importance is also retrospective: later breakthroughs showed that the paper had opened the door to an entire low-temperature atomic physics regime. Laser cooling enabled precision spectroscopy with greatly reduced Doppler broadening, atomic clocks with far higher stability, and trapped neutral-atom experiments. Together with subsequent developments such as sub-Doppler cooling, evaporative cooling, and magnetic or optical trapping, it became one of the technical foundations that made Bose-Einstein condensation in dilute gases and modern quantum-gas experiments possible.

## Abstract

It is shown that a low-density gas can be cooled by illuminating it with intense, quasi-monochromatic light confined to the lower-frequency half of a resonance line's Doppler width. Translational kinetic energy can be transferred from the gas to the scattered light, until the atomic velocity is reduced by the ratio of the Doppler width to the natural line width.
