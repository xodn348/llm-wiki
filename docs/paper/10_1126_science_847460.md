# The Need for a New Medical Model: A Challenge for Biomedicine

## Why this mattered

Engel’s 1977 paper mattered because it named a limitation at the center of modern medicine: the biomedical model treated disease as a deviation in biological variables, but had no principled place for the patient’s experience, behavior, social setting, or the clinical relationship itself. The proposed biopsychosocial model did not reject biology; it challenged biological reductionism as an incomplete model for explaining illness and organizing care. That made the paper paradigm-shifting: it reframed disease and illness as phenomena occurring across interacting biological, psychological, and social levels, turning factors previously treated as “context” into legitimate objects of medical explanation, research, teaching, and intervention.

After Engel, it became easier to justify research and clinical programs that crossed the old boundary between pathophysiology and lived illness: behavioral medicine, health psychology, consultation-liaison psychiatry, patient-centered care, chronic disease self-management, pain medicine, adherence research, and social determinants of health all fit more naturally within the framework he articulated. The paper also gave medicine a vocabulary for conditions in which symptoms, disability, prognosis, and treatment response cannot be understood from lesions or laboratory values alone, including chronic pain, functional disorders, depression in medical illness, addiction, and long-term management of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancer survivorship.

Its later influence was not that it supplied a finished mechanism or a single testable theory; critics have often noted that the biopsychosocial model can become vague if used as a slogan. Its importance was more foundational: it changed what counted as medically relevant evidence. Subsequent breakthroughs in psychoneuroimmunology, stress physiology, behavioral epidemiology, implementation science, and integrated primary care all developed in a world where Engel’s argument had made it intellectually respectable to study how bodies, minds, relationships, and institutions jointly shape illness and healing.

## Abstract

The dominant model of disease today is biomedical, and it leaves no room within tis framework for the social, psychological, and behavioral dimensions of illness. A biopsychosocial model is proposed that provides a blueprint for research, a framework for teaching, and a design for action in the real world of health care.
