# The reflective practitioner: How professionals think in action

## Why this mattered

Strictly speaking, the 1984 *Patient Education and Counseling* item by Cynthia J. Stewart was a brief review of Donald A. Schön’s 1983 book *The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action*, not the original paradigm-shifting work itself. The influence usually attached to this title comes from Schön’s book, which challenged the dominant “technical rationality” model of professional expertise: the idea that good practice mainly consists of applying formal scientific knowledge to well-defined problems. Schön argued instead that much professional work begins with uncertain, unstable, value-laden situations in which the practitioner must frame the problem while acting within it.

What became newly possible after Schön’s formulation was a serious scholarly language for forms of expertise that had often been treated as tacit, informal, or merely intuitive. Concepts such as “knowing-in-action” and “reflection-in-action” helped education, medicine, counseling, design, management, and planning study professional judgment as an active inquiry process rather than as failed rule-following. This shifted attention from only teaching more theory to also cultivating reflective habits: case discussion, supervision, practicum learning, design studios, clinical reasoning exercises, portfolios, and after-action review.

Its later importance lies in how it connected earlier pragmatist ideas, especially John Dewey’s account of reflective thought, to late twentieth-century professional education. Subsequent breakthroughs in reflective practice, experiential learning, evidence-informed practice, design thinking, and practitioner research all drew on the opening Schön created: expertise could be examined as situated, iterative, and self-correcting without reducing it to either pure science or personal intuition. For patient education and counseling, that mattered because communication with patients rarely presents itself as a fixed technical problem; it requires professionals to interpret context, negotiate meaning, and revise their approach in real time.
