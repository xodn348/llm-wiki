# Particle creation by black holes

## Why this mattered

Hawking’s 1975 paper changed black holes from purely absorptive classical objects into quantum thermodynamic systems. By analyzing quantum fields on the curved spacetime of a collapsing star, Hawking showed that black holes emit particles with a thermal spectrum at a temperature proportional to their surface gravity and inversely proportional to their mass. This made Bekenstein’s proposal of black-hole entropy physically compelling: black holes were not just analogous to thermodynamic bodies, but actually radiated as such.

The result created a new arena where general relativity, quantum field theory, and thermodynamics could no longer be treated separately. If a black hole radiates, it loses mass and can eventually evaporate, so questions about the final state of evaporation and the fate of information became unavoidable. The paper therefore opened the black hole information problem: a sharp conflict between semiclassical gravity’s apparently thermal radiation and quantum mechanics’ unitary evolution.

Much of later quantum-gravity research can be read as a response to this paper’s implications. It helped motivate precise work on black-hole entropy, Euclidean quantum gravity, string-theoretic microstate counting, holography, AdS/CFT, and modern discussions of entanglement wedges, islands, and the Page curve. Its importance lies not only in predicting Hawking radiation, but in making black holes the central testing ground for any theory that claims to reconcile gravity with quantum mechanics.
