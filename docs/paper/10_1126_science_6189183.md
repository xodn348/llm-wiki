# Isolation of a T-Lymphotropic Retrovirus from a Patient at Risk for Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS)

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it shifted AIDS from a clinically defined syndrome of immune collapse toward a tractable virological problem. In 1983, the cause of AIDS was still unknown; Barré-Sinoussi and colleagues reported the isolation of a new human retrovirus from a patient with lymphadenopathy, a condition then recognized as often preceding AIDS. The isolate resembled the recently discovered HTLV family in broad retroviral features, including reverse transcriptase activity and T-lymphocyte association, but it was immunologically distinct from known HTLV-I isolates. That distinction was crucial: the paper did not merely add another virus to an existing category, but opened the possibility that AIDS was linked to a previously unrecognized human retrovirus.

The immediate consequence was that AIDS research gained a physical target. Once a candidate virus could be propagated in lymphocytes, visualized, biochemically characterized, and tested against patient sera, investigators could move from epidemiological inference to assays, viral antigen detection, serology, and eventually molecular cloning and sequencing. Those steps made it possible to show that the virus, later named HIV, was the cause of AIDS; to screen blood supplies; to trace transmission; and to define the viral life cycle as a therapeutic target. The paper’s framing still reflected the uncertainties of 1983, including its placement of the isolate within a broad “T-lymphotropic retrovirus” family and cautious language about involvement in AIDS, but that caution is part of its historical importance: it marks the transition point at which AIDS became experimentally approachable as an infectious retroviral disease.

Subsequent breakthroughs depended on that transition. Diagnostic tests emerged from the ability to detect antibodies to viral proteins; antiretroviral therapy followed from understanding the roles of reverse transcriptase, protease, integration, and viral entry; and HIV/AIDS became a central model for modern viral pathogenesis, global surveillance, and drug-combination strategy. In retrospect, the paper’s paradigm shift was not simply the discovery of a virus, but the conversion of a frightening and poorly understood epidemic into a research program with isolatable agents, measurable markers, and intervention points.

## Abstract

A retrovirus belonging to the family of recently discovered human T-cell leukemia viruses (HTLV), but clearly distinct from each previous isolate, has been isolated from a Caucasian patient with signs and symptoms that often precede the acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS). This virus is a typical type-C RNA tumor virus, buds from the cell membrane, prefers magnesium for reverse transcriptase activity, and has an internal antigen (p25) similar to HTLV p24. Antibodies from serum of this patient react with proteins from viruses of the HTLV-I subgroup, but type-specific antisera to HTLV-I do not precipitate proteins of the new isolate. The virus from this patient has been transmitted into cord blood lymphocytes, and the virus produced by these cells is similar to the original isolate. From these studies it is concluded that this virus as well as the previous HTLV isolates belong to a general family of T-lymphotropic retroviruses that are horizontally transmitted in humans and may be involved in several pathological syndromes, including AIDS.
