# FINANCIAL RATIOS, DISCRIMINANT ANALYSIS AND THE PREDICTION OF CORPORATE BANKRUPTCY

## Why this mattered

Altman’s 1968 paper mattered because it turned corporate bankruptcy prediction from a largely qualitative credit-judgment problem into a reproducible statistical classification problem. By applying multiple discriminant analysis to accounting ratios, Altman showed that a small set of financial indicators could be combined into a single score, later known as the Z-score, that separated bankrupt from non-bankrupt manufacturing firms with striking accuracy in his sample. The paradigm shift was not that financial ratios were useful individually, but that their joint structure could be modeled systematically to estimate default risk before failure occurred.

This made bankruptcy prediction operational in a new way. Lenders, investors, auditors, and researchers could now treat corporate distress as a measurable probability-like signal rather than an after-the-fact narrative. The paper helped establish empirical credit-risk modeling as a field and gave subsequent work a benchmark: later logit, probit, hazard-rate, machine-learning, and structural credit-risk models all inherited the basic ambition Altman made concrete, namely to convert observable firm data into forward-looking default forecasts.

Its influence also reached beyond bankruptcy itself. The Z-score became one of the canonical examples of how statistical learning could be applied to finance using firm-level data, helping normalize model-based screening in credit analysis, portfolio management, and regulatory thinking. Later breakthroughs used richer data, different assumptions, and more flexible estimation methods, but Altman’s paper supplied the durable template: define an economically important failure event, assemble measurable predictors, validate classification performance, and use the result as a decision tool.
