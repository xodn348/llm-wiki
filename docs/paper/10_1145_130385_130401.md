# A training algorithm for optimal margin classifiers

## Why this mattered

This paper helped turn statistical learning theory into a practical learning algorithm. Earlier classifiers such as perceptrons and many neural-network methods could find separating decision boundaries, but Boser, Guyon, and Vapnik made the *margin* itself the optimization target: choose the separator that leaves the largest gap between classes, and express the solution through only the “supporting” training examples nearest the boundary. That reframed classification from fitting many adjustable parameters to solving a constrained optimization problem whose capacity could be tied to generalization bounds.

Its central paradigm shift was the combination of maximum-margin learning with nonlinear feature mappings through kernels. By allowing perceptrons, polynomials, and radial basis functions to be treated in a common framework, the method made high-dimensional nonlinear classification computationally usable without explicitly constructing all features. This made it newly practical to build flexible classifiers with strong generalization behavior, especially in domains such as handwriting and optical character recognition where decision boundaries were complex but training data were finite.

The work became the foundation of support vector machines, one of the defining machine-learning methods of the 1990s and early 2000s. It influenced later kernel methods for regression, novelty detection, structured prediction, and bioinformatics, and it also shaped how the field thought about model complexity: not simply as a count of parameters, but as a property controlled by margins, constraints, and data geometry. Even after deep learning displaced SVMs in many large-scale perception tasks, the paper’s ideas remained central to modern machine learning’s vocabulary of margins, support examples, regularization, and generalization.

## Abstract

A training algorithm that maximizes the margin between the training patterns and the decision boundary is presented. The technique is applicable to a wide variety of the classification functions, including Perceptrons, polynomials, and Radial Basis Functions. The effective number of parameters is adjusted automatically to match the complexity of the problem. The solution is expressed as a linear combination of supporting patterns. These are the subset of training patterns that are closest to the decision boundary. Bounds on the generalization performance based on the leave-one-out method and the VC-dimension are given. Experimental results on optical character recognition problems demonstrate the good generalization obtained when compared with other learning algorithms.
