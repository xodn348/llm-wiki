# Statistical method for testing the neutral mutation hypothesis by DNA polymorphism.

## Why this mattered

Tajima’s 1989 paper made DNA polymorphism itself a statistical test of evolutionary neutrality. Before this, molecular evolution had strong theoretical predictions and growing sequence data, but detecting departures from the neutral mutation hypothesis often depended on comparisons between species or on locus-specific interpretations. Tajima showed that two within-population summaries, the number of segregating sites and the average pairwise nucleotide difference, estimate the same neutral parameter under the standard neutral model, but respond differently to the allele-frequency spectrum. Their standardized difference, later known as **Tajima’s D**, turned raw polymorphism data into a portable diagnostic for whether observed variation was compatible with neutral mutation and constant population size.

The shift was conceptual as much as technical: selection, demographic change, and other forces could now be inferred from the *shape* of variation within a population, not only from fixed differences between populations or species. Negative values indicated an excess of rare variants, as expected after population expansion or purifying/positive selection; positive values indicated an excess of intermediate-frequency variants, as expected under balancing selection or population structure. This gave population genetics a simple bridge between coalescent theory, DNA sequencing, and empirical inference, at exactly the moment when sequence-level polymorphism data were beginning to accumulate.

Its influence runs through later breakthroughs in evolutionary genomics. Genome scans for selective sweeps, balancing selection, demographic expansion, bottlenecks, and background selection all build on the same premise that the allele-frequency spectrum contains historical information. Even when later methods became more model-rich, likelihood-based, simulation-based, or genome-wide, Tajima’s D remained a baseline statistic: interpretable, easy to compute, and central for asking whether a genomic region looks neutral before invoking more elaborate explanations.

## Abstract

The relationship between the two estimates of genetic variation at the DNA level, namely the number of segregating sites and the average number of nucleotide differences estimated from pairwise comparison, is investigated. It is found that the correlation between these two estimates is large when the sample size is small, and decreases slowly as the sample size increases. Using the relationship obtained, a statistical method for testing the neutral mutation hypothesis is developed. This method needs only the data of DNA polymorphism, namely the genetic variation within population at the DNA level. A simple method of computer simulation, that was used in order to obtain the distribution of a new statistic developed, is also presented. Applying this statistical method to the five regions of DNA sequences in Drosophila melanogaster, it is found that large insertion/deletion (greater than 100 bp) is deleterious. It is suggested that the natural selection against large insertion/deletion is so weak that a large amount of variation is maintained in a population.
