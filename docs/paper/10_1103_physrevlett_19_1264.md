# A Model of Leptons

## Why this mattered

Weinberg’s 1967 paper mattered because it gave a compact, predictive model in which the weak and electromagnetic interactions were not separate forces but different low-energy manifestations of a single gauge theory. By using an \(SU(2)\times U(1)\) symmetry with spontaneous symmetry breaking, the paper showed how the photon could remain massless while the weak force carriers acquired mass, avoiding the obvious conflict between gauge symmetry and the short range of weak interactions. It also incorporated the charged lepton masses through symmetry breaking, tying particle masses to what later became the Higgs mechanism.

The paradigm shift was not only unification, but calculability. Before this framework, weak-interaction theory worked phenomenologically but did not provide a satisfactory high-energy quantum field theory. Weinberg’s model made neutral weak currents, massive \(W^\pm\) and \(Z^0\) bosons, and a scalar Higgs particle natural consequences of the same structure. Although the paper itself did not prove renormalizability, it set the form of the electroweak theory that Gerard ’t Hooft and Martinus Veltman later showed could be consistently renormalized, turning the model from an elegant proposal into a viable foundation for precision particle physics.

Its consequences were confirmed over the following decades: neutral currents were observed in the 1970s, the \(W\) and \(Z\) bosons in 1983, and the Higgs boson in 2012. Together with related work by Sheldon Glashow and Abdus Salam, Weinberg’s paper became a central pillar of the Standard Model. What changed after it was the standard of explanation in particle physics: successful theories were expected not just to fit known interactions, but to derive them from gauge symmetry, symmetry breaking, and experimentally testable quantum field theory.
