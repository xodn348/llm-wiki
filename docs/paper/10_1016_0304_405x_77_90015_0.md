# Determinants of corporate borrowing

## Why this mattered

Myers’s 1977 paper mattered because it made capital structure endogenous to the firm’s investment opportunity set, not just a balance between tax shields and expected bankruptcy costs. Its central insight was the underinvestment, or “debt overhang,” problem: when a firm already has risky debt outstanding, shareholders may rationally reject positive-net-present-value projects because much of the gain would accrue to existing debtholders. That reframed corporate borrowing as a problem of incentive alignment across claims on the firm, especially when value depends on future growth opportunities rather than only assets already in place.

The paper helped explain why high-growth firms tend to use less debt, while firms with tangible assets and relatively stable cash flows can borrow more. This was a shift from asking how much debt a firm could sustain in liquidation or distress to asking how debt changes future investment behavior. It also connected corporate finance to contingent-claims logic: equity and debt resemble option-like claims, so financing choices affect who captures the upside from new projects.

After Myers, “debt overhang” became a foundational mechanism in corporate finance, restructuring, banking crises, and sovereign debt analysis. It shaped later work on agency costs of debt, financial contracting, and security design, and it directly foreshadowed Myers’s own later contributions on financing hierarchies and information problems. The paper’s lasting importance is that it made financing and investment inseparable: capital structure was no longer merely a way to divide cash flows, but a force that could determine whether valuable real activity happened at all.
