# A haplotype map of the human genome

## Why this mattered

Authored by **The International HapMap Consortium**, this paper changed human genetics from a mostly gene-by-gene enterprise into a genome-wide, population-scale mapping science. The key shift was practical as much as conceptual: by showing that common variants are correlated through linkage disequilibrium, often in local haplotype blocks shaped by recombination hotspots, HapMap made it possible to test representative “tag” SNPs rather than resequence every variant in every study. That turned the recently completed human reference genome into an actionable map of inherited variation.

What became newly possible was the modern genome-wide association study. Researchers could now design arrays that sampled common variation across the genome, impute untyped variants from reference haplotypes, compare association signals across cohorts, and localize disease-risk loci without starting from a prior candidate gene. The paper also made recombination, population history, structural variation, and signatures of selection measurable at genome scale, helping establish public reference resources as core infrastructure for biomedical discovery.

Its influence is visible in the wave of GWAS hits that followed for diabetes, inflammatory disease, height, lipid traits, cancers, and many other complex phenotypes, and later in larger reference panels such as the 1000 Genomes Project and modern biobank-scale genetics. HapMap did not solve the biology of complex disease by itself: most associated variants had small effects and often pointed to regulatory regions rather than obvious causal genes. But it supplied the coordinate system and statistical logic that made those discoveries systematic.

## Abstract

Inherited genetic variation has a critical but as yet largely uncharacterized role in human disease. Here we report a public database of common variation in the human genome: more than one million single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for which accurate and complete genotypes have been obtained in 269 DNA samples from four populations, including ten 500-kilobase regions in which essentially all information about common DNA variation has been extracted. These data document the generality of recombination hotspots, a block-like structure of linkage disequilibrium and low haplotype diversity, leading to substantial correlations of SNPs with many of their neighbours. We show how the HapMap resource can guide the design and analysis of genetic association studies, shed light on structural variation and recombination, and identify loci that may have been subject to natural selection during human evolution.
