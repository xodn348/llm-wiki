# Quantum computation and quantum information

## Why this mattered

This entry appears to refer to the 2001 ACM SIGSOFT Software Engineering Notes notice/review of *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information*, the textbook by Michael A. Nielsen and Isaac L. Chuang, rather than a new research paper by Jim Law. Its importance lies in consolidating a rapidly emerging field into a coherent intellectual framework: quantum bits, quantum gates, entanglement, quantum algorithms, quantum error correction, and quantum information theory became teachable as one discipline rather than scattered results across physics, mathematics, and computer science.

That synthesis mattered because it made quantum computation newly accessible to a broad technical audience. After Shor’s factoring algorithm and Grover’s search algorithm had shown that quantum computers could alter basic assumptions about computation, the field still lacked a standard reference connecting those breakthroughs to implementable models, noise, fault tolerance, and information-theoretic limits. Nielsen and Chuang’s treatment helped establish the common language in which later work on quantum complexity, quantum cryptography, topological codes, and fault-tolerant architectures would be developed.

Its influence is visible less as a single theorem than as an infrastructure shift. Subsequent breakthroughs, from surface-code threshold results to variational quantum algorithms and modern quantum software stacks, drew on concepts that this book helped standardize for researchers entering the field. In that sense, the 2001 SIGSOFT entry marks the diffusion of quantum information into software engineering and computer science: quantum computing was no longer only a theoretical curiosity or physics subfield, but a computational paradigm with algorithms, abstractions, and engineering constraints that software researchers could begin to study systematically.
