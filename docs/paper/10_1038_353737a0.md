# A low-cost, high-efficiency solar cell based on dye-sensitized colloidal TiO2 films

## Why this mattered

O’Regan and Grätzel’s 1991 paper mattered because it showed that an efficient photovoltaic device did not have to be built around a high-purity, crystalline semiconductor junction. By coating a nanocrystalline, high-surface-area TiO2 film with a light-absorbing dye and using a redox electrolyte to regenerate it, the cell separated the functions of light absorption, charge injection, and charge transport. That architecture turned cheap, abundant, disordered oxide films into a viable solar-energy platform, reaching efficiencies high enough to make dye-sensitized solar cells a serious alternative research direction rather than a curiosity.

The paradigm shift was mesoscopic photovoltaics: performance could come from engineered interfaces and enormous internal surface area, not only from bulk semiconductor perfection. After this paper, researchers could tune dyes, electrolytes, oxide morphologies, and interfacial chemistry almost independently, opening a broad field of low-temperature, potentially low-cost solar-cell design. It also made transparent, flexible, colored, and indoor-light-harvesting photovoltaic devices conceptually and practically plausible.

Its influence extended beyond dye-sensitized solar cells themselves. The mesoporous TiO2 scaffold, sensitizer-based light harvesting, and interfacial charge-transfer logic became direct antecedents to later solid-state dye cells and helped shape the early development of perovskite solar cells, which first emerged from dye-sensitized architectures before becoming a major photovoltaic class of their own. The 1991 Nature paper therefore shifted photovoltaic thinking from “make a better semiconductor wafer” toward “design a better photoelectrochemical interface,” a change that redirected decades of solar-cell research.
