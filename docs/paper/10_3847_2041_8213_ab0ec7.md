# First M87 Event Horizon Telescope Results. I. The Shadow of the Supermassive Black Hole

## Why this mattered

This paper marked the first resolved, event-horizon-scale image of a black hole candidate, turning black holes from objects inferred through stellar or gas dynamics, accretion luminosity, and relativistic jets into objects whose near-horizon appearance could be directly tested. The result did not “photograph” the event horizon itself; it reconstructed millimeter-VLBI images of emission around M87* and found a bright, asymmetric ring surrounding a central brightness depression. That morphology matched the long-standing prediction that strong gravitational lensing and photon capture near a Kerr black hole should produce a shadow-like feature. The measured angular diameter, stability across observing days, and agreement across independent imaging and calibration methods made the result a new kind of empirical constraint on strong-field general relativity.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as observational. By linking telescopes across the Earth at 1.3 mm, the Event Horizon Telescope made horizon-scale astronomy possible for nearby supermassive black holes. After this paper, black-hole studies could compare images directly against general-relativistic magnetohydrodynamic simulations rather than relying only on indirect signatures. For M87*, that enabled an independent mass estimate of about \(6.5 \times 10^9 M_\odot\), supported the interpretation of active galactic nuclei as powered by supermassive black holes, and opened a way to study plasma dynamics, relativistic beaming, and gravity in a regime inaccessible to Solar System tests or gravitational-wave observations of stellar-mass mergers.

Its importance also lay in establishing the observational and computational template for later breakthroughs. The M87* image validated the EHT’s global interferometric strategy, imaging pipelines, uncertainty analysis, and simulation-based interpretation, making subsequent horizon-scale studies scientifically credible rather than merely spectacular. It directly prepared the ground for later EHT results, including polarized images of M87* that probed magnetic fields near the event horizon and the 2022 image of Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the center of the Milky Way. Together, these works shifted black-hole astrophysics toward a comparative, image-based discipline in which the spacetime-scale structure of different supermassive black holes could be measured and tested.

## Abstract

Abstract When surrounded by a transparent emission region, black holes are expected to reveal a dark shadow caused by gravitational light bending and photon capture at the event horizon. To image and study this phenomenon, we have assembled the Event Horizon Telescope, a global very long baseline interferometry array observing at a wavelength of 1.3 mm. This allows us to reconstruct event-horizon-scale images of the supermassive black hole candidate in the center of the giant elliptical galaxy M87. We have resolved the central compact radio source as an asymmetric bright emission ring with a diameter of 42 ± 3 μ as, which is circular and encompasses a central depression in brightness with a flux ratio ≳10:1. The emission ring is recovered using different calibration and imaging schemes, with its diameter and width remaining stable over four different observations carried out in different days. Overall, the observed image is consistent with expectations for the shadow of a Kerr black hole as predicted by general relativity. The asymmetry in brightness in the ring can be explained in terms of relativistic beaming of the emission from a plasma rotating close to the speed of light around a black hole. We compare our images to an extensive library of ray-traced general-relativistic magnetohydrodynamic simulations of black holes and derive a central mass of M = (6.5 ± 0.7) × 10 9 M ⊙ . Our radio-wave observations thus provide powerful evidence for the presence of supermassive black holes in centers of galaxies and as the central engines of active galactic nuclei. They also present a new tool to explore gravity in its most extreme limit and on a mass scale that was so far not accessible.
