# Self-consistent molecular orbital methods. XX. A basis set for correlated wave functions

## Why this mattered

Krishnan, Binkley, Seeger, and Pople’s 1980 paper mattered because it helped move quantum chemistry from qualitative molecular-orbital calculations toward systematically improvable, correlated *ab initio* prediction. Earlier Gaussian basis sets such as 3-21G and 6-31G made Hartree-Fock calculations broadly practical, but electron correlation methods like Moller-Plesset perturbation theory exposed the need for more flexible valence descriptions and polarization functions. The 6-311G** basis set answered that need: triple-split valence functions allowed molecular orbitals to change shape more finely across bonding environments, while polarization functions let electron density bend away from spherical atomic forms. In practical terms, it gave chemists a standard, computationally tractable basis for treating correlation beyond Hartree-Fock.

The paradigm shift was not that this paper invented correlated wave-function theory, but that it made correlated calculations reproducible and widely usable on ordinary molecular systems. By optimizing and testing the basis at the MP2 level, the authors aligned the basis-set design with the physics that post-Hartree-Fock methods were trying to recover. This helped establish a template for modern computational chemistry: named, benchmarked basis sets paired with standard levels of theory, so that molecular structures, reaction energies, conformational preferences, and spectroscopic quantities could be compared across laboratories.

Its influence carried into later breakthroughs in both wave-function and density-functional chemistry. 6-311G** and related Pople basis sets became default choices in major quantum-chemistry packages and in thousands of applied studies, helping normalize routine calculations that included electron correlation or polarization effects. The same culture of standardized basis-set families also shaped later developments: correlation-consistent basis sets, composite thermochemical methods, and hybrid DFT protocols all depended on the idea that reliable prediction required carefully designed, transferable basis sets matched to the intended electronic-structure model.

## Abstract

A contracted Gaussian basis set (6-311G**) is developed by optimizing exponents and coefficients at the Mo/ller–Plesset (MP) second-order level for the ground states of first-row atoms. This has a triple split in the valence s and p shells together with a single set of uncontracted polarization functions on each atom. The basis is tested by computing structures and energies for some simple molecules at various levels of MP theory and comparing with experiment.
