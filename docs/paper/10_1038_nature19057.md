# Analysis of protein-coding genetic variation in 60,706 humans

## Why this mattered

Before ExAC, clinical and functional interpretation of rare coding variants was constrained by small, uneven reference datasets: a variant absent from controls could too easily be treated as suspicious, and genes were often assessed without a population-scale baseline for how much damaging variation they normally tolerate. Lek et al. changed that by aggregating high-quality exome data from 60,706 people and making human coding variation visible at a scale where rarity itself could be measured rather than guessed. The paper showed that the exome is densely populated with variation, including recurrent mutations, and that many variants once plausibly labeled disease-causing were present in the general population at frequencies incompatible with highly penetrant severe disease.

The deeper shift was from variant interpretation by anecdote or small comparison cohorts to interpretation against a quantitative map of human constraint. By estimating how depleted each gene was for missense and protein-truncating variation, ExAC made it possible to identify genes under strong purifying selection and to prioritize candidate disease genes even before a human disease phenotype had been established. Its finding that thousands of genes were nearly depleted of predicted loss-of-function variants, most without known disease associations, reframed the human genome as a measurable landscape of dosage sensitivity and natural selection, not just a list of known Mendelian loci.

This paper also set the template for the population-genomics infrastructure that followed. ExAC, and later gnomAD, became essential background references for rare-disease diagnosis, gene discovery, variant classification, penetrance estimation, and human “knockout” studies. Subsequent breakthroughs in clinical genomics, constraint-based gene prioritization, and large biobank-scale interpretation all depended on the same principle crystallized here: to know whether a variant or gene is medically meaningful, one must first know how it behaves across tens or hundreds of thousands of ostensibly unaffected humans.

## Abstract

Large-scale reference data sets of human genetic variation are critical for the medical and functional interpretation of DNA sequence changes. Here we describe the aggregation and analysis of high-quality exome (protein-coding region) DNA sequence data for 60,706 individuals of diverse ancestries generated as part of the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC). This catalogue of human genetic diversity contains an average of one variant every eight bases of the exome, and provides direct evidence for the presence of widespread mutational recurrence. We have used this catalogue to calculate objective metrics of pathogenicity for sequence variants, and to identify genes subject to strong selection against various classes of mutation; identifying 3,230 genes with near-complete depletion of predicted protein-truncating variants, with 72% of these genes having no currently established human disease phenotype. Finally, we demonstrate that these data can be used for the efficient filtering of candidate disease-causing variants, and for the discovery of human 'knockout' variants in protein-coding genes.
