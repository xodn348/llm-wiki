# The central role of the propensity score in observational studies for causal effects

## Why this mattered

Rosenbaum and Rubin’s paper changed observational causal inference by showing that the high-dimensional problem of adjusting for many observed covariates could, under the stated assumptions, be reduced to adjustment for a single scalar: the propensity score. The key insight was not merely a new matching heuristic, but a balancing theorem: conditional on the propensity score, the distribution of observed covariates is the same in treated and control groups. This gave researchers a principled way to design observational studies so that treatment groups could be made comparable on measured pre-treatment variables before examining outcomes.

What became newly possible was a practical separation between study design and outcome analysis in nonrandomized settings. Instead of modeling outcomes directly with many covariates, analysts could first estimate each unit’s probability of receiving treatment, then match, stratify, weight, or diagnose overlap using that score. This made causal analysis more transparent and scalable, especially in medicine, economics, epidemiology, and the social sciences, where randomized experiments were often infeasible or unethical but rich covariate data were available.

The paper also became a foundation for later causal-inference breakthroughs. Propensity-score matching, subclassification, inverse-probability weighting, doubly robust estimation, targeted learning, and modern causal machine-learning methods all rely, directly or indirectly, on the idea that treatment assignment mechanisms can be modeled and used to create balance. Its limits were equally influential: because propensity scores address bias from observed covariates, not hidden confounding, the paper sharpened later work on sensitivity analysis, overlap, diagnostics, and the distinction between statistical adjustment and causal identification.

## Abstract

The propensity score is the conditional probability of assignment to a particular treatment given a vector of observed covariates. Both large and small sample theory show that adjustment for the scalar propensity score is sufficient to remove bias due to all observed covariates. Applications include: (i) matched sampling on the univariate propensity score, which is a generalization of discriminant matching, (ii) multivariate adjustment by subclassification on the propensity score where the same subclasses are used to estimate treatment effects for all outcome variables and in all subpopulations, and (iii) visual representation of multivariate covariance adjustment by a two- dimensional plot.
