# A theory of human motivation.

## Why this mattered

Maslow’s 1943 paper mattered because it reframed motivation as an organized system rather than a collection of isolated drives. Instead of treating behavior as reducible to hunger, sex, reward, or habit, Maslow argued that human motives form a hierarchy: physiological needs, safety, love and belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. The shift was not merely a new list of motives, but a new explanatory architecture: a need’s psychological force depends on whether more basic needs are relatively satisfied. This made it possible to discuss human aspiration, creativity, and moral development within a general theory of motivation rather than outside scientific psychology.

The paper also helped move psychology toward a more organismic and developmental view of the person. Maslow insisted that motivation concerns the “integrated wholeness” of the organism, and that people are not only tension-reducing systems but beings capable of growth once deprivation pressures are less dominant. That claim became foundational for humanistic psychology, influencing later work in personality theory, psychotherapy, education, management, and organizational behavior. Even where later researchers criticized the strict ordering or empirical support for the hierarchy, the paper supplied a durable vocabulary for distinguishing deficit-driven motivation from growth-oriented motivation.

Its broader significance lies in what it made thinkable: that well-being, flourishing, and self-realization could be legitimate scientific topics. Subsequent traditions such as positive psychology, self-determination theory, and needs-based approaches to education and work did not simply inherit Maslow’s model unchanged, but they developed in a landscape partly opened by it. The paper’s lasting impact comes from that paradigm shift: motivation could be studied not only as response to deprivation, but also as the basis for human development, meaning, and excellence.
