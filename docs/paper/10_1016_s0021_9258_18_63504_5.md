# Superoxide Dismutase

## Why this mattered

McCord and Fridovich’s paper changed superoxide from a chemical curiosity into a biological actor. Before this work, erythrocuprein/hemocuprein was known as a copper protein, but its physiological function was obscure; the 1969 paper identified it as an enzyme whose substrate was the superoxide radical. That was a conceptual shift: cells were not merely damaged passively by oxygen chemistry, but had evolved a specific catalytic defense against a defined reactive oxygen species. The finding made superoxide measurable by inhibition with purified superoxide dismutase, and gave researchers a tool for distinguishing superoxide-mediated reactions from other oxidative processes.

The paper also helped establish the modern field of oxidative stress. By showing that superoxide dismutase was widely distributed in mammalian tissues, McCord and Fridovich implied that superoxide formation was a routine feature of aerobic life, not an exceptional artifact. This made it possible to ask where superoxide was produced, how it interacted with hydrogen peroxide, metal ions, and later nitric oxide, and how failures in antioxidant defenses might contribute to inflammation, ischemia-reperfusion injury, neurodegeneration, aging, and radiation damage.

Subsequent breakthroughs depended on this reclassification of reactive oxygen species as regulated biological intermediates. The discovery of distinct superoxide dismutase families in cytosol, mitochondria, and extracellular spaces, the genetics of SOD mutations in familial ALS, and the broader mapping of redox signaling all trace back to the paper’s central move: assigning a precise enzymatic countermeasure to a precise radical. In that sense, “Superoxide Dismutase” did not just name an enzyme; it made oxygen radical biology experimentally tractable.

## Abstract

An enzyme which catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide radicals (O2·- + O2·- + 2H+ → O2 + H2O2) has been purified by a simple procedure from bovine erythrocytes. This enzyme, called superoxide dismutase, contains 2 eq of copper per mole of enzyme. The copper may be reversibly removed, and it is required for activity. Superoxide dismutase has been shown to be identical with the previously described copper-containing erythrocuprein (human) and hemocuprein (bovine). Stable solutions of the superoxide radical were generated by the electrolytic reduction of O2 in an aprotic solvent, dimethylformamide. Slow infusion of such solutions into buffered aqueous media permitted the demonstration that O2·- can reduce ferricytochrome c and tetranitromethane, and that superoxide dismutase, by competing for the superoxide radicals, can markedly inhibit these reactions. Superoxide dismutase was used to show that the oxidation of epinephrine to adrenochrome by milk xanthine oxidase is mediated by the superoxide radical. An assay of several tissues indicates that superoxide dismutase is widely distributed within mammalian organisms.
