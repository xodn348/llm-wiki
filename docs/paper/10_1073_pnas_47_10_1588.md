# The dependence of cell-free protein synthesis in <i>E. coli</i> upon naturally occurring or synthetic polyribonucleotides

## Why this mattered

Nirenberg and Matthaei’s paper mattered because it turned the genetic code from an abstract problem into an experimentally tractable one. By showing that a cell-free *E. coli* translation system could be driven by added polyribonucleotides, they separated protein synthesis from the intact cell and made the informational role of RNA directly testable. The decisive paradigm shift was that synthetic RNA could be used as an artificial message: polyuridylic acid directed incorporation of phenylalanine, linking the RNA sequence UUU to one amino acid. That result provided the first clear assignment in the genetic code.

The work made it newly possible to decode heredity by biochemical experiment rather than inference from genetics alone. Once synthetic or defined RNAs could be fed into a translation extract, researchers could ask which nucleotide patterns specified which amino acids, leading rapidly to triplet-binding assays, systematic codon assignments, and the near-complete cracking of the genetic code by Nirenberg, Khorana, Holley, and others. It also strengthened the emerging messenger RNA concept: information flowed from nucleic acid sequence into protein sequence through a template read by ribosomes and soluble factors.

In historical terms, this paper helped move molecular biology from the structural triumph of DNA toward the operational logic of gene expression. After Watson and Crick had proposed how genetic information could be copied, Nirenberg and Matthaei showed how such information could be read. The ability to program translation in vitro became a foundation for later work on codon degeneracy, reading frames, translation mechanisms, and eventually recombinant DNA and synthetic biology, where nucleic acid sequences are deliberately designed to produce chosen proteins.
