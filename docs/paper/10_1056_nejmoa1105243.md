# Prevention of HIV-1 Infection with Early Antiretroviral Therapy

## Why this mattered

This trial turned “treatment as prevention” from a biologically plausible idea into randomized clinical evidence. Before HPTN 052, antiretroviral therapy was primarily framed as treatment for the infected individual, while prevention relied on condoms, behavior change, testing, and later male circumcision or pre-exposure prophylaxis. Cohen and colleagues showed that starting ART earlier, while CD4 counts were still 350–550 cells/mm³, reduced virologically linked HIV-1 transmission within serodiscordant couples by about 96%, with only one linked transmission in the early-therapy group. That result made viral suppression a prevention intervention, not just a clinical endpoint.

The paper’s importance was also practical: it changed what public health systems could aim for. If ART could protect both the treated person and their partners, then earlier diagnosis, faster linkage to care, and broader treatment access became central prevention strategies. The finding helped support the move away from waiting for immune decline before treatment, contributing to the later global shift toward “treat all” recommendations and to population-level strategies built around viral suppression.

Its influence also reached into later HIV prevention breakthroughs. HPTN 052 provided the clinical foundation for the principle later summarized as “undetectable = untransmittable” (U=U), which was strengthened by subsequent observational studies of couples with sustained viral suppression. It also complemented PrEP rather than replacing it: together, ART-based prevention for people living with HIV and antiretroviral prophylaxis for HIV-negative people made it possible to imagine HIV control through biomedical tools at scale, provided testing, adherence, access, and equity could be achieved.

## Abstract

BACKGROUND: Antiretroviral therapy that reduces viral replication could limit the transmission of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) in serodiscordant couples. METHODS: In nine countries, we enrolled 1763 couples in which one partner was HIV-1-positive and the other was HIV-1-negative; 54% of the subjects were from Africa, and 50% of infected partners were men. HIV-1-infected subjects with CD4 counts between 350 and 550 cells per cubic millimeter were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive antiretroviral therapy either immediately (early therapy) or after a decline in the CD4 count or the onset of HIV-1-related symptoms (delayed therapy). The primary prevention end point was linked HIV-1 transmission in HIV-1-negative partners. The primary clinical end point was the earliest occurrence of pulmonary tuberculosis, severe bacterial infection, a World Health Organization stage 4 event, or death. RESULTS: As of February 21, 2011, a total of 39 HIV-1 transmissions were observed (incidence rate, 1.2 per 100 person-years; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.9 to 1.7); of these, 28 were virologically linked to the infected partner (incidence rate, 0.9 per 100 person-years, 95% CI, 0.6 to 1.3). Of the 28 linked transmissions, only 1 occurred in the early-therapy group (hazard ratio, 0.04; 95% CI, 0.01 to 0.27; P<0.001). Subjects receiving early therapy had fewer treatment end points (hazard ratio, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.40 to 0.88; P=0.01). CONCLUSIONS: The early initiation of antiretroviral therapy reduced rates of sexual transmission of HIV-1 and clinical events, indicating both personal and public health benefits from such therapy. (Funded by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and others; HPTN 052 ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT00074581.).
