# The Economic Institutions of Capitalism

## Why this mattered

[Williamson’s work](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&id=MUPVLuiy9uQC&lr=&oi=fn) mattered because it made the firm, the contract, and the institution analytically central rather than residual. Building on Coase, it shifted the question from “why do markets fail?” to “which governance structure best fits a transaction?” The key move was to treat asset specificity, uncertainty, frequency, bounded rationality, and opportunism as variables that explain why economic activity is organized through spot markets, long-term contracts, vertical integration, franchises, or other hybrids.

That made possible a comparative institutional economics: institutions could be studied not as background legal scenery, but as mechanisms for economizing on transaction costs. The paradigm shift was especially important for antitrust and industrial organization, because vertical integration and nonstandard contracts no longer had to be interpreted only as monopoly devices; they could also be tested as responses to contracting hazards. This did not make every such arrangement efficient, but it gave economists and legal scholars a disciplined way to ask when hierarchy, relational contracting, or market exchange was the least-cost form of governance.

Its influence ran through the subsequent rise of the [new institutional economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_E._Williamson), empirical work on contracting and firm boundaries, and later theories of incomplete contracts and property rights. The broader breakthrough was methodological: capitalist institutions became objects of economic explanation rather than assumptions outside the model. That opened a durable research program linking law, organization theory, economics, and strategy, and helped make institutional design a central problem in explaining economic performance.

## Abstract

The 2008 crash has left all the established economic doctrines - equilibrium models, real business cycles, disequilibria models - in disarray. Part of the problem is due to Smith’s veil of ignorance: individuals unknowingly pursue society’s interest and, as a result, have no clue as to the macroeconomic effects of their actions: witness the Keynes and Leontief multipliers, the concept of value added, fiat money, Engel’s law and technical progress, to name but a few of the macrofoundations of microeconomics. A good viewpoint to take bearings anew lies in comparing the post-Great Depression institutions with those emerging from Thatcher and Reagan’s economic policies: deregulation, exogenous vs. endoge- nous money, shadow banking vs. Volcker’s Rule. Very simply, the banks, whose lending determined deposits after Roosevelt, and were a public service became private enterprises whose deposits determine lending. These underlay the great moderation preceding 2006, and the subsequent crash.
