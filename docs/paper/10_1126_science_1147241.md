# Mussel-Inspired Surface Chemistry for Multifunctional Coatings

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, surface functionalization was often substrate-specific: silanes for oxides, thiols for noble metals, plasma treatments or primers for polymers, and separate chemistries for metals, ceramics, semiconductors, and biomaterials. Lee, Dellatore, Miller, and Messersmith reframed the problem by showing that a mussel-inspired small molecule, dopamine, could self-polymerize under mild aqueous conditions into a thin adherent polydopamine film on an unusually broad range of materials. The conceptual shift was not merely a new coating recipe, but a general interfacial “primer” chemistry: many surfaces could first be made chemically addressable through the same simple dip-coating step.

That made previously cumbersome multilayer surface engineering far more modular. Once deposited, polydopamine could support secondary reactions, including metal deposition, attachment of long-chain molecular layers, and grafting of macromolecules that made surfaces bioactive or bioinert. This converted surface modification from a set of specialized, substrate-dependent procedures into a broadly portable platform: first coat with polydopamine, then build the desired function on top. The paper therefore helped open a route to multifunctional coatings for biomedical implants, membranes, sensors, catalysis supports, nanoparticles, and energy-related materials without redesigning the base material itself.

Its later influence came from this portability. Subsequent work expanded polydopamine coatings into antifouling interfaces, cell-adhesive and cell-repellent biomaterials, drug-delivery particles, filtration membranes, adhesive hydrogels, photothermal materials, and templates for metal or oxide nanostructures. Not every later application depended on the exact mechanistic interpretation of dopamine polymerization, which remains chemically complex, but the practical paradigm endured: mussel-inspired catechol chemistry could serve as a universal bridge between otherwise incompatible materials and functions.

## Abstract

We report a method to form multifunctional polymer coatings through simple dip-coating of objects in an aqueous solution of dopamine. Inspired by the composition of adhesive proteins in mussels, we used dopamine self-polymerization to form thin, surface-adherent polydopamine films onto a wide range of inorganic and organic materials, including noble metals, oxides, polymers, semiconductors, and ceramics. Secondary reactions can be used to create a variety of ad-layers, including self-assembled monolayers through deposition of long-chain molecular building blocks, metal films by electroless metallization, and bioinert and bioactive surfaces via grafting of macromolecules.
