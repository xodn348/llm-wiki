# A new method for sequencing DNA.

## Why this mattered

Maxam and Gilbert’s 1977 paper helped turn DNA sequence from an inferred or fragmentary property into a directly readable experimental object. Before methods like this, determining nucleotide order was slow, specialized, and generally limited to short or indirect cases. Their chemical-cleavage method showed that a terminally labeled DNA molecule could be split in base-specific reactions, separated on a gel, and read as an ordered sequence of bands. That made it newly practical to determine stretches of DNA sequence with single-base resolution, not merely map restriction sites or estimate composition.

The paradigm shift was that genes and regulatory regions could now be studied as exact linear information. Researchers could compare mutations to phenotypes at the nucleotide level, identify coding sequences, inspect promoters and other control elements, and verify cloned DNA constructs directly. The method also fit the emerging recombinant-DNA era: once fragments could be cloned, labeled, and sequenced, molecular biology gained a way to connect physical DNA molecules to precise biological meaning.

Although Sanger’s chain-termination approach soon became the dominant route to automated and large-scale sequencing, the Maxam-Gilbert paper was part of the same decisive transition: DNA sequencing became a general laboratory technology. That transition underwrote later breakthroughs in gene discovery, molecular diagnostics, evolutionary comparison, genome mapping, and ultimately whole-genome sequencing. Its lasting importance lies less in the long-term dominance of the chemistry itself than in proving that DNA could be read systematically, base by base, from experimental data.

## Abstract

DNA can be sequenced by a chemical procedure that breaks a terminally labeled DNA molecule partially at each repetition of a base. The lengths of the labeled fragments then identify the positions of that base. We describe reactions that cleave DNA preferentially at guanines, at adenines, at cytosines and thymines equally, and at cytosines alone. When the products of these four reactions are resolved by size, by electrophoresis on a polyacrylamide gel, the DNA sequence can be read from the pattern of radioactive bands. The technique will permit sequencing of at least 100 bases from the point of labeling.
