# Cosmic Black-Body Radiation.

## Why this mattered

Dicke, Peebles, Roll, and Wilkinson’s 1965 paper mattered because it supplied the cosmological interpretation for the newly detected microwave background: a nearly isotropic black-body radiation field as a relic of an early hot, dense phase of the universe. In doing so, it shifted the microwave signal from a local or instrumental curiosity into evidence bearing directly on cosmic history. The paper connected observable radiation temperature to big-bang nucleosynthesis, cosmic expansion, and the thermal past of the universe, helping make the hot Big Bang model empirically testable in a way that steady-state cosmology was not.

What became newly possible was precision cosmology from background radiation. Once the cosmic microwave background was understood as a fossil signal, its spectrum, isotropy, and later its tiny anisotropies became probes of the universe’s age, geometry, matter content, and early perturbations. This line of reasoning led directly to later breakthroughs: COBE’s confirmation of the black-body spectrum and detection of anisotropy, WMAP and Planck’s high-precision cosmological parameter measurements, and the modern ΛCDM framework. The paper’s importance is therefore not only that it explained a discovery, but that it helped define the CMB as a central observational pillar of cosmology.
