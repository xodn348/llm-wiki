# Relationship of Childhood Abuse and Household Dysfunction to Many of the Leading Causes of Death in Adults

## Why this mattered

Felitti et al. made childhood adversity legible as a population-level determinant of adult morbidity and mortality. The paper’s paradigm shift was not simply that abuse was harmful, but that ten categories of “adverse childhood experiences” clustered, showed a graded dose-response relationship, and were associated decades later with major adult risk factors and diseases, including smoking, alcoholism, drug use, depression, suicide attempt, ischemic heart disease, cancer, chronic lung disease, skeletal fractures, and liver disease. It reframed many leading causes of death as partly downstream of early social and household environments, not only of adult choices, genetics, or isolated biomedical exposures.

The study also made a new kind of preventive medicine possible. By giving clinicians and public-health researchers a simple cumulative ACE score, it turned diffuse childhood trauma and household dysfunction into a measurable exposure that could be studied epidemiologically, screened for clinically, and linked to later health outcomes across large cohorts. This helped shift attention from treating adult disease after onset toward identifying upstream developmental risks, trauma-informed care, and prevention strategies aimed at families, schools, and communities.

Subsequent work in developmental neuroscience, stress biology, psychiatry, and social epidemiology built on this framework by investigating mechanisms: chronic stress physiology, inflammation, altered neurodevelopment, health-risk behaviors, and intergenerational transmission of adversity. The paper did not prove that every observed association was causal, and its cohort was drawn from insured adults receiving care in a large health-maintenance organization, but its influence came from showing that early adversity could be studied with the same seriousness as other major risk exposures. It helped make childhood environment a central variable in explaining adult health.
