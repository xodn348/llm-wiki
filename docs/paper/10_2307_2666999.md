# Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams

## Why this mattered

Edmondson’s 1999 paper mattered because it shifted the study of organizational learning from an individual or cultural abstraction to a measurable team-level mechanism. “Psychological safety” gave researchers and managers a precise construct for something previously discussed more loosely: why some teams surface errors, ask for help, challenge assumptions, and learn in public, while others suppress the same information. The finding that psychological safety predicted learning behavior more strongly than team efficacy was especially important because it separated confidence in a team’s capability from willingness to take interpersonal risks. A team could believe it was competent and still fail to learn if members feared embarrassment, rejection, or blame.

The paper also made error reporting and speaking up newly interpretable as signs of a healthier learning system rather than simply as evidence of worse performance or lower discipline. This was a paradigm shift for high-stakes organizations, especially healthcare, manufacturing, aviation, and technology, where hidden failures can compound into systemic risk. By showing that learning behavior mediated the relationship between psychological safety and performance, Edmondson connected micro-level interpersonal climate to concrete organizational outcomes. That link helped move psychological safety from a “soft” workplace concern into a central variable in research on team effectiveness, safety culture, leadership, and continuous improvement.

Subsequent breakthroughs in team science and management practice built directly on this foundation. Later work on voice behavior, inclusive leadership, error management, agile teams, and high-reliability organizing treated psychological safety as a precondition for adaptation under uncertainty. Its influence also reached practitioner frameworks, including the widespread emphasis on blameless retrospectives, learning from failure, and leader behaviors that invite dissent. The paper’s lasting contribution was not merely naming a desirable climate, but showing why modern knowledge work depends on teams being able to convert uncertainty, mistakes, and disagreement into shared learning.

## Abstract

This paper presents a model of team learning and tests it in a multimethod field study. It introduces the construct of team psychological safety—a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking—and models the effects of team psychological safety and team efficacy together on learning and performance in organizational work teams. Results of a study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company, measuring antecedent, process, and outcome variables, show that team psychological safety is associated with learning behavior, but team efficacy is not, when controlling for team psychological safety. As predicted, learning behavior mediates between team psychological safety and team performance. The results support an integrative perspective in which both team structures, such as context support and team leader coaching, and shared beliefs shape team outcomes.
