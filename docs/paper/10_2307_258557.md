# Building Theories from Case Study Research

## Why this mattered

Eisenhardt’s 1989 article mattered because it made theory-building from case studies a legitimate, explicit, and teachable research strategy in management scholarship. Before it, case research was often treated as exploratory, descriptive, or methodologically suspect relative to large-sample hypothesis testing. Eisenhardt reframed it as a disciplined route to theory: cases could be selected theoretically, data could be collected with protocols, within-case and cross-case patterns could be compared systematically, and emerging constructs could be sharpened through iteration with evidence and literature. The paradigm shift was not simply “case studies are useful,” but that case studies could generate analytically rigorous, empirically grounded theory rather than merely illustrate theory developed elsewhere.

The paper made new kinds of organizational research possible, especially in settings where phenomena were new, fast-moving, poorly theorized, or hard to reduce to preexisting variables. Its method gave scholars a way to study entrepreneurial firms, technological change, strategic decision-making, innovation, alliances, and other organizational processes while theory was still forming. By emphasizing iteration between data, constructs, hypotheses, and literature, Eisenhardt helped normalize a mode of research in which discovery and rigor were not opposites. That was especially consequential for fields such as strategy, entrepreneurship, information systems, and organization theory, where important phenomena often appear before there are stable datasets or mature theoretical categories.

Its later influence is visible in the many influential process and organization studies that used comparative cases to build midrange theory: work on dynamic capabilities, new ventures, organizational learning, product development, and technology adoption all benefited from the methodological permission and structure Eisenhardt articulated. The paper also became a reference point for later debates about qualitative rigor, including how case-based theory differs from grounded theory, ethnography, and positivist hypothesis testing. Its enduring importance lies in giving management research a credible bridge between rich field observation and generalizable theoretical contribution, while also stating the risks clearly: case-built theory can become too complex, too narrow, or too idiosyncratic unless disciplined by comparison, evidence, and engagement with prior literature.

## Abstract

Describes the process of building theories from case studies and examines the strengths and weaknesses of this process. The case study research strategy is one in which the goal is to understand the dynamics present within an individual environment. The steps identified for building theory include selecting cases, crafting instruments and protocols, entering the field, analyzing data, shaping hypotheses, enfolding literature, and reaching closure. This process is highly iterative and closely tied to the empirical data. Using case studies to build theory has several advantages. First, it is likely to generate novel theory. Additionally, the theories generated are more likely to be built on constructs that are measurable, and these theories are likely to be empirically valid. On the other hand, this approach has weaknesses which include theories that are overly complex or narrow and idiosyncratic. This approach is best used in situations where one does not want to rely on previous literature or prior empirical evidence. The theories generated from this approach must be grounded in convincing evidence and should present novel ideas which meet the requirements of good theory. (SRD)
