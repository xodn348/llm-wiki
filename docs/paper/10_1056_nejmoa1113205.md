# Intratumor Heterogeneity and Branched Evolution Revealed by Multiregion Sequencing

## Why this mattered

Gerlinger et al. helped turn intratumor heterogeneity from a theoretical complication into a directly measurable evolutionary structure. By sequencing multiple spatially separated regions from the same renal cancers and metastases, the paper showed that a single biopsy could miss most of a tumor’s mutational landscape: 63–69% of somatic mutations were not present in every sampled region. The key shift was not simply that tumors were heterogeneous, but that this heterogeneity could be organized into phylogenetic trees, revealing branched evolution rather than a single linear path of progression.

This changed what cancer genomics could claim. Before this, genomic profiling often treated a tumor sample as a representative molecular snapshot suitable for prognosis, target selection, or biomarker discovery. This study showed that such snapshots could be misleading: different regions of the same tumor carried different driver alterations, different copy-number states, and even conflicting gene-expression signatures associated with good or poor prognosis. Its examples of convergent evolution, with distinct spatially separated inactivating mutations affecting genes such as *SETD2*, *PTEN*, and *KDM5C*, made clear that selection could repeatedly push different subclones toward similar functional outcomes.

The paper became foundational for later work on cancer evolution, clonal architecture, therapeutic resistance, and longitudinal tumor monitoring. It helped motivate multi-region sequencing studies, phylogenetic cancer atlases, and the search for “truncal” alterations shared by all tumor cells as more reliable therapeutic targets. It also clarified why resistance can emerge even when a therapy is well matched to one biopsy: treatment acts on a diverse population, not a uniform genome. In that sense, the study reframed precision oncology from matching drugs to a tumor’s detected mutations toward understanding the evolutionary population structure from which progression and relapse arise.

## Abstract

BACKGROUND: Intratumor heterogeneity may foster tumor evolution and adaptation and hinder personalized-medicine strategies that depend on results from single tumor-biopsy samples. METHODS: To examine intratumor heterogeneity, we performed exome sequencing, chromosome aberration analysis, and ploidy profiling on multiple spatially separated samples obtained from primary renal carcinomas and associated metastatic sites. We characterized the consequences of intratumor heterogeneity using immunohistochemical analysis, mutation functional analysis, and profiling of messenger RNA expression. RESULTS: Phylogenetic reconstruction revealed branched evolutionary tumor growth, with 63 to 69% of all somatic mutations not detectable across every tumor region. Intratumor heterogeneity was observed for a mutation within an autoinhibitory domain of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) kinase, correlating with S6 and 4EBP phosphorylation in vivo and constitutive activation of mTOR kinase activity in vitro. Mutational intratumor heterogeneity was seen for multiple tumor-suppressor genes converging on loss of function; SETD2, PTEN, and KDM5C underwent multiple distinct and spatially separated inactivating mutations within a single tumor, suggesting convergent phenotypic evolution. Gene-expression signatures of good and poor prognosis were detected in different regions of the same tumor. Allelic composition and ploidy profiling analysis revealed extensive intratumor heterogeneity, with 26 of 30 tumor samples from four tumors harboring divergent allelic-imbalance profiles and with ploidy heterogeneity in two of four tumors. CONCLUSIONS: Intratumor heterogeneity can lead to underestimation of the tumor genomics landscape portrayed from single tumor-biopsy samples and may present major challenges to personalized-medicine and biomarker development. Intratumor heterogeneity, associated with heterogeneous protein function, may foster tumor adaptation and therapeutic failure through Darwinian selection.
