# Profiling of complex microbial populations by denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis analysis of polymerase chain reaction-amplified genes coding for 16S rRNA

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, complex microbial communities were still difficult to study without cultivation, which strongly biased what could be observed. Muyzer, de Waal, and Uitterlinden showed that PCR-amplified 16S rRNA gene fragments of identical length could be separated by sequence-dependent melting behavior using DGGE, producing a reproducible community “fingerprint” from environmental DNA. This made it possible to compare microbial populations directly, detect organisms present at roughly 1% abundance, and associate individual bands with phylogenetic groups using targeted probes.

The paradigm shift was that microbial ecology could move from asking which organisms could be grown in the laboratory to asking which organisms were present in a community at all. The paper did not yet provide the exhaustive taxonomic resolution later associated with high-throughput sequencing, but it established a practical molecular route for profiling uncultured microbial diversity across samples. Its biofilm example, where sulfate-reducing bacteria were detected in an aerobic setting, illustrated the new kind of ecological inference the method enabled: organisms could be found in unexpected niches because the assay read community DNA rather than culture behavior.

DGGE became one of the key bridge technologies between early 16S rRNA phylogenetics and later amplicon sequencing surveys. It helped normalize the idea that environmental microbial communities could be represented as molecular profiles, compared across conditions, and linked to specific taxa through sequence or probe information. Later breakthroughs in clone-library surveys, metagenomics, and next-generation 16S sequencing greatly expanded scale and resolution, but they built on the same conceptual turn: microbial communities could be studied as DNA-defined assemblages rather than as collections of cultivable isolates.

## Abstract

We describe a new molecular approach to analyzing the genetic diversity of complex microbial populations. This technique is based on the separation of polymerase chain reaction-amplified fragments of genes coding for 16S rRNA, all the same length, by denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE). DGGE analysis of different microbial communities demonstrated the presence of up to 10 distinguishable bands in the separation pattern, which were most likely derived from as many different species constituting these populations, and thereby generated a DGGE profile of the populations. We showed that it is possible to identify constituents which represent only 1% of the total population. With an oligonucleotide probe specific for the V3 region of 16S rRNA of sulfate-reducing bacteria, particular DNA fragments from some of the microbial populations could be identified by hybridization analysis. Analysis of the genomic DNA from a bacterial biofilm grown under aerobic conditions suggests that sulfate-reducing bacteria, despite their anaerobicity, were present in this environment. The results we obtained demonstrate that this technique will contribute to our understanding of the genetic diversity of uncharacterized microbial populations.
