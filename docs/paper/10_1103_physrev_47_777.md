# Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?

## Why this mattered

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen reframed a technical dispute over quantum measurement into a sharp criterion for what a physical theory should explain. The paper’s central move was to connect “completeness” to predictable properties of spatially separated systems: if one can predict a quantity with certainty without disturbing the system, then that quantity should correspond to an element of reality. By applying this to entangled particles, EPR argued that the quantum wave function could not be a complete description unless one abandoned the idea that distant systems have independent real properties. This made the tension between quantum mechanics, locality, and realism explicit in a way earlier debates had not.

The immediate historical impact was not that EPR overthrew quantum mechanics; Bohr’s reply defended the orthodox view, and the formalism continued to succeed. Its deeper importance was that it isolated a conceptual pressure point that could later be made experimentally testable. Schrödinger’s subsequent discussion of “entanglement” was directly stimulated by EPR, and John Bell’s 1964 theorem transformed the EPR dilemma from philosophy into physics: any theory preserving EPR-style local realism must satisfy inequalities that quantum mechanics can violate. Later experiments by Freedman and Clauser, Aspect, and ultimately loophole-closing Bell tests showed that nature does not obey those local-realist constraints.

In retrospect, the EPR paper helped create the conceptual foundation for quantum information science. What EPR presented as evidence of incompleteness became, in later work, a resource: entanglement underlies quantum cryptography, teleportation, dense coding, and quantum computing. The paper mattered because it forced physicists to ask not merely whether quantum mechanics predicts correctly, but what kind of reality its predictions permit. That question reshaped the foundations of physics and opened a path from interpretive debate to experimentally grounded technologies.

## Abstract

In a complete theory there is an element corresponding to each element of reality. A sufficient condition for the reality of a physical quantity is the possibility of predicting it with certainty, without disturbing the system. In quantum mechanics in the case of two physical quantities described by non-commuting operators, the knowledge of one precludes the knowledge of the other. Then either (1) the description of reality given by the wave function in quantum mechanics is not complete or (2) these two quantities cannot have simultaneous reality. Consideration of the problem of making predictions concerning a system on the basis of measurements made on another system that had previously interacted with it leads to the result that if (1) is false then (2) is also false. One is thus led to conclude that the description of reality as given by a wave function is not complete.
