# The Theory of Island Biogeography

## Why this mattered

*Bibliographic note: the 1968* Geographical Journal *item appears to be a review/notice by M. J. Kirkby of MacArthur and Wilson’s 1967 Princeton monograph, not the original theory paper itself. The scientific contribution discussed here is MacArthur and Wilson’s equilibrium theory of island biogeography, documented in library and publisher records.*

This mattered because it turned island biogeography from a largely descriptive, historical field into a quantitative, predictive theory. MacArthur and Wilson argued that the number of species on an island could be modeled as a dynamic balance between immigration and extinction, with isolation chiefly affecting immigration and area chiefly affecting extinction. That was the paradigm shift: islands were no longer just special natural-history cases, but simplified systems in which general ecological processes could be formalized, tested, and compared.

After this work, ecologists could ask sharper questions: how many species should a habitat patch contain, how fast should turnover occur, and how should size and distance alter biodiversity? The framework helped make field experiments, species-area analyses, and reserve-design debates theoretically tractable. Its influence extended beyond oceanic islands to fragmented forests, mountaintops, lakes, urban habitat patches, and other “islands” of habitat, making it central to conservation biology and landscape ecology.

The theory also shaped later breakthroughs by exposing both the power and limits of neutral, process-based community models. Subsequent work built on its immigration-extinction logic in metapopulation biology, reserve design, and Hubbell’s unified neutral theory of biodiversity, while also challenging its simplifying assumptions about species equivalence, speciation, and relative abundance. Its lasting importance is therefore not that it solved biogeography, but that it supplied a compact model ecology could argue with, test, generalize, and revise.
