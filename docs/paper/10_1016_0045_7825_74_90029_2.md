# The numerical computation of turbulent flows

## Why this mattered

Launder and Spalding’s 1974 paper mattered because it helped turn turbulent-flow simulation from a case-by-case research craft into a general engineering method. By formulating a practical two-equation Reynolds-averaged turbulence closure, now known as the standard *k-ε* model, the paper gave computational fluid dynamics a reusable way to estimate turbulent viscosity from transported quantities: turbulent kinetic energy *k* and its dissipation rate *ε*. This did not solve turbulence from first principles, but it made turbulent flows numerically tractable on the computers of the time.

The paradigm shift was pragmatic: instead of requiring detailed resolution of turbulent eddies or highly problem-specific empirical correlations, engineers could compute mean turbulent flows in ducts, boundary layers, jets, wakes, and recirculating regions within a common finite-volume framework. The model’s use of wall functions and semi-empirical constants made it especially suited to industrial prediction, where full near-wall resolution was then impossible. After this paper, CFD could increasingly be used as a design tool rather than only as a research demonstration.

Its influence is visible in nearly every later branch of applied turbulence modeling. The standard *k-ε* model became a baseline against which variants such as RNG *k-ε*, realizable *k-ε*, *k-ω*, SST, Reynolds-stress models, LES, and hybrid RANS–LES methods were compared. Many later breakthroughs improved its treatment of separation, anisotropy, rotation, near-wall behavior, or unsteady large-scale structures, but they did so in a field whose computational workflow and engineering expectations had been shaped by Launder and Spalding’s formulation.
