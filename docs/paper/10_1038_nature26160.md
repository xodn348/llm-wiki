# Unconventional superconductivity in magic-angle graphene superlattices

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it showed that superconductivity could be engineered in a material made only from carbon sheets by controlling a geometric parameter: the twist angle between two graphene layers. Near the “magic angle” of about 1.1 degrees, the moiré superlattice produces extremely flat electronic bands, making electron-electron interactions dominant. Cao and colleagues found superconductivity emerging when carriers were added near correlated insulating states, establishing twisted bilayer graphene as a clean, tunable platform for studying strongly correlated quantum matter.

The paradigm shift was not simply another superconducting material, but a new way to make and control correlated phases. Instead of relying on chemically complex compounds such as cuprates, heavy fermion materials, or iron-based superconductors, researchers could tune bandwidth, carrier density, displacement field, and topology in a van der Waals device. This made “twistronics” a central experimental program: by rotating atomically thin layers, one could design electronic bands and induce phases that previously seemed tied to specific crystal chemistries.

Subsequent work built directly on this result, extending magic-angle physics to orbital magnetism, Chern insulators, strange-metal behavior, cascade transitions, spin- and valley-polarized states, and superconductivity in other moiré systems. The paper helped turn moiré materials into a broadly applicable route for quantum simulation in solids: a way to reproduce, tune, and compare many-body phenomena that had long been difficult to isolate in conventional materials.
