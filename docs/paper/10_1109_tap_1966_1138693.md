# Numerical solution of initial boundary value problems involving maxwell's equations in isotropic media

## Why this mattered

Yee’s 1966 paper mattered because it turned Maxwell’s equations from a primarily analytic or frequency-domain boundary-value problem into a directly computable time-domain evolution problem. Its central innovation was not merely “using finite differences,” but placing the electric and magnetic field components on a staggered space-time lattice so that the discrete update equations mirror the curl structure of Maxwell’s equations. This made it possible to propagate electromagnetic fields step by step while naturally accommodating perfectly conducting boundaries, as demonstrated by pulse scattering from a conducting cylinder.

The resulting Yee scheme became the foundation of finite-difference time-domain methods. After this paper, engineers and physicists could simulate broadband electromagnetic transients, scattering, radiation, waveguides, antennas, microwave circuits, and later photonic and nanophotonic structures without deriving a new closed-form solution for each geometry. The method shifted computational electromagnetics toward general-purpose numerical experimentation: geometry, materials, sources, and boundary conditions could be encoded and evolved rather than solved case by case.

Its influence also came from how well the discretization matched later computational trends. As digital computers grew more powerful, Yee’s grid became a practical engine for large-scale electromagnetic simulation, and later developments such as absorbing boundary conditions, perfectly matched layers, subgridding, dispersive-material models, and parallel FDTD solvers extended the original framework rather than replacing it. In that sense, the paper did not just solve one class of Maxwell problems; it supplied the computational template through which much of modern time-domain electromagnetics became possible.

## Abstract

Maxwell's equations are replaced by a set of finite difference equations. It is shown that if one chooses the field points appropriately, the set of finite difference equations is applicable for a boundary condition involving perfectly conducting surfaces. An example is given of the scattering of an electromagnetic pulse by a perfectly conducting cylinder.
