# Positive psychology: An introduction.

## Why this mattered

Before this article, postwar psychology was largely organized around treating disorder: diagnosis, pathology, and repair. Seligman and Csíkszentmihályi argued that this emphasis had narrowed the field’s mission, leaving underdeveloped the scientific study of ordinary and exceptional human strengths. The paradigm shift was not the claim that happiness or virtue were new topics, but that well-being, meaning, optimism, flow, resilience, creativity, and civic virtue should be treated as legitimate objects of rigorous psychological science rather than as philosophical, moral, or self-help concerns.

The paper helped make possible a coordinated research program around positive subjective experience, positive individual traits, and positive institutions. By naming “positive psychology” as a field and placing it in *American Psychologist*, the authors gave researchers a shared vocabulary and agenda: measure well-being, identify conditions that support flourishing, and test interventions aimed not only at reducing symptoms but at building capacities. This reframed mental health as more than the absence of illness and encouraged empirical work on life satisfaction, character strengths, gratitude, meaning, hope, and engagement.

Its later influence is visible in the growth of well-being science, positive education, organizational scholarship on engagement and strengths, and clinical approaches that supplement symptom reduction with practices designed to increase flourishing. Subsequent work also complicated the original program, challenging simplistic happiness-maximization claims and pressing for stronger evidence, cultural breadth, and attention to inequality. Even so, the 2000 article mattered because it shifted psychology’s center of gravity: it made the study of what goes right in human life a mainstream scientific project.
