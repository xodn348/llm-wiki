# The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money.

## Why this mattered

Keynes’s *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* mattered because it broke with the classical view that market economies naturally tend toward full employment through flexible wages, prices, and interest rates. Keynes argued that aggregate demand could settle at a level too low to employ all willing workers, making prolonged unemployment an equilibrium outcome rather than a temporary friction. This shifted macroeconomics from a theory centered on individual markets and price adjustment to one centered on national income, investment, saving, liquidity preference, and expectations.

The work made possible a new policy framework: governments and central banks could analyze recessions as failures of demand and respond with fiscal expansion, monetary policy, and stabilization tools rather than waiting for self-correction. It helped establish macroeconomics as a distinct field, shaped national income accounting, and gave intellectual foundations to postwar demand management, welfare-state planning, and institutions concerned with employment and growth.

Its later influence was partly through debate and revision. The Keynesian synthesis, IS-LM modeling, econometrics, monetarist criticism, rational expectations, New Keynesian economics, and modern DSGE models all developed in relation to problems Keynes posed: how expectations, money, interest rates, investment, and coordination failures shape economy-wide outcomes. Note: the canonical 1936 work was authored by John Maynard Keynes and published as a book, not by Schumpeter in the *Journal of the American Statistical Association*.
