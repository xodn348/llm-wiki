# Psychophysical bases of perceived exertion

## Why this mattered

Borg’s paper mattered because it helped legitimize perceived exertion as a quantitative scientific variable rather than a vague subjective impression. Its central shift was psychophysical: effort could be scaled, compared, and interpreted with enough structure to complement heart rate, workload, oxygen uptake, and other physiological measures. The Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion made internal bodily experience usable in laboratories, clinics, workplaces, and sports settings, especially where direct physiological measurement was impractical or incomplete.

The paper also clarified that different measurement purposes required different scaling methods. Ratio-scaling approaches were useful for describing general perceptual variation, while category scales were often better for applied settings involving individual differences. By introducing and defending scales that connected subjective categories with psychophysical properties, Borg made it possible to prescribe, monitor, and study exercise intensity through a person’s own perception without treating that perception as merely anecdotal.

That framework became foundational for later work in exercise testing, rehabilitation, occupational health, sports training, and fatigue research. Subsequent breakthroughs in self-regulated exercise, clinical exercise prescription, cardiac and pulmonary rehabilitation, and human factors all relied on the idea that perceived effort is not just a byproduct of physiology but an informative measurement channel in its own right. The enduring influence of the paper comes from that bridge: it joined psychophysics with applied physiology and gave researchers and practitioners a common language for the subjective experience of physical work.

## Abstract

There is a great demand for perceptual effort ratings in order to better understand man at work. Such ratings are important complements to behavioral and physiological measurements of physical performance and work capacity. This is true for both theoretical analysis and application in medicine, human factors, and sports. Perceptual estimates, obtained by psychophysical ratio-scaling methods, are valid when describing general perceptual variation, but category methods are more useful in several applied situations when differences between individuals are described. A presentation is made of ratio-scaling methods, category methods, especially the Borg Scale for ratings of perceived exertion, and a new method that combines the category method with ratio properties. Some of the advantages and disadvantages of the different methods are discussed in both theoretical-psychophysical and psychophysiological frames of reference.
