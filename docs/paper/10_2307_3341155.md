# Risk Society, Towards a New Modernity

## Why this mattered

*Risk Society* shifted social theory from asking mainly how modern societies distribute wealth, status, and class position to asking how they also produce, define, and distribute hazards. Beck’s central move was to treat risks such as nuclear catastrophe, toxic pollution, ecological degradation, and technological side effects not as external accidents of modernization but as products of modernization itself. That reframed advanced industrial society as “living on the volcano” of its own success: science, industry, and state administration had become sources of both security and danger.

The work made it newly possible to analyze environmental and technological controversies as problems of knowledge, legitimacy, and politics, not simply as technical failures awaiting expert correction. Its account of “reflexive modernization” showed how modern institutions come under pressure from the unintended consequences they generate, while its emphasis on individualization connected large-scale structural change to altered biographies, family forms, labor markets, and identities. In this sense, the book helped move sociology beyond a simple industrial/post-industrial divide and gave later researchers a vocabulary for studying uncertainty, precaution, expertise, public trust, and contested science.

Its influence is visible in later work on reflexive modernization, world risk society, science and technology studies, environmental sociology, risk communication, public health, security, and climate politics. Subsequent breakthroughs did not simply accept Beck’s thesis; many refined or challenged it by asking how risk is culturally framed, institutionally governed, globally unequal, and politically mobilized. But that debate itself marks the paradigm shift: after *Risk Society*, the dangers produced by progress became central evidence for understanding modernity, rather than anomalies at its margins.
