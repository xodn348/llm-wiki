# Amazon's Mechanical Turk

## Why this mattered

Buhrmester, Kwang, and Gosling’s paper mattered because it helped legitimize a new experimental infrastructure for the social sciences. Before MTurk, much behavioral research depended on convenience samples, especially undergraduates, or on slower and more expensive survey panels. The paper did not claim that MTurk solved sampling bias; its central contribution was more practical and methodological: it showed that an online labor market could supply participants quickly, cheaply, and with data quality comparable to traditional methods, while offering greater demographic diversity than standard college samples. That shifted MTurk from a curious web platform into a credible research instrument.

The paradigm shift was that participant recruitment became programmable. Researchers could now run studies at larger scale, iterate instruments quickly, test replications, pretest stimuli, and collect behavioral or judgment data within hours rather than weeks. This changed the tempo of empirical psychology and adjacent fields: experimental economics, political science, marketing, linguistics, human-computer interaction, and judgment-and-decision-making all gained access to fast, inexpensive human-subject data collection. The paper became one of the early methodological anchors for that transition, giving researchers evidence they could cite when defending online samples to reviewers and editors.

Its influence also set the stage for later developments in online experimentation and reproducibility. MTurk enabled many high-powered studies, rapid replication attempts, norming datasets, and large-scale behavioral experiments that would have been difficult under the older lab-based model. At the same time, the very success of MTurk exposed new methodological problems: nonnaivete, inattentive responding, bot activity, duplicated participation, and limits on population generalizability. In that sense, the paper was paradigm-shifting not because it made online samples unproblematic, but because it made them central enough that the field had to build new standards for screening, attention checks, preregistration, replication, and platform-aware research design.

## Abstract

Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk) is a relatively new website that contains the major elements required to conduct research: an integrated participant compensation system; a large participant pool; and a streamlined process of study design, participant recruitment, and data collection. In this article, we describe and evaluate the potential contributions of MTurk to psychology and other social sciences. Findings indicate that (a) MTurk participants are slightly more demographically diverse than are standard Internet samples and are significantly more diverse than typical American college samples; (b) participation is affected by compensation rate and task length, but participants can still be recruited rapidly and inexpensively; (c) realistic compensation rates do not affect data quality; and (d) the data obtained are at least as reliable as those obtained via traditional methods. Overall, MTurk can be used to obtain high-quality data inexpensively and rapidly.
