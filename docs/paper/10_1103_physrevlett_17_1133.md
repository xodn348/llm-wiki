# Absence of Ferromagnetism or Antiferromagnetism in One- or Two-Dimensional Isotropic Heisenberg Models

## Why this mattered

Mermin and Wagner’s paper changed the status of low-dimensional magnetism from a matter of model intuition to a rigorous constraint on what equilibrium statistical mechanics permits. By proving that one- and two-dimensional isotropic Heisenberg systems with finite-range interactions cannot sustain ferromagnetic or antiferromagnetic long-range order at any nonzero temperature, it showed that dimensionality and continuous symmetry are not secondary details: they can forbid an ordered phase even when the microscopic interaction strongly favors alignment. The result helped make fluctuations, especially long-wavelength spin-wave fluctuations, central to the understanding of phases rather than mere corrections to mean-field pictures.

Its importance was also methodological. The proof became a template for excluding spontaneous breaking of continuous symmetries in low dimensions, later generalized beyond spin systems and absorbed into what is now called the Mermin-Wagner theorem. It clarified why ordinary symmetry-breaking order is absent in many one- and two-dimensional systems while leaving room for more subtle forms of organization. That distinction became essential for later developments such as the Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless transition, where two-dimensional systems evade conventional long-range order yet still undergo a topological phase transition with quasi-long-range order.

The paper therefore helped redirect condensed matter theory toward the modern language of symmetry, dimensionality, correlations, and collective modes. It set a boundary condition for any theory of low-dimensional materials: claims of finite-temperature magnetic order in isotropic short-range models had to confront the theorem’s assumptions, by invoking anisotropy, long-range interactions, finite-size effects, nonequilibrium behavior, or reduced symmetry. In that sense, the result did not close the subject of low-dimensional order; it made the field sharper by defining exactly where conventional order fails and where new mechanisms must begin.

## Abstract

It is rigorously proved that at any nonzero temperature, a one- or two-dimensional isotropic spin-$S$ Heisenberg model with finite-range exchange interaction can be neither ferromagnetic nor antiferromagnetic. The method of proof is capable of excluding a variety of types of ordering in one and two dimensions.
