# The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project.

## Why this mattered

The 2013 [GTEx project paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23715323/) mattered because it reframed a central problem left by genome-wide association studies: disease-associated loci were abundant, but their biological mechanisms were usually opaque. Since most GWAS hits did not alter protein sequence, GTEx made regulatory variation the object of systematic measurement, linking genotype to RNA expression across many normal human tissues. The paradigm shift was from asking only *where* disease-associated variants lie in the genome to asking *in which tissues, for which genes, and through what regulatory effects* they act.

What became newly possible was a population-scale reference map for expression quantitative trait loci, allele-specific expression, splicing effects, and tissue-specific regulatory architecture. Instead of treating blood or cell lines as imperfect proxies for the whole body, researchers could compare genetic effects across organs and tissues, making it easier to prioritize candidate causal genes at noncoding GWAS loci and to separate shared regulatory effects from tissue-restricted ones. The associated tissue bank also gave the field a durable infrastructure model: genotype, transcriptome, metadata, and biospecimens organized as a community resource rather than a one-off study.

Subsequent work built directly on this shift. Later GTEx releases and atlas papers, including the mature cross-tissue regulatory map reported by the [GTEx Consortium](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32913098/), made regulatory interpretation a routine part of human genetics. Methods such as colocalization, transcriptome-wide association studies, fine-mapping of eQTLs and sQTLs, and tissue-prioritized functional annotation all gained power from the GTEx framework. Its lasting importance was not a single disease discovery, but the creation of a reference layer that connected statistical genetics to molecular mechanism at human-tissue scale.

## Abstract

Genome-wide association studies have identified thousands of loci for common diseases, but, for the majority of these, the mechanisms underlying disease susceptibility remain unknown. Most associated variants are not correlated with protein-coding changes, suggesting that polymorphisms in regulatory regions probably contribute to many disease phenotypes. Here we describe the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project, which will establish a resource database and associated tissue bank for the scientific community to study the relationship between genetic variation and gene expression in human tissues.
