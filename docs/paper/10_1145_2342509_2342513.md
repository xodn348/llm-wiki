# Fog computing and its role in the internet of things

## Why this mattered

Bonomi, Milito, Zhu, and Addepalli’s paper mattered because it gave a clear name and architecture to a shift already becoming unavoidable: computation could no longer be treated as something that happened only in distant cloud data centers. By defining “fog” as an extension of cloud computing to the network edge, the paper reframed latency, mobility, geography, wireless access, and real-time data streams as first-class design constraints rather than peripheral engineering problems. This was especially important for IoT, where devices such as vehicles, grid sensors, city infrastructure, and wireless actuator networks produce data that is local, time-sensitive, and often impractical to route entirely through centralized clouds.

The paradigm shift was not simply moving servers closer to users; it was treating the edge of the network as an active computing layer. After this framing, applications such as connected vehicles, smart grids, and smart cities could be described as distributed systems spanning sensors, access networks, edge nodes, and cloud backends. That made newly practical a class of services requiring low-latency decisions, location awareness, and resilience under mobility: traffic coordination, grid protection, industrial monitoring, emergency response, and real-time analytics over geographically dispersed devices.

The paper also anticipated the later rise of edge computing, mobile edge/cloudlet architectures, 5G multi-access edge computing, and AI inference at the edge. Its influence came from articulating why cloud centralization alone was insufficient for IoT-scale systems and why computation needed to be placed along the continuum between device and cloud. Subsequent breakthroughs in autonomous systems, smart infrastructure, edge analytics, and privacy-preserving local processing all built on this same premise: for many important workloads, where computation happens is as consequential as what computation is performed.

## Abstract

Fog Computing extends the Cloud Computing paradigm to the edge of the network, thus enabling a new breed of applications and services. Defining characteristics of the Fog are: a) Low latency and location awareness; b) Wide-spread geographical distribution; c) Mobility; d) Very large number of nodes, e) Predominant role of wireless access, f) Strong presence of streaming and real time applications, g) Heterogeneity. In this paper we argue that the above characteristics make the Fog the appropriate platform for a number of critical Internet of Things (IoT) services and applications, namely, Connected Vehicle, Smart Grid, Smart Cities, and, in general, Wireless Sensors and Actuators Networks (WSANs).
