# LIII. <i>On lines and planes of closest fit to systems of points in space</i>

## Why this mattered

Pearson’s paper gave a geometric and statistical formulation for reducing a cloud of observations to the line or plane that best represents its variation. Unlike ordinary regression, which treats one variable as dependent and minimizes vertical residuals, Pearson minimized distances perpendicular to the fitted line or plane. This reframed multivariate data as a shape in space whose principal directions could be found and interpreted. In modern terms, the paper introduced the core idea of principal component analysis: replacing many correlated measurements with a smaller set of orthogonal axes that capture the greatest variance.

The shift mattered because it made high-dimensional empirical structure mathematically tractable. After Pearson, scientists could summarize complex systems of measurements without first deciding which variable should explain which other variable. That was especially important for biometrics, anthropology, psychometrics, and later genetics, fields in which many traits vary together and the main question is often the pattern of covariation itself. The paper helped move statistics from pairwise association toward the analysis of entire systems of variables.

Its later influence is visible across twentieth-century statistics and machine learning. Hotelling’s 1930s formalization of principal components built directly on this geometric idea, and PCA became a standard tool for dimensionality reduction, visualization, noise filtering, feature extraction, and latent-structure discovery. Many later breakthroughs, from factor analysis and multivariate statistics to modern unsupervised learning and data compression, depend on the paradigm Pearson helped establish: that the essential information in complex data can often be found by rotating the coordinate system to expose its dominant directions of variation.
