# The obligatory role of endothelial cells in the relaxation of arterial smooth muscle by acetylcholine

## Why this mattered

Furchgott and Zawadzki’s 1980 paper overturned the prevailing view that acetylcholine acted directly on arterial smooth muscle to cause relaxation. By showing that relaxation required an intact endothelial lining, they made the endothelium an active signaling organ rather than a passive barrier between blood and vessel wall. The key conceptual shift was that vascular tone could be controlled by a diffusible factor released from endothelial cells in response to a stimulus.

This made a new experimental program possible: identifying the “endothelium-derived relaxing factor” (EDRF), mapping how it was produced, and explaining how it acted on smooth muscle. In the years that followed, EDRF was shown to be nitric oxide, linking vascular physiology to a simple gaseous signaling molecule and helping establish cyclic GMP signaling as a central pathway in vasodilation.

The paper also became a foundation for modern vascular biology and cardiovascular pharmacology. It reframed hypertension, atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and inflammatory vascular disease as conditions involving endothelial dysfunction, not only smooth-muscle or hemodynamic abnormalities. The line from this experiment runs through the nitric oxide discoveries recognized by the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine and into therapies and research programs built around endothelial signaling, nitric oxide bioavailability, and vascular homeostasis.
