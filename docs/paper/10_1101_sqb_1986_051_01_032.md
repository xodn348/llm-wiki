# Specific Enzymatic Amplification of DNA In Vitro: The Polymerase Chain Reaction

## Why this mattered

Before PCR, isolating and analyzing a defined DNA sequence usually depended on cloning: the sequence had to be inserted into a vector, propagated in cells, and recovered using restriction sites or hybridization-based methods. Mullis and colleagues described a different paradigm: a chosen segment of DNA could be amplified directly *in vitro* by repeated cycles of primer-directed synthesis. The crucial shift was conceptual as much as technical. DNA was no longer only something to be cloned out of biological material; a specific region could be selectively made abundant from a complex mixture, provided that short flanking sequences were known.

That change made trace amounts of DNA experimentally usable. PCR turned rare or inaccessible sequences into material that could be detected, sequenced, cloned, or tested for variation, opening routine analysis of single genes, pathogens, ancient or forensic samples, and clinical specimens. It also changed the economics and timescale of molecular biology: experiments that had required days to weeks of cloning and screening could often be reduced to designed primers, thermal cycling, and downstream analysis.

The later impact of PCR was amplified by subsequent improvements, especially thermostable DNA polymerases that made automated cycling practical and robust. From that foundation came major breakthroughs in genetic diagnostics, infectious-disease testing, forensic DNA profiling, genome mapping, mutational screening, and eventually high-throughput sequencing workflows in which targeted amplification became a standard preparatory step. The paper therefore marks a transition from molecular biology as manipulation of cloned DNA to molecular biology as programmable amplification of information-bearing sequences.

## Abstract

The discovery of specific restriction endonucleases (Smith and Wilcox 1970) made possible the isolation of discrete molecular fragments of naturally occurring DNA for the first time. This capability was crucial to the development of molecular cloning (Cohen et al. 1973); and the combination of molecular cloning and endonuclease restriction allowed the synthesis and isolation of any naturally occurring DNA sequence that could be cloned into a useful vector and, on the basis of flanking restriction sites, excised from it. The availability of a large variety of restriction enzymes (Roberts 1985) has significantly extended the utility of these methods.
