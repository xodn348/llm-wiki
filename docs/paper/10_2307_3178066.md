# Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective

## Why this mattered

Haraway’s “Situated Knowledges” mattered because it reframed the feminist critique of objectivity from a rejection of science into a reconstruction of what stronger objectivity could mean. Against both universal “view from nowhere” claims and simple relativism, Haraway argued that all knowledge is produced from embodied, historically specific positions, and that acknowledging those positions can make inquiry more accountable rather than less scientific. The paper helped turn “partial perspective” into a positive epistemic principle: not a weakness to be overcome, but a condition for responsible knowledge-making.

The paradigm shift was to make location, embodiment, power, and accountability central to theories of scientific knowledge without abandoning truth claims. After the paper, feminist science studies, science and technology studies, anthropology of science, critical geography, disability studies, critical race theory, and later data and AI ethics had a vocabulary for asking who sees, from where, with what instruments, and with what consequences. It made possible a style of analysis in which objectivity is assessed not by pretending the observer is absent, but by examining the relations, exclusions, and material practices that make observations count as knowledge.

Its influence is visible in later work on standpoint epistemology, strong objectivity, participatory research, postcolonial science studies, and critiques of algorithmic neutrality. Haraway did not supply a method in the narrow sense; she supplied a durable epistemic orientation. Subsequent breakthroughs in studying laboratories, classification systems, medical knowledge, environmental science, and machine learning bias often depend on the move she crystallized: treating scientific facts as real and consequential while also treating their conditions of production as politically and materially situated.
