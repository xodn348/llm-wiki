# Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm

## Why this mattered

Entman’s paper mattered because it turned “framing” from a widely used but conceptually loose term into a portable analytic paradigm. Before 1993, scholars across sociology, psychology, political communication, media studies, and discourse analysis used related ideas, but often without a shared vocabulary or cumulative research program. Entman’s key move was to define framing as selecting aspects of perceived reality and making them more salient in a text, in ways that promote particular problem definitions, causal interpretations, moral evaluations, and treatment recommendations. That formulation made framing empirically tractable: researchers could ask not only whether media influenced opinion, but *how* a message organized reality and made some interpretations more available than others.

The paradigm shift was especially important because it offered a bridge between micro-level cognition and macro-level public discourse. Framing could explain how individual information processing, journalistic routines, elite strategy, institutional power, and cultural narratives interacted without reducing communication effects to simple persuasion or agenda-setting. After Entman, scholars could systematically study the movement of frames across political speeches, news coverage, public opinion, and policy debate. This made possible a large body of later work on media bias, race and crime coverage, war reporting, public health communication, social movements, and strategic political messaging.

Its influence also came from the paper’s disciplinary argument. Entman treated communication’s “fractured” status not merely as a weakness but as an opportunity: the field could synthesize concepts scattered across other disciplines and turn them into rigorous, shared theory. Subsequent breakthroughs in agenda-building, priming-framing distinctions, cascading activation models, and comparative media systems all benefited from this clarification. The paper did not settle every dispute about framing, but it gave later researchers a common reference point for cumulative debate, measurement, and theory-building.

## Abstract

In response to the proposition that communication lacks disciplinary sta-tus because of deficient core knowledge, I propose that we turn an osten-sible weakness into a strength. We should identify our mission as bring-ing together insights and theories that would otherwise remain scattered in other disciplines. Because of the lack of interchange among the disci-plines, hypotheses thoroughly discredited in one field may receive wide acceptance in another. Potential research paradigms remain fractured, with pieces here and there but no comprehensive statement to guide re-search. By bringing ideas together in one location, communication can aspire to become a master discipline that synthesizes related theories and concepts and exposes them to the most rigorous, comprehensive state-ment and exploration. Reaching this goal would require a more self-con-scious determination by communication scholars to plumb other fields and feed back their studies to outside researchers. At the same time, such an enterprise would enhance the theoretical rigor of communication
