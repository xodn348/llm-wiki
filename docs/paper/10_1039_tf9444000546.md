# Wettability of porous surfaces

## Why this mattered

Cassie and Baxter’s paper mattered because it changed wettability from a property of a flat, chemically uniform solid into an interfacial design problem. By treating a liquid drop on a porous or rough surface as contacting a composite of solid and trapped air, they showed that the observed contact angle could be predicted from the area fractions of those phases. This was a conceptual shift: apparent water repellency was not only a matter of surface chemistry, as in Young’s ideal surface picture, but could be engineered through texture and porosity.

The Cassie-Baxter model made it possible to reason quantitatively about surfaces that suspend liquid above air pockets, explaining why some rough hydrophobic materials exhibit much higher apparent contact angles than their chemistry alone would allow. Together with Wenzel’s earlier roughness model, it became one of the two foundational limiting descriptions of wetting on textured solids: Wenzel for fully wetted roughness, Cassie-Baxter for composite wetting. That distinction later became central to understanding contact-angle hysteresis, droplet mobility, wetting transitions, and the stability or failure of water-repellent surfaces.

Its influence became especially visible after the rise of biomimetic and micro/nanostructured materials research, including work inspired by natural superhydrophobic surfaces such as lotus leaves. The paper supplied the language and governing relation behind later breakthroughs in superhydrophobic coatings, self-cleaning surfaces, anti-icing materials, drag-reducing interfaces, and liquid-repellent textiles. Its lasting importance is that it turned extreme wettability into something that could be modeled, fabricated, and optimized rather than merely observed.
