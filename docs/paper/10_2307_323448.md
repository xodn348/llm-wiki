# The Social Construction of Reality

## Why this mattered

Berger and Luckmann’s *The Social Construction of Reality* mattered because it shifted the sociology of knowledge away from treating “knowledge” mainly as formal ideas, ideologies, or elite intellectual systems, and toward the ordinary, taken-for-granted world of everyday life. Its central move was to show how institutions, roles, language, typifications, and legitimation make humanly produced social arrangements appear objective, natural, and durable. That gave social theory a disciplined vocabulary for analyzing how society is both made by people and experienced by people as an external reality that shapes them.

The work made it newly possible to study “reality” itself as a social achievement without reducing it to individual opinion or dismissing it as illusion. Its account of externalization, objectivation, and internalization helped connect phenomenology, symbolic interactionism, sociology of knowledge, and institutional theory. Later research on social constructionism, identity formation, professions, deviance, gender, nationalism, science studies, organizational culture, and discourse all drew on this framework, even when later scholars revised or criticized its broad claims.

A bibliographic caveat: the canonical work is Berger and Luckmann’s 1966 book *The Social Construction of Reality: A Treatise in the Sociology of Knowledge*, not a 1967 *Modern Language Journal* paper by Daniel M. Rose. The impact described here refers to that historically influential Berger-Luckmann text.
