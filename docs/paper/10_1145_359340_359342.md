# A method for obtaining digital signatures and public-key cryptosystems

## Why this mattered

Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman’s paper turned public-key cryptography from a conceptual possibility into a concrete, implementable construction. Diffie and Hellman had introduced the public-key idea in 1976, but RSA showed how to realize both public-key encryption and digital signatures with one mathematically simple mechanism: modular exponentiation modulo a product of two large primes. The key paradigm shift was that secure communication no longer required a prior shared secret. A recipient could publish an encryption key openly, while keeping the corresponding decryption key private, making cryptography scalable for open networks rather than limited to parties that had already exchanged secret keys through trusted channels.

Equally important, RSA made digital signatures operational. By using the private exponent to produce a value that anyone could verify with the public exponent, the paper gave a practical method for authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation in electronic settings. This changed the role of cryptography from merely hiding messages to enabling trust between strangers: a signed message, transaction, certificate, or software update could be checked without a private communication channel to the signer. The paper’s own references to electronic mail and electronic funds transfer anticipated much of the infrastructure later required for internet commerce, secure software distribution, and identity systems.

The work also redirected cryptography toward computational hardness as an engineering foundation. RSA’s security rested in part on the apparent difficulty of factoring the public modulus, linking practical cryptographic design to number theory and complexity assumptions. Later systems refined, supplemented, or replaced aspects of RSA with elliptic-curve methods, modern signature schemes, authenticated key exchange, and post-quantum candidates, but they largely inherited the public-key worldview that RSA made usable: public identifiers, private secrets, verifiable signatures, and security based on problems believed to be infeasible at scale.

## Abstract

An encryption method is presented with the novel property that publicly revealing an encryption key does not thereby reveal the corresponding decryption key. This has two important consequences: (1) Couriers or other secure means are not needed to transmit keys, since a message can be enciphered using an encryption key publicly revealed by the intented recipient. Only he can decipher the message, since only he knows the corresponding decryption key. (2) A message can be “signed” using a privately held decryption key. Anyone can verify this signature using the corresponding publicly revealed encryption key. Signatures cannot be forged, and a signer cannot later deny the validity of his signature. This has obvious applications in “electronic mail” and “electronic funds transfer” systems. A message is encrypted by representing it as a number M, raising M to a publicly specified power e, and then taking the remainder when the result is divided by the publicly specified product, n , of two large secret primer numbers p and q. Decryption is similar; only a different, secret, power d is used, where e * d ≡ 1(mod (p - 1) * (q - 1)). The security of the system rests in part on the difficulty of factoring the published divisor, n .
