# MAXIMUM LIKELIHOOD ESTIMATION AND INFERENCE ON COINTEGRATION — WITH APPLICATIONS TO THE DEMAND FOR MONEY

## Why this mattered

Johansen and Jusélius made cointegration a workable multivariate inference problem rather than a mostly equation-by-equation diagnostic. Earlier cointegration work had shown why non-stationary economic series could share stable long-run relations, but this paper put that idea into a full-system Gaussian VAR likelihood framework: the number of cointegrating relations became a rank restriction, the cointegrating vectors and adjustment coefficients became estimable parameters, and hypotheses about them could be tested directly. That changed empirical macroeconometrics because it let researchers model several jointly determined non-stationary variables without either differencing away long-run information or relying on ad hoc single-equation residual tests.

What became newly possible was disciplined inference about long-run economic structure inside dynamic systems. The paper’s treatment of constants, seasonal dummies, deterministic trends, rank tests, and linear restrictions made the method usable on real macroeconomic data, not just in abstract asymptotic theory. Its money-demand applications mattered for the same reason: they showed that economically meaningful long-run relations could be estimated and tested while allowing short-run adjustment dynamics to remain multivariate. In practice, this helped establish the vector error-correction model as a standard empirical framework for monetary economics, exchange rates, term-structure models, purchasing-power parity, consumption-income relations, and many other areas where persistent series move together.

Its broader influence was to turn cointegration from a warning about spurious regression into a constructive modeling program. Later breakthroughs in structural VARs with long-run restrictions, common-trend analysis, permanent-transitory decompositions, weak exogeneity testing, and modern macro-finance all drew on the same idea: non-stationarity need not be a nuisance if the long-run rank structure is modeled explicitly. The paper did not invent cointegration, but it supplied the inferential machinery that made multivariate cointegration central to applied time-series econometrics.

## Abstract

This paper gives a systematic application of maximum likelihood inference concerning cointegration vectors in non-stationary vector valued autoregressive time series models with Gaussian errors, where the model includes a constant term and seasonal dummies. The hypothesis of cointegration is given a simple parametric form in terms of cointegration vectors and their weights. The relation between the constant term and a linear trend in the non-stationary part of the process is discussed and related to the weights. Tests for the presence of cointegration vectors, both with and without a linear trend in the non-stationary part of the process are derived. Then estimates and tests under linear restrictions on the cointegration vectors and their weights are given. The methods are illustrated by data from the Danish and the Finnish economy on the demand for money. Copyright 1990 by Blackwell Publishing Ltd
