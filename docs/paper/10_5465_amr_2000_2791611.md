# The Promise of Entrepreneurship as a Field of Research

## Why this mattered

Shane and Venkataraman’s paper mattered because it gave entrepreneurship research a distinct object of inquiry: the nexus between enterprising individuals and profitable opportunities. Rather than treating entrepreneurship as a personality type, a small-business setting, or a special case of strategic management, the paper argued that the field should explain how opportunities to create future goods and services are discovered, evaluated, and exploited, by whom, and with what effects. That move supplied a conceptual boundary for a fragmented literature and made entrepreneurship research less dependent on borrowing its core questions from economics, psychology, or management.

The paradigm shift was methodological as well as conceptual. Once “opportunity” became central, scholars could study entrepreneurship before firms existed, before performance could be measured, and before founders could be sorted retrospectively into successes and failures. This opened space for research on opportunity discovery and creation, entrepreneurial cognition, information asymmetry, prior knowledge, alertness, resource mobilization, and institutional context. It also clarified why entrepreneurship was not reducible to firm formation: the relevant phenomenon was the process by which new means-ends relationships are recognized or enacted and then pursued under uncertainty.

Subsequent breakthroughs in entrepreneurship theory often developed by extending or contesting this framework. The opportunity-discovery tradition, effectuation, bricolage, entrepreneurial ecosystems, institutional entrepreneurship, and work on opportunity creation all inherited the paper’s central problem even when they rejected parts of its answer. Its lasting contribution was to make entrepreneurship a theory-building domain organized around how novelty enters economic and organizational life, giving later researchers a common vocabulary for cumulative debate rather than a loose collection of founder stories, small-firm studies, and economic residuals.

## Abstract

To date, the phenomenon of entrepreneurship has lacked a conceptual framework. In this note we draw upon previous research conducted in the different social science disciplines and applied fields of business to create a conceptual framework for the field. With this framework we explain a set of empirical phenomena and predict a set of outcomes not explained or predicted by conceptual frameworks already in existence in other fields.
