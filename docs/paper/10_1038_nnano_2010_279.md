# Single-layer MoS2 transistors

## Why this mattered

Radisavljevic and colleagues changed the status of monolayer MoS2 from an interesting two-dimensional crystal into a credible electronic material. In the graphene-centered context of the late 2000s, atomically thin channels were attractive for scaling, but graphene’s lack of an intrinsic band gap made it difficult to turn transistors fully off. This paper showed that a single layer of MoS2, only a few atoms thick but semiconducting, could operate at room temperature with a current on/off ratio around \(10^8\), low standby power, and mobility reported at least \(200\ \mathrm{cm^2 V^{-1} s^{-1}}\) using a high-\(\kappa\) HfO2 gate dielectric. The key conceptual move was not merely making a thin transistor; it was demonstrating that the “ultimate thin body” could also be a practical switch.

After this result, two-dimensional electronics no longer meant choosing between graphene’s exceptional transport and conventional semiconductors’ switchability. MoS2 provided a working template for the broader transition-metal dichalcogenide field: atomically thin semiconductors with sizable band gaps, strong electrostatic gate control, and compatibility with flexible or transparent device concepts. Subsequent work rapidly pursued MoS2 logic circuits, contact engineering, dielectric engineering, wafer-scale growth, and related monolayer semiconductors such as WS2 and WSe2. Many later devices exposed limitations already implicit in the platform, especially contacts, disorder, and the gap between exfoliated demonstrations and scalable manufacturing, but the 2011 paper established the benchmark that made those problems worth solving.
