# Ferroptosis: An Iron-Dependent Form of Nonapoptotic Cell Death

## Why this mattered

Dixon and colleagues made ferroptosis legible as a distinct biological process rather than an odd failure mode of apoptosis assays. The paper showed that erastin and related lethal compounds killed cells through a pathway that did not match canonical apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy: it depended on iron, involved oxidative lipid damage, and could be blocked by iron chelators and by the newly identified inhibitor ferrostatin-1. That combination of morphology, genetics, and chemical rescue gave the field a new category of regulated cell death, with a name and experimental handles.

The paradigm shift was that cell death could now be studied through iron metabolism and membrane lipid peroxidation as primary causal machinery, not merely as downstream damage. After this paper, researchers could ask whether disease phenotypes reflected ferroptosis, induce or suppress the process with probes, and connect it to specific metabolic nodes such as cystine import, glutathione depletion, and later GPX4-dependent detoxification of lipid peroxides. This changed both cancer biology and degenerative-disease research: apoptosis resistance was no longer the only major framework for thinking about selectively killing tumor cells, and tissue injury could be reinterpreted through a regulated oxidative death pathway.

Subsequent breakthroughs built directly on this scaffold. Work in the following years established GPX4 as a central ferroptosis suppressor, connected ferroptosis sensitivity to lipid remodeling enzymes and antioxidant systems, and identified parallel protective axes such as FSP1-CoQ10. The 2012 paper therefore mattered less as a single mechanistic endpoint than as the founding map for an entire research program: it turned scattered observations about iron, reactive oxygen species, and nonapoptotic death into a coherent pathway that could be measured, perturbed, and pursued therapeutically.
