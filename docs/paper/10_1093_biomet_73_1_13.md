# Longitudinal data analysis using generalized linear models

## Why this mattered

Liang and Zeger’s paper made longitudinal and clustered response data usable within the generalized linear model framework without requiring a full multivariate probability model for each subject’s repeated observations. Before this, analysts often had to choose between methods that handled correlation only in special Gaussian settings and likelihood-based models that demanded strong assumptions about the joint distribution of binary, count, or other non-normal repeated outcomes. The key shift was the generalized estimating equation: specify the marginal mean model of scientific interest, use a “working” correlation structure to improve efficiency, and still obtain consistent regression estimates and robust variance estimates even when that correlation structure is wrong, under regularity conditions.

This changed what could be estimated routinely. Researchers could now ask population-average questions about longitudinal binary disease outcomes, repeated counts, clustered survey responses, and correlated clinical measurements using a common regression language. The paper separated modeling the mean from fully modeling within-subject dependence, making longitudinal analysis less hostage to unverifiable distributional assumptions. Its asymptotic sandwich variance also helped normalize a style of inference in which robustness to misspecified nuisance structure was not a fallback but a central design principle.

The influence was broad because the method sat at the junction of generalized linear models, quasi-likelihood, and modern semiparametric inference. GEE became a standard tool in biostatistics, epidemiology, clinical trials, and social science, especially where the target was a marginal or population-level effect rather than a subject-specific random effect. Later developments in clustered-data methods, robust covariance estimation, marginal structural models, and correlated-outcome software all drew on the same conceptual move: make the primary estimand explicit, treat dependence as partly nuisance structure, and preserve valid inference without pretending the full joint distribution is known.

## Abstract

This paper proposes an extension of generalized linear models to the analysis of longitudinal data. We introduce a class of estimating equations that give consistent estimates of the regression parameters and of their variance under mild assumptions about the time dependence. The estimating equations are derived without specifying the joint distribution of a subject's observations yet they reduce to the score equations for multivariate Gaussian outcomes. Asymptotic theory is presented for the general class of estimators. Specific cases in which we assume independence, m-dependence and exchangeable correlation structures from each subject are discussed. Efficiency of the proposed estimators in two simple situations is considered. The approach is closely related to quasi-likelihood.
