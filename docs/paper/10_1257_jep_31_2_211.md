# Social Media and Fake News in the 2016 Election

## Why this mattered

Allcott and Gentzkow’s paper mattered because it turned the post-2016 “fake news” debate from a largely anecdotal political panic into an empirical research program. Its central move was to measure exposure, sharing, belief, and partisan asymmetry using web-browsing data, fact-checking archives, and survey evidence, while also framing fake news as an economic phenomenon: producers respond to attention and advertising incentives, platforms mediate distribution, and consumers process information through partisan priors.

The paradigm shift was not the claim that misinformation existed, but that its role in democratic politics could be quantified with the tools of applied economics and media measurement. The paper showed that social media was important but not the dominant news source, that pro-Trump false stories circulated far more widely than pro-Clinton ones on Facebook in the observed period, and that belief was strongly conditioned by whether a story favored one’s preferred candidate. This combination made it possible to ask sharper causal and policy questions: not simply “did fake news matter?” but how much exposure occurred, who believed it, through what channels, and under what incentive structures.

Subsequent work on misinformation, platform governance, algorithmic amplification, fact-checking, media literacy, and election integrity built on this measurement agenda. The paper helped define the baseline against which later studies of social-media interventions, content moderation, ideological segregation, and the diffusion of misinformation could be compared. Its lasting influence comes from making fake news a tractable object of empirical study rather than only a rhetorical label for a political crisis.

## Abstract

Following the 2016 US presidential election, many have expressed concern about the effects of false stories (“fake news”), circulated largely through social media. We discuss the economics of fake news and present new data on its consumption prior to the election. Drawing on web browsing data, archives of fact-checking websites, and results from a new online survey, we find: 1) social media was an important but not dominant source of election news, with 14 percent of Americans calling social media their “most important” source; 2) of the known false news stories that appeared in the three months before the election, those favoring Trump were shared a total of 30 million times on Facebook, while those favoring Clinton were shared 8 million times; 3) the average American adult saw on the order of one or perhaps several fake news stories in the months around the election, with just over half of those who recalled seeing them believing them; and 4) people are much more likely to believe stories that favor their preferred candidate, especially if they have ideologically segregated social media networks.
