# The Mechanism of Nuclear Fission

## Why this mattered

Bohr and Wheeler turned fission from a surprising experimental fact into a quantitative nuclear-mechanical process. Using the liquid-drop model, they showed how a heavy nucleus could deform, pass a critical barrier, and divide, with the threshold depending systematically on nuclear charge, mass, and excitation energy. This reframed the nucleus not merely as a collection of particles but as a collective dynamical object whose stability could be calculated. In doing so, the paper gave physicists a common theoretical language for interpreting why some nuclei fissioned readily while others required higher-energy projectiles.

The paper mattered especially because it connected mechanism to observables. By relating fission barriers and cross sections to nuclear properties, Bohr and Wheeler made it possible to predict which isotopes and projectile energies would be most favorable for fission. That was crucial in 1939: it helped clarify why slow neutrons were effective for uranium-235, why uranium-238 behaved differently, and why isotope identity mattered for chain reactions. The work therefore helped transform fission from an isolated discovery by Hahn, Strassmann, Meitner, and Frisch into a calculable foundation for reactor physics and nuclear-energy research.

Its later influence lay in the bridge it built between nuclear structure theory and applied nuclear technology. The concepts of deformation, fission barriers, and excitation-dependent probabilities became central to subsequent models of heavy nuclei, neutron-induced reactions, reactor design, and nuclear weapons physics. While later theories added shell effects, microscopic corrections, and more detailed reaction dynamics, Bohr and Wheeler’s paper established the paradigm: fission is a collective instability of a charged quantum liquid drop, governed by calculable thresholds rather than by chemical analogy or empirical surprise alone.

## Abstract

On the basis of the liquid drop model of atomic nuclei, an account is given of the mechanism of nuclear fission. In particular, conclusions are drawn regarding the variation from nucleus to nucleus of the critical energy required for fission, and regarding the dependence of fission cross section for a given nucleus on energy of the exciting agency. A detailed discussion of the observations is presented on the basis of the theoretical considerations. Theory and experiment fit together in a reasonable way to give a satisfactory picture of nuclear fission.
