# Inhomogeneous Electron Gas

## Why this mattered

Hohenberg and Kohn’s paper changed the basic object of many-electron theory. Instead of treating the many-electron wavefunction as the fundamental variable, with its prohibitive dependence on all particle coordinates, it proved that the ground-state density alone determines the external potential, and therefore the full ground-state energy and properties of the interacting system. The second theorem gave this statement a variational form: a universal density functional exists whose minimization yields the exact ground-state energy. This was not merely a new approximation scheme; it was an existence proof that the density could, in principle, replace the wavefunction as the central variable for ground-state electronic structure.

What became newly possible was a rigorous foundation for calculating the properties of inhomogeneous interacting electron systems, including atoms, molecules, surfaces, and solids, using a three-dimensional density rather than an exponentially complicated wavefunction. The paper did not supply the exact functional, but it made clear why approximate functionals could be meaningful and how they could be tied to the uniform electron gas, response functions, and generalized Thomas-Fermi ideas. In doing so, it turned earlier density-based methods from useful heuristics into part of a formally justified framework, while also identifying their limitations.

Its immediate sequel was the Kohn-Sham formulation of 1965, which converted the Hohenberg-Kohn variational principle into a practical computational scheme using noninteracting orbitals plus exchange-correlation approximations. Together, these papers founded density functional theory. Subsequent local-density, generalized-gradient, hybrid, and more modern functionals all inherit the central premise established here: that much of the physics and chemistry of interacting electrons can be organized around the electron density. This made first-principles calculations broadly feasible across condensed-matter physics, quantum chemistry, and materials science, and it remains the conceptual starting point for much of computational electronic-structure theory.

## Abstract

This paper deals with the ground state of an interacting electron gas in an external potential $v(\mathrm{r})$. It is proved that there exists a universal functional of the density, $F[n(\mathrm{r})]$, independent of $v(\mathrm{r})$, such that the expression $E\ensuremath{\equiv}\ensuremath{\int}v(\mathrm{r})n(\mathrm{r})d\mathrm{r}+F[n(\mathrm{r})]$ has as its minimum value the correct ground-state energy associated with $v(\mathrm{r})$. The functional $F[n(\mathrm{r})]$ is then discussed for two situations: (1) $n(\mathrm{r})={n}_{0}+\stackrel{\ifmmode \tilde{}\else \~{}\fi{}}{n}(\mathrm{r})$, $\frac{\stackrel{\ifmmode \tilde{}\else \~{}\fi{}}{n}}{{n}_{0}}\ensuremath{\ll}1$, and (2) $n(\mathrm{r})=\ensuremath{\phi}(\frac{\mathrm{r}}{{r}_{0}})$ with $\ensuremath{\phi}$ arbitrary and ${r}_{0}\ensuremath{\rightarrow}\ensuremath{\infty}$. In both cases $F$ can be expressed entirely in terms of the correlation energy and linear and higher order electronic polarizabilities of a uniform electron gas. This approach also sheds some light on generalized Thomas-Fermi methods and their limitations. Some new extensions of these methods are presented.
