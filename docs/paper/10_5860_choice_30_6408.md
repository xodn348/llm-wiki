# Making democracy work: civic traditions in modern Italy

## Why this mattered

*Making Democracy Work* shifted comparative politics by making “social capital” a central explanatory variable for institutional performance. Putnam, Leonardi, and Nanetti used the unusual natural experiment of Italy’s regional governments, created under the same national framework in the 1970s but operating in regions with sharply different civic histories, to argue that formal institutions alone did not explain democratic effectiveness. Regions with dense traditions of civic association, horizontal trust, and reciprocal cooperation performed better than regions marked by hierarchy, patron-client relations, and weak associational life.

What became newly possible after the work was a systematic way to connect long-run social structures to modern governance outcomes without reducing politics only to constitutions, parties, or economic development. The book helped move “civic traditions” from background description into a measurable research agenda: institutional performance, trust, associational density, and historical path dependence could be compared across regions and later across countries. Its claims also made social capital a portable concept for political science, sociology, economics, and development studies, even as later work debated whether Putnam’s causal story overstated historical continuity or underplayed state capacity and inequality.

The book’s influence is visible in later breakthroughs on social capital, collective action, and institutional economics, including research on trust, networks, corruption, public goods provision, and the persistence of historical institutions. It helped frame a generation of work asking why democratic institutions with similar formal designs produce very different outcomes. Its lasting importance lies less in settling that question than in changing what counted as an answer: after *Making Democracy Work*, democracy’s performance could not be studied only through laws and elections, but also through the civic habits and social relations that make cooperation politically durable.
