# The next generation of scenarios for climate change research and assessment

## Why this mattered

Moss and colleagues changed climate-scenario research from a mostly sequential exercise into a coordinated, parallel framework. Earlier assessment cycles often moved from socioeconomic storylines to emissions scenarios to climate-model runs to impact studies in a slow chain. This paper formalized a new architecture built around Representative Concentration Pathways, defined by radiative forcing trajectories rather than single socioeconomic futures. That made it possible for integrated assessment modelers, Earth-system modelers, and impacts, adaptation, and vulnerability researchers to work simultaneously while still using mutually intelligible assumptions.

The shift mattered because it separated two questions that had often been bundled together: what level of climate forcing might occur, and what social, technological, economic, or policy pathway could lead there. After this paper, researchers could compare climate outcomes under common forcing pathways while later pairing them with alternative socioeconomic narratives. This enabled a much larger, more modular research enterprise, including the RCP-based experiments used in CMIP5 and IPCC AR5, and the later coupling of RCPs with the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways.

Its legacy was not a single prediction but an infrastructure for comparison. The paper helped turn scenarios into shared boundary objects across disciplines: climate models could produce standardized physical projections, while impact and policy studies could test vulnerability, adaptation, and mitigation under consistent futures. Subsequent breakthroughs in detection, attribution, risk assessment, carbon-budget analysis, and regional impacts depended heavily on that common scenario language, even as later work refined and criticized particular pathways.
