# Integrative analysis of 111 reference human epigenomes

## Why this mattered

Before this work, the human genome had a reference sequence, but regulatory genomics lacked an equivalent reference atlas for interpreting how the same DNA sequence is used differently across cell types. Kundaje and colleagues helped shift the field from treating regulatory annotations as scattered, experiment-specific tracks to treating epigenomic state as a systematic, comparable layer of genome annotation. By integrating histone marks, chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, and expression across 111 primary cells and tissues, the study made enhancers, promoters, repressed regions, and tissue-specific regulatory programs legible at human-genome scale.

The paper mattered especially because it connected noncoding genetic variation to cellular context. Genome-wide association studies had already shown that many disease- and trait-associated variants fall outside protein-coding genes; this work provided a principled way to ask *which cell types and regulatory elements* those variants might act through. Its enrichment analyses helped establish epigenomic maps as a standard interpretive framework for complex-trait genetics, moving disease genomics beyond nearest-gene annotation toward cell-type-specific regulatory mechanisms.

Subsequent breakthroughs in single-cell epigenomics, enhancer-gene linking, fine-mapping, and functional variant prioritization built on this paradigm: disease risk could be read not only against the linear genome, but against maps of regulatory activity in relevant tissues and developmental states. Later atlases became larger, more cellularly resolved, and more dynamic, but the conceptual template was already clear here: reference epigenomes could serve as an infrastructure for understanding gene regulation, differentiation, and the molecular basis of human disease.

## Abstract

The reference human genome sequence set the stage for studies of genetic variation and its association with human disease, but epigenomic studies lack a similar reference. To address this need, the NIH Roadmap Epigenomics Consortium generated the largest collection so far of human epigenomes for primary cells and tissues. Here we describe the integrative analysis of 111 reference human epigenomes generated as part of the programme, profiled for histone modification patterns, DNA accessibility, DNA methylation and RNA expression. We establish global maps of regulatory elements, define regulatory modules of coordinated activity, and their likely activators and repressors. We show that disease- and trait-associated genetic variants are enriched in tissue-specific epigenomic marks, revealing biologically relevant cell types for diverse human traits, and providing a resource for interpreting the molecular basis of human disease. Our results demonstrate the central role of epigenomic information for understanding gene regulation, cellular differentiation and human disease.
