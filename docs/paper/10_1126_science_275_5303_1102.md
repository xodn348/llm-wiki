# Probing Single Molecules and Single Nanoparticles by Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering

## Why this mattered

Nie and Emory’s 1997 paper helped shift surface-enhanced Raman scattering from an ensemble-averaged curiosity into a single-entity spectroscopy tool. Earlier SERS measurements showed very large Raman gains near rough metal surfaces, but the signals were usually interpreted over many molecules and many heterogeneous enhancement sites. This paper demonstrated that selected individual silver nanoparticles could generate enhancement factors of roughly \(10^{14}\) to \(10^{15}\), high enough to detect Raman spectra from single rhodamine 6G molecules at room temperature. That changed the practical meaning of Raman spectroscopy: a method long valued for chemically specific vibrational fingerprints could now, under the right nanoscale conditions, operate at the single-molecule limit.

The key paradigm shift was not only sensitivity, but selection and localization. By screening individual colloidal nanoparticles from a heterogeneous population, Nie and Emory showed that the strongest SERS activity came from rare nanoscale structures rather than from a uniform bulk property of the colloid. This helped redirect the field toward “hot spots,” plasmonic nanostructure engineering, and single-particle characterization, linking molecular spectroscopy to nanoscale electromagnetic confinement. After this work, it became newly plausible to ask chemically specific questions about individual adsorbed molecules, local surface environments, nanoparticle-to-nanoparticle variability, and transient binding events rather than relying solely on averaged spectra.

Its influence is visible in later developments across plasmonics, analytical chemistry, and bio-nanotechnology. The paper helped motivate controlled SERS substrates, nanoparticle dimers and junctions, tip-enhanced Raman spectroscopy, SERS tags for biological imaging, and ultrasensitive chemical detection schemes. It also clarified why reproducibility was difficult: the most dramatic enhancements depended on rare geometries and local fields. In that sense, the work was foundational both technically and conceptually, because it made single-molecule vibrational spectroscopy credible while forcing the field to treat nanoscale structure as the central variable rather than background disorder.

## Abstract

Optical detection and spectroscopy of single molecules and single nanoparticles have been achieved at room temperature with the use of surface-enhanced Raman scattering. Individual silver colloidal nanoparticles were screened from a large heterogeneous population for special size-dependent properties and were then used to amplify the spectroscopic signatures of adsorbed molecules. For single rhodamine 6G molecules adsorbed on the selected nanoparticles, the intrinsic Raman enhancement factors were on the order of 10(14) to 10(15), much larger than the ensemble-averaged values derived from conventional measurements. This enormous enhancement leads to vibrational Raman signals that are more intense and more stable than single-molecule fluorescence.
