# Those Who Understand: Knowledge Growth in Teaching

## Why this mattered

Shulman’s 1986 paper mattered because it changed the unit of analysis in teacher knowledge. Before it, research and policy often treated teaching expertise as either general pedagogical skill or subject-matter competence. Shulman argued that this split missed the distinctive knowledge teachers use when they transform disciplinary understanding into forms learners can grasp. His formulation of **pedagogical content knowledge** made teacher expertise visible as a specialized intellectual domain: knowledge of representations, explanations, examples, misconceptions, curricular sequencing, and the difficulties students encounter in learning particular subject matter.

That shift made new research programs possible. It gave scholars a vocabulary for studying teacher cognition within specific disciplines rather than treating teaching as generic technique, and it helped legitimize professional knowledge in teaching as something that could be developed, assessed, and debated. In mathematics education, science education, and later technology-integration research, Shulman’s categories became a foundation for models of mathematical knowledge for teaching, subject-specific didactics, and frameworks such as technological pedagogical content knowledge. These later developments extended his core insight: effective teaching depends not only on knowing content or knowing learners, but on knowing how particular content becomes teachable and learnable.

The paper also mattered institutionally. By framing teaching as knowledge-intensive professional work, Shulman helped redirect debates about teacher preparation, certification, and assessment away from simple measures of subject coverage or classroom management. Subsequent breakthroughs in teacher education built on this premise by examining how teachers learn to anticipate student thinking, choose productive representations, and connect disciplinary ideas to classroom practice. Its long influence comes from making a previously under-theorized form of expertise central to educational research.

## Abstract

Esta investigación tiene como propósito analizar algunos referentes teóricos sobre la Filosofía de las Matemáticas (FM) y el conocimiento del profesor de Matemáticas (CPM), con el fin de presentar evidencias de la articulación e integración de la FM en los modelos del CPM en función del quehacer docente, particularmente el sugerido por Stacey (2008). Por lo anterior, se sistematizan y describen los planteamientos de algunas fuentes bibliográficas referentes a la FM y a los modelos del CPM con la intención de aportar información sobre la relación que hay entre la Didáctica de las Matemáticas (DM), la FM, y el CPM.
