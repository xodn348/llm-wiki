# A Simplex Method for Function Minimization

## Why this mattered

Nelder and Mead’s paper made derivative-free optimization practical for a broad class of scientific and statistical problems. Its key shift was to treat minimization as an adaptive geometric search over function values alone: a simplex of \(n+1\) points moves, reflects, expands, and contracts according to sampled objective values, without requiring gradients, smoothness assumptions, or elaborate algebraic machinery. That mattered because many real objective functions in simulation, experimental fitting, calibration, and likelihood-based estimation were expensive, noisy, or analytically opaque. After this paper, researchers had a compact, implementable method for improving such functions even when calculus-based optimization was unavailable or inconvenient.

The method’s influence came partly from its portability. It was simple enough to code on 1960s computers, yet general enough to become a default local optimizer across statistics, engineering design, chemistry, physics, and later machine learning workflows. The paper also connected optimization directly to statistical estimation: its discussion of estimating the Hessian near the minimum reflected the needs of maximum-likelihood and nonlinear least-squares practice, where locating an optimum was only part of the inferential problem. In this sense, Nelder-Mead helped normalize the idea that numerical optimization could be a routine scientific instrument rather than a specialized mathematical project.

Its later importance is also visible in what it made possible and what it exposed. Nelder-Mead became a baseline against which more formal derivative-free methods, trust-region approaches, pattern searches, evolutionary strategies, Bayesian optimization, and modern black-box optimization methods could be compared. Subsequent theory showed that the algorithm’s behavior is not guaranteed in all settings, especially in higher dimensions or pathological landscapes, but that did not diminish its practical role. The paper shifted expectations: when a model could be evaluated but not differentiated, one could still optimize it effectively enough to make computation part of scientific discovery.

## Abstract

A method is described for the minimization of a function of n variables, which depends on the comparison of function values at the (n + 1) vertices of a general simplex, followed by the replacement of the vertex with the highest value by another point. The simplex adapts itself to the local landscape, and contracts on to the final minimum. The method is shown to be effective and computationally compact. A procedure is given for the estimation of the Hessian matrix in the neighbourhood of the minimum, needed in statistical estimation problems.
