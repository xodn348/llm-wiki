# Self-efficacy mechanism in human agency.

## Why this mattered

Bandura’s [1982 article](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361981469484860032?lang=en) mattered because it made perceived self-efficacy a central causal mechanism linking knowledge, motivation, emotion, and action. Rather than treating behavior mainly as the output of reinforcement histories, skills, traits, or information, Bandura argued that people’s judgments about what they can do shape whether they initiate action, how much effort they invest, how long they persist, and how strongly they react emotionally under threat or failure. The paradigm shift was subtle but large: agency became experimentally tractable through beliefs about capability, not merely inferred from outcomes.

The paper also made a broad research program newly possible. If efficacy beliefs could be induced, measured, and causally tested, then psychotherapy, health behavior, education, occupational choice, achievement, stress, and self-regulation could be studied with a common explanatory variable. Bandura’s review connected laboratory manipulations of efficacy to real-world domains such as coping behavior, physiological stress reactions, persistence after failure, intrinsic interest, career pursuits, and collective action, giving psychologists a portable construct that was specific enough to test yet general enough to organize diverse findings.

Its later influence came through social cognitive theory, especially Bandura’s 1986 synthesis and his subsequent work on self-efficacy, but also through applied breakthroughs in exposure-based treatment, health-promotion models, educational motivation research, organizational behavior, and studies of collective efficacy. The paper helped shift psychology toward models in which people are neither passive responders to environments nor unconstrained rational choosers, but self-reflective agents whose beliefs about capability partly determine what environments they enter, endure, alter, or avoid.

## Abstract

This article addresses the centrality of the self-efficacy mechanism in human agency. Self-per- cepts of efficacy influence thought patterns, actions, and emotional arousal. In causal tests the higher the level of induced self-efficacy, the higher the perfor- mance accomplishments and the lower the emotional arousal. Different lines of research are reviewed, show- ing that the self-efficacy mechanism may have wide explanatory power. Perceived self-efficacy helps to ac- count for such diverse phenomena as changes in coping behavior produced by different modes of influence, level of physiological stress reactions, self-regulation of refractory behavior, resignation and despondency to failure experiences, self-debilitating effects of proxy control and illusory inefficaciousness, achievement strivings, growth of intrinsic interest, and career pur- suits. The influential role of perceived collective effi- cacy in social change is analyzed, as are the social con- ditions conducive to development of collective inefficacy. Psychological theorizing and research tend to cen- ter on issues concerning either acquisition of knowledge or execution of response patterns. As a result the processes governing the interrelation- ship between knowledge and action have been largely neglected (Newell, 1978). Some of the re- cent efforts to bridge this gap have been directed at the biomechanics problem—how efferent com- mands of action plans guide the production of ap- propriate response patterns (Stelmach, 1976,1978).
