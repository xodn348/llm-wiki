# Continuous cultures of fused cells secreting antibody of predefined specificity

## Why this mattered

Köhler and Milstein’s 1975 paper introduced the hybridoma method: fusing antibody-producing B cells with immortal myeloma cells to create stable cell lines that secreted antibodies of a single, predefined specificity. Before this, antisera were mixtures of antibodies from many B-cell clones, varying from animal to animal and batch to batch. The new technique made it possible to produce homogeneous monoclonal antibodies continuously, turning antibody specificity from a biological variable into a reproducible experimental reagent.

The shift was conceptual as much as technical. It showed that immune-cell specificity could be captured, cloned, and propagated indefinitely in culture. That gave immunology, cell biology, pathology, and biochemistry a precise molecular probe for detecting, isolating, and classifying proteins, cells, pathogens, and tumor markers. Monoclonal antibodies rapidly became standard tools for diagnostics, flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, affinity purification, and receptor mapping.

The paper also opened the route to antibody therapeutics. Early mouse monoclonals had limitations in humans, including immunogenicity, but the hybridoma principle established the production logic later extended by chimeric, humanized, fully human, bispecific, and antibody-drug conjugate technologies. Subsequent breakthroughs in cancer treatment, autoimmune disease therapy, infectious-disease diagnostics, and targeted biologics trace a direct lineage to the practical possibility this paper demonstrated: antibodies could be made as durable, selectable, industrially reproducible products rather than as transient immune-serum mixtures.
