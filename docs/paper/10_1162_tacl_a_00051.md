# Enriching Word Vectors with Subword Information

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, widely used word embedding models such as word2vec generally treated each word type as an atomic symbol: *dog*, *dogs*, and an unseen misspelling or inflected form were separate lookup-table entries, or absent entirely. Bojanowski et al. changed that assumption by making the word vector a composition of character n-gram vectors. This was a modest architectural change with large consequences: morphology, spelling regularities, prefixes, suffixes, and productive word formation became part of the representation rather than noise outside the vocabulary. It made distributional semantics work better for rare words and morphologically rich languages, and it gave the model a principled way to construct vectors for words never observed during training.

The paper mattered because it helped move NLP away from fixed word identities toward representations built from reusable subword units. Its fastText formulation retained the practical strengths of skip-gram training: speed, scalability, and usability on large unlabeled corpora, while removing one of the major weaknesses of word-level embeddings. That made high-quality embeddings more accessible for languages where data sparsity and inflection had made English-centric word-vector methods less effective, and it gave practitioners a simple, robust baseline that often worked well without task-specific supervision.

Historically, the paper sits between the word2vec/GloVe era and the later dominance of subword-tokenized contextual models. It did not introduce contextual meaning in the transformer sense, but it normalized the idea that lexical representations should be decomposable below the word level. Subsequent systems such as BPE- and WordPiece-based neural machine translation models, ELMo-style character-aware representations, and transformer language models with subword vocabularies pursued a related lesson at larger scale: open-vocabulary language processing depends on modeling internal word structure, not merely memorizing whole-word entries.

## Abstract

Continuous word representations, trained on large unlabeled corpora are useful for many natural language processing tasks. Popular models that learn such representations ignore the morphology of words, by assigning a distinct vector to each word. This is a limitation, especially for languages with large vocabularies and many rare words. In this paper, we propose a new approach based on the skipgram model, where each word is represented as a bag of character n-grams. A vector representation is associated to each character n-gram; words being represented as the sum of these representations. Our method is fast, allowing to train models on large corpora quickly and allows us to compute word representations for words that did not appear in the training data. We evaluate our word representations on nine different languages, both on word similarity and analogy tasks. By comparing to recently proposed morphological word representations, we show that our vectors achieve state-of-the-art performance on these tasks.
