# A metal-free polymeric photocatalyst for hydrogen production from water under visible light

## Why this mattered

Before this work, visible-light water-splitting photocatalysis was dominated by inorganic semiconductors, often metal oxides, oxynitrides, sulfides, or sensitized systems. Wang and colleagues showed that polymeric carbon nitride, an abundant carbon-nitrogen solid, could absorb visible light and drive hydrogen evolution from water in the presence of a sacrificial donor. The important shift was not that it solved overall solar water splitting, but that it made a metal-free, chemically robust, conjugated polymer credible as the light-harvesting photocatalyst for solar fuel chemistry.

That changed the design space. Photocatalysts no longer had to be treated only as crystalline inorganic band solids; they could be engineered like polymer semiconductors, with tunable composition, porosity, defects, heterojunctions, and molecular building blocks. The paper helped launch graphitic/polymeric carbon nitride as a central platform for visible-light photocatalysis, quickly followed by mesoporous carbon nitride, doped and exfoliated variants, carbon nitride heterostructures, and related organic frameworks for hydrogen evolution, CO₂ reduction, H₂O₂ production, and other artificial-photosynthesis reactions.

Its legacy is therefore best understood as a field-opening demonstration. The original activity was modest and relied on sacrificial chemistry, so it was not a practical hydrogen technology by itself. But it proved that inexpensive organic/polymeric semiconductors could perform the core photochemical role once reserved for inorganic materials, setting up much of the later work on metal-free and molecularly programmable photocatalysts.
