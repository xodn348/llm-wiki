# A flexible new technique for camera calibration

## Why this mattered

Zhang’s paper mattered because it turned camera calibration from a specialized laboratory procedure into a practical routine. Earlier accurate calibration methods often depended on carefully manufactured 3D calibration rigs, known camera motion, or restrictive setups. Zhang showed that a camera could instead be calibrated from several views of a flat printed pattern at unknown orientations, combining a closed-form initialization with nonlinear maximum-likelihood refinement and radial distortion estimation. That change made calibration cheap, repeatable, and accessible to ordinary labs, engineers, and eventually consumer-facing vision systems.

The paradigm shift was not just convenience; it changed the scale at which 3D computer vision could be deployed. Once reliable intrinsic calibration could be obtained with a planar target and a few images, methods for stereo reconstruction, structure from motion, augmented reality, robot perception, visual metrology, and multi-camera systems became much easier to initialize outside controlled environments. The now-familiar checkerboard calibration workflow in OpenCV and other vision toolkits descends directly from this idea: calibration became a standard preprocessing step rather than a bespoke experimental obstacle.

Its influence also lies in how it bridged projective geometry and practical engineering. By exploiting homographies induced by a plane, the paper connected elegant geometric constraints to a procedure robust enough for real cameras with lens distortion. Subsequent breakthroughs in SLAM, visual odometry, depth sensing, marker-based AR, and camera-equipped robotics all benefited from the broader normalization of accurate, low-cost calibration. Zhang’s method helped move 3D vision from calibrated rigs and specialist equipment toward ordinary cameras operating in ordinary environments.

## Abstract

We propose a flexible technique to easily calibrate a camera. It only requires the camera to observe a planar pattern shown at a few (at least two) different orientations. Either the camera or the planar pattern can be freely moved. The motion need not be known. Radial lens distortion is modeled. The proposed procedure consists of a closed-form solution, followed by a nonlinear refinement based on the maximum likelihood criterion. Both computer simulation and real data have been used to test the proposed technique and very good results have been obtained. Compared with classical techniques which use expensive equipment such as two or three orthogonal planes, the proposed technique is easy to use and flexible. It advances 3D computer vision one more step from laboratory environments to real world use.
