# Atoms, molecules, solids, and surfaces: Applications of the generalized gradient approximation for exchange and correlation

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it showed that density-functional theory could move beyond the local-spin-density approximation without losing its practical universality. LSD had made large-scale electronic-structure calculations possible, but it systematically overbound many systems and often gave lattice constants that were too small. Perdew and coauthors demonstrated that a generalized gradient approximation, using not only the local electron density but also its spatial variation, could improve results across an unusually wide range of targets: atoms, small molecules, alkali metals, magnetic iron, and metallic surfaces. The key shift was not a single numerical improvement, but evidence that one functional form could capture chemically and structurally important nonlocal exchange-correlation effects while remaining computationally tractable.

That changed what first-principles calculation could be trusted to do. The paper showed markedly better atomization energies for hydrocarbons than LSD and Hartree-Fock, corrected major LSD failures in Li and Na lattice constants, and restored the magnetic ground state of solid Fe. These were not niche benchmarks: they connected quantum electronic structure directly to chemistry, condensed-matter structure, magnetism, and surface physics. After results like these, DFT was no longer mainly a qualitative tool for simple solids; it became a practical predictive method for realistic materials and molecules.

Its larger legacy is that it helped establish GGA as the working level of theory for modern computational materials science and quantum chemistry. The Perdew-Wang GGA tested here was soon followed by more widely standardized forms, especially PBE, which inherited the same central program: build nonempirical, constraint-based exchange-correlation functionals that improve LSD while preserving broad transferability. Later breakthroughs in high-throughput materials discovery, surface catalysis modeling, defect physics, magnetism, and nanoscale simulation all depended on this middle ground: accurate enough to be useful across real systems, cheap enough to run repeatedly, and general enough to become infrastructure rather than a specialist method.

## Abstract

Generalized gradient approximations (GGA's) seek to improve upon the accuracy of the local-spin-density (LSD) approximation in electronic-structure calculations. Perdew and Wang have developed a GGA based on real-space cutoff of the spurious long-range components of the second-order gradient expansion for the exchange-correlation hole. We have found that this density functional performs well in numerical tests for a variety of systems: (1) Total energies of 30 atoms are highly accurate. (2) Ionization energies and electron affinities are improved in a statistical sense, although significant interconfigurational and interterm errors remain. (3) Accurate atomization energies are found for seven hydrocarbon molecules, with a rms error per bond of 0.1 eV, compared with 0.7 eV for the LSD approximation and 2.4 eV for the Hartree-Fock approximation. (4) For atoms and molecules, there is a cancellation of error between density functionals for exchange and correlation, which is most striking whenever the Hartree-Fock result is furthest from experiment. (5) The surprising LSD underestimation of the lattice constants of Li and Na by 3--4 % is corrected, and the magnetic ground state of solid Fe is restored. (6) The work function, surface energy (neglecting the long-range contribution), and curvature energy of a metallic surface are all slightly reduced in comparison with LSD. Taking account of the positive long-range contribution, we find surface and curvature energies in good agreement with experimental or exact values. Finally, a way is found to visualize and understand the nonlocality of exchange and correlation, its origins, and its physical effects.
