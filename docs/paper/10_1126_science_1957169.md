# Optical Coherence Tomography

## Why this mattered

Huang et al. mattered because it showed that optical interferometry could become a practical tomographic imaging modality for living tissues, not merely a precision measurement technique. By using low-coherence interferometry to localize backscattered light by depth, OCT translated the logic of ultrasound pulse-echo imaging into the optical domain, achieving micrometer-scale axial resolution without cutting, staining, or physically sectioning tissue. The 1991 demonstrations in retina and coronary artery were important because they spanned two clinically different regimes: the eye, where transparent media made high-resolution optical imaging especially promising, and vascular tissue, where scattering limited conventional optical inspection.

The paradigm shift was the creation of an “optical biopsy” concept: cross-sectional microstructure could be inspected noninvasively and in near-real time at resolutions between gross clinical imaging and histology. Before OCT, clinicians could photograph or inspect surfaces optically, and pathologists could examine excised tissue microscopically, but there was no widely applicable method for depth-resolved, micron-scale imaging inside intact biological structures. After this paper, it became newly plausible to monitor retinal layers, optic nerve head morphology, plaque structure, and other subsurface tissue features repeatedly in the same patient, turning anatomy that had been inferred indirectly into measurable image data.

Its largest downstream impact was in ophthalmology, where OCT became central to diagnosing and managing retinal disease, glaucoma, and macular pathology, and later enabled quantitative longitudinal tracking of disease and treatment response. The same principle also supported later breakthroughs such as spectral-domain and swept-source OCT, OCT angiography, and intravascular OCT. Those developments improved speed, sensitivity, depth range, and functional contrast, but they followed the conceptual path opened in 1991: coherent optical ranging could make microscopic tissue architecture visible in vivo, creating a new class of clinical imaging between microscopy and radiology.

## Abstract

A technique called optical coherence tomography (OCT) has been developed for noninvasive cross-sectional imaging in biological systems. OCT uses low-coherence interferometry to produce a two-dimensional image of optical scattering from internal tissue microstructures in a way that is analogous to ultrasonic pulse-echo imaging. OCT has longitudinal and lateral spatial resolutions of a few micrometers and can detect reflected signals as small as approximately 10(-10) of the incident optical power. Tomographic imaging is demonstrated in vitro in the peripapillary area of the retina and in the coronary artery, two clinically relevant examples that are representative of transparent and turbid media, respectively.
