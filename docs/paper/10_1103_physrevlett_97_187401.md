# Raman Spectrum of Graphene and Graphene Layers

## Why this mattered

Ferrari et al. made Raman spectroscopy into a practical “fingerprint” for graphene at the moment when the field most needed one. In 2006, mechanically exfoliated graphene had only recently become a credible experimental platform, but identifying whether a flake was a monolayer, bilayer, or few-layer sample was still slow and uncertain. This paper showed that the Raman spectrum, especially the shape, width, and position of the second-order D-related 2D peak, changed systematically with layer number because the double-resonant Raman process tracked the evolving electronic band structure. That connected a simple optical measurement to the defining physics of graphene rather than treating Raman peaks as merely empirical labels.

The result changed what laboratories could do. Graphene samples could now be identified rapidly, nondestructively, and at high throughput, without relying only on atomic force microscopy or device fabrication. This made Raman mapping a routine gatekeeping tool for graphene research: before measuring transport, optical absorption, strain, doping, disorder, or growth quality, researchers could first verify what material they actually had. The paper therefore helped turn graphene from a rare hand-selected specimen into a characterizable platform.

Its influence also extended beyond exfoliated flakes. As graphene research moved toward epitaxial growth, chemical vapor deposition, transfer processes, heterostructures, and wafer-scale materials, Raman spectroscopy became one of the central quality-control methods. Later breakthroughs in graphene electronics, optoelectronics, strain engineering, and two-dimensional material stacks all depended on reliable, nondestructive characterization of atomically thin layers. Ferrari et al. did not discover graphene, but they supplied one of the measurement standards that allowed the field to scale.

## Abstract

Graphene is the two-dimensional building block for carbon allotropes of every other dimensionality. We show that its electronic structure is captured in its Raman spectrum that clearly evolves with the number of layers. The D peak second order changes in shape, width, and position for an increasing number of layers, reflecting the change in the electron bands via a double resonant Raman process. The G peak slightly down-shifts. This allows unambiguous, high-throughput, nondestructive identification of graphene layers, which is critically lacking in this emerging research area.
