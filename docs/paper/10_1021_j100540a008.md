# Exact stochastic simulation of coupled chemical reactions

## Why this mattered

Gillespie’s 1977 paper mattered because it replaced an informal choice between deterministic rate equations and approximate stochastic treatments with an exact simulation procedure for well-stirred chemically reacting systems. The paper showed how to generate statistically correct sample paths from the chemical master equation by drawing both the next reaction time and the next reaction channel from the reaction propensities. This made molecular discreteness and intrinsic reaction noise computable, not just conceptually acknowledged, especially in regimes where copy numbers are small and concentration-based differential equations can give misleading behavior.

The paradigm shift was methodological: chemical kinetics could now be studied event by event, with randomness treated as part of the physical model rather than as numerical error or an external perturbation. After Gillespie’s algorithm, researchers could ask questions about fluctuation-driven switching, extinction, rare events, and noise-amplified dynamics in coupled reaction networks using a principled simulator. That capability became central in systems biology and biochemical network modeling, where gene regulation, signaling, and cellular reaction networks often operate with small numbers of molecules.

The paper also became a foundation for later breakthroughs in stochastic simulation itself. Many subsequent methods, including tau-leaping, hybrid deterministic-stochastic schemes, accelerated next-reaction methods, and spatial stochastic simulators, can be understood as attempts to preserve Gillespie’s exactness where necessary while reducing its computational cost or expanding its scope. Its influence is therefore not only that it introduced the stochastic simulation algorithm, but that it established the chemical master equation as a practical computational object for modern reaction-network science.
