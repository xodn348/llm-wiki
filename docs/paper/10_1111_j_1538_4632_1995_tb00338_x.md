# Local Indicators of Spatial Association—LISA

## Why this mattered

Anselin’s 1995 paper shifted spatial statistics from asking only whether a whole map exhibited spatial autocorrelation to asking where that association was concentrated. Global measures such as Moran’s I could summarize clustering across a study region, but they obscured local heterogeneity: a strong global statistic might be driven by a few influential places, while meaningful local clusters could disappear inside a weak aggregate result. LISA made it possible to decompose spatial association into observation-level contributions, turning spatial autocorrelation into something that could be mapped, inspected, and tested locally.

This mattered because it matched the emerging capabilities of GIS with a formal inferential framework for exploratory spatial data analysis. Analysts could now identify hot spots, cold spots, spatial outliers, and local pockets of nonstationarity rather than treating space as a single homogeneous process. The paper also clarified that local statistics were not just visualization aids: they connected local pattern detection to established global measures, especially Moran’s I, and made individual locations analytically accountable for the overall spatial structure.

The broader consequence was a new style of spatial analysis in which maps became diagnostic instruments rather than merely presentation devices. LISA influenced later work in geovisual analytics, spatial epidemiology, crime analysis, regional economics, environmental justice, and public-health surveillance, where the central question is often not whether clustering exists but where it occurs and which places drive it. Its logic also anticipated later emphasis on spatial heterogeneity, local modeling, and geographically weighted approaches: the recognition that relationships measured over space may vary sharply from place to place, and that those variations are often the phenomenon of interest.

## Abstract

The capabilities for visualization, rapid data retrieval, and manipulation in geographic information systems (GIS) have created the need for new techniques of exploratory data analysis that focus on the “spatial” aspects of the data. The identification of local patterns of spatial association is an important concern in this respect. In this paper, I outline a new general class of local indicators of spatial association (LISA) and show how they allow for the decomposition of global indicators, such as Moran's I, into the contribution of each observation. The LISA statistics serve two purposes. On one hand, they may be interpreted as indicators of local pockets of nonstationarity, or hot spots, similar to the G i and G* i statistics of Getis and Ord (1992). On the other hand, they may be used to assess the influence of individual locations on the magnitude of the global statistic and to identify “outliers,” as in Anselin's Moran scatterplot (1993a). An initial evaluation of the properties of a LISA statistic is carried out for the local Moran, which is applied in a study of the spatial pattern of conflict for African countries and in a number of Monte Carlo simulations.
