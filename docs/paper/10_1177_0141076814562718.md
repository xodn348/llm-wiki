# The environment and disease: association or causation?

## Why this mattered

Hill’s essay mattered because it gave epidemiology a disciplined way to reason about causation when randomized experiments were impossible, unethical, or too slow. Using the smoking–lung cancer debate as its central backdrop, Hill argued that observed associations should not be dismissed merely because they came from observational data. His now-famous considerations, including strength, consistency, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, and analogy, reframed causal inference as a structured judgment rather than a demand for a single decisive proof.

The paradigm shift was practical as much as philosophical: public-health action could proceed under uncertainty when multiple lines of evidence converged. After Hill, researchers and policymakers had a shared vocabulary for evaluating environmental, occupational, behavioral, and infectious causes of disease. This made it newly possible to justify interventions against hazards such as tobacco, asbestos, air pollution, and later many chronic-disease risk factors without waiting for impossible “perfect” experiments.

Its influence also shaped later breakthroughs in evidence-based medicine, environmental epidemiology, regulatory science, and modern causal inference. Although Hill’s considerations were not a formal algorithm and do not replace statistical or mechanistic analysis, they became a durable bridge between empirical observation and responsible action. The paper’s lasting importance lies in legitimizing a careful, pluralistic standard of causal reasoning for population health.
