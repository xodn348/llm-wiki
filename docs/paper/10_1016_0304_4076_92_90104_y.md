# Testing the null hypothesis of stationarity against the alternative of a unit root

## Why this mattered

Kwiatkowski, Phillips, Schmidt, and Shin changed empirical time-series practice by making stationarity itself a testable maintained hypothesis rather than a default assumption or a conclusion reached only by failing to reject a unit-root null. Before the paper, the dominant unit-root tests, including Dickey-Fuller and Phillips-Perron tests, treated nonstationarity as the null hypothesis. The KPSS test inverted that logic: its null was level or trend stationarity, and its alternative was a unit root. This mattered because “failure to reject a unit root” and “evidence for a unit root” are not the same thing, especially in finite samples with low power. The paper gave applied researchers a complementary diagnostic that could distinguish more carefully among stationary, trend-stationary, and difference-stationary specifications.

The shift was methodological as much as technical. After KPSS, standard empirical work could use joint evidence from stationarity-null and unit-root-null tests rather than relying on one asymmetrical testing framework. That changed how economists handled persistence in macroeconomic and financial series: whether shocks were transitory or permanent, whether detrending or differencing was appropriate, and whether regressions risked spurious inference. The test became part of the routine toolkit for pretesting time-series models, alongside ADF, Phillips-Perron, and later panel unit-root and stationarity procedures.

Its broader influence was to make the uncertainty around persistence explicit. Subsequent work on cointegration, long-run equilibria, structural breaks, near-unit-root behavior, and panel time-series testing built on the recognition that the null hypothesis matters. KPSS did not replace earlier unit-root tests; it made their interpretation more disciplined. That is why the paper became foundational: it reframed nonstationarity as a question requiring opposing inferential perspectives, not a binary verdict from a single test.
