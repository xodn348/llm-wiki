# Induction of Pluripotent Stem Cells from Adult Human Fibroblasts by Defined Factors

## Why this mattered

This paper showed that differentiated adult human cells could be returned to a pluripotent state by introducing a small defined set of transcription factors: OCT3/4, SOX2, KLF4, and c-MYC. That result shifted the stem-cell field away from the assumption that human pluripotency had to be obtained from embryos or through oocyte-based nuclear transfer. It made cellular identity seem less like a fixed endpoint and more like a regulatory state that could be experimentally rewritten.

The immediate importance was practical as well as conceptual. Patient-specific pluripotent stem cells became newly attainable from accessible adult tissue, opening routes to disease modeling, drug screening, and eventually autologous cell-therapy concepts without requiring embryo-derived stem-cell lines. The work also gave researchers a reproducible platform for studying human development and disease in cell types that were previously difficult or impossible to obtain directly from patients.

Its later influence was broad: induced pluripotent stem cells became foundational to organoid biology, genome-edited disease models, toxicity testing, regenerative-medicine pipelines, and studies of cellular reprogramming itself. The original method still carried major limitations, especially the use of integrating retroviral vectors and oncogenic c-MYC, but those constraints helped define the next wave of breakthroughs: non-integrating delivery, small-molecule and mRNA-based reprogramming, safer differentiation protocols, and increasingly precise patient-derived models.
