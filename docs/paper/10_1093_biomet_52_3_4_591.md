# An analysis of variance test for normality (complete samples)

## Why this mattered

Shapiro and Wilk’s 1965 paper mattered because it turned normality testing into a practical, powerful diagnostic for ordinary complete samples. Earlier tests for departures from normality existed, but many were either tailored to particular alternatives, less powerful in small samples, or awkward to apply in routine statistical work. The paper introduced what became the Shapiro-Wilk test: a statistic based on the ordered sample values and the expected order statistics of a normal distribution, framed through an analysis-of-variance interpretation. This made it possible to ask, with unusual sensitivity for the time, whether a modest-sized dataset plausibly came from a normal population.

The shift was important because normality was not a peripheral assumption. Much of classical inference, including *t*-tests, analysis of variance, regression diagnostics, and control-chart practice, depended on approximate normality or on understanding when that approximation failed. By giving analysts a compact and empirically strong test, Shapiro and Wilk helped move normality assessment from informal visual judgment toward reproducible diagnostic testing. The paper’s extensive percentage points and power comparisons also made the method usable before modern statistical software, which helped it diffuse into applied statistics.

Its later influence came from becoming a default benchmark rather than merely one more test. The Shapiro-Wilk test is still widely implemented in statistical software and is often treated as one of the most powerful general-purpose tests for normality, especially for small and moderate samples. That role shaped subsequent work on omnibus goodness-of-fit testing, residual diagnostics, robust methods, and model-checking workflows: once normality could be tested routinely, departures from it became easier to document, compare, and motivate as reasons for transformation, nonparametric methods, generalized models, or robust inference.
