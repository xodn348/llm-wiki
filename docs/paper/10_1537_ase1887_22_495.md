# The descent of man and selection in relation to sex

## Why this mattered

Darwin’s *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex* mattered because it extended evolutionary explanation to the most contentious case: humans themselves. Where *On the Origin of Species* had largely avoided direct treatment of human ancestry, this work argued that human bodies, minds, emotions, and social instincts could be studied as products of common descent and natural selection. It also made sexual selection a central evolutionary mechanism, explaining traits shaped not only by survival but by competition for mates and mate choice.

The 1907 Japanese publication is significant as part of the transmission of Darwinian thought into Japanese anthropology and biology. Appearing in *Tokyo Jinrui Gakkai zasshi*, a leading venue for anthropology in Japan, it helped place human origins, racial variation, and comparative anatomy within an evolutionary framework available to Japanese scholars. That shift made it possible to discuss humanity as a natural-historical subject rather than as an exception outside biology, though later anthropological uses of such ideas were often shaped by the racial and imperial assumptions of their time.

Subsequent breakthroughs in genetics, population biology, ethology, and evolutionary psychology transformed Darwin’s arguments, replacing many nineteenth-century mechanisms and assumptions while preserving the central claim that humans are part of the same evolutionary continuum as other animals. Modern work on sexual selection, parental investment, social behavior, and human evolution all descends in part from the conceptual opening this book created: that traits central to human identity could be investigated comparatively, historically, and biologically.
