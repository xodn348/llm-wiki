# The Radiation Theories of Tomonaga, Schwinger, and Feynman

## Why this mattered

Dyson’s paper mattered because it showed that two seemingly different postwar formulations of quantum electrodynamics were not rival theories but equivalent descriptions of the same physics. Tomonaga and Schwinger had developed a covariant operator-based approach, while Feynman had introduced a diagrammatic and spacetime-oriented method that made calculations look radically simpler. Dyson’s synthesis established that Feynman’s rules could be understood as a compact computational form of the more formal Tomonaga-Schwinger theory. This turned Feynman diagrams from an ingenious heuristic into a legitimate calculational language for relativistic quantum field theory.

The practical consequence was large: QED became a usable theory for systematic perturbative calculation. Problems involving radiative corrections, higher-order processes, and vacuum polarization could now be organized by diagrammatic rules rather than by unwieldy operator manipulations. Dyson was careful that the higher-order program was not a proof of convergence, but the paper made clear that the theory could be extended order by order in a controlled and reproducible way. That shift helped make precision comparisons between theory and experiment, such as the Lamb shift and anomalous magnetic moment calculations, central evidence for the power of renormalized QED.

Its longer-term importance was methodological. Dyson’s unification helped establish the template for modern quantum field theory: particles and fields described by covariant perturbation theory, interactions represented by diagrams, and infinities handled through renormalization procedures. The same style of reasoning later became essential in the development of renormalized theories of the weak and strong interactions, the Standard Model, and the broader use of Feynman diagrams across particle physics and condensed matter theory. The paper did not solve every foundational problem, but it made the new QED coherent, teachable, and operationally powerful.

## Abstract

A unified development of the subject of quantum electrodynamics is outlined, embodying the main features both of the Tomonaga-Schwinger and of the Feynman radiation theory. The theory is carried to a point further than that reached by these authors, in the discussion of higher order radiative reactions and vacuum polarization phenomena. However, the theory of these higher order processes is a program rather than a definitive theory, since no general proof of the convergence of these effects is attempted.The chief results obtained are (a) a demonstration of the equivalence of the Feynman and Schwinger theories, and (b) a considerable simplification of the procedure involved in applying the Schwinger theory to particular problems, the simplification being the greater the more complicated the problem.
