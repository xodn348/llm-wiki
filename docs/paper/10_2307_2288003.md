# Classification and Regression Trees.

## Why this mattered

The CART framework mattered because it made predictive modeling less dependent on a pre-specified functional form. Instead of forcing data into linear regression, logistic regression, or discriminant-analysis templates, it learned a hierarchy of if-then splits that partitioned the predictor space and assigned a simple prediction within each region. That made nonlinear effects, threshold behavior, high-order interactions, mixed variable types, and missing-value handling practically usable in routine statistical work while preserving an interpretable model form.

Its deeper shift was methodological: model building became an algorithmic search problem governed by out-of-sample error, pruning, and validation, not mainly by closed-form estimation inside a chosen parametric family. The grow-then-prune strategy, cost-complexity pruning, impurity-based splitting, and cross-validation gave researchers a disciplined way to build flexible models without treating the full tree as automatically trustworthy.

CART also became a foundation for later machine-learning breakthroughs. Single trees are often unstable, but their structure proved ideal for aggregation and boosting: bagging, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, Bayesian additive regression trees, and modern tabular-learning systems all inherit the central idea that many simple recursive partitions can approximate complex predictive relationships. In that sense, the paper’s importance lies not only in popularizing decision trees, but in helping move statistics toward the algorithmic, validation-centered paradigm that now dominates applied prediction.
