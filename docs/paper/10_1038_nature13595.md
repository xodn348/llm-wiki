# Biological insights from 108 schizophrenia-associated genetic loci

## Why this mattered

Before this study, schizophrenia genetics had been dominated by small candidate-gene claims and a handful of genome-wide signals, leaving the disorder’s biology difficult to connect to reproducible molecular evidence. This paper changed the scale of the field: by combining data from up to 36,989 cases and 113,075 controls, it identified 128 independent associations across 108 genome-wide significant loci, 83 of them not previously reported. The result made schizophrenia a clear example of a highly polygenic psychiatric disorder: risk was not concentrated in one or a few “schizophrenia genes,” but distributed across many common variants of small effect.

Its importance was not only statistical. The loci pointed to biologically interpretable systems, including genes expressed in brain, glutamatergic signaling, calcium-channel biology, the dopamine D2 receptor region, and immune-related tissues. That combination mattered because it connected genetic evidence to both long-standing pharmacological facts, such as the centrality of dopamine D2 blockade in antipsychotic treatment, and less settled hypotheses about synaptic and immune mechanisms. The paper therefore moved schizophrenia genetics from association discovery toward pathway-level biology.

After this work, larger psychiatric-genomics studies could treat schizophrenia as a tractable quantitative-genetic problem: expanding GWAS sample sizes, improving polygenic risk scores, prioritizing loci for fine-mapping, and integrating results with epigenomic and single-cell brain datasets. It also helped set the stage for later mechanistic work on the major histocompatibility complex region, including complement component 4 and synaptic pruning, and for subsequent GWAS that identified still more loci. The paradigm shift was that schizophrenia became experimentally approachable through reproducible population-scale genetics, even though each individual locus explained only a small fraction of risk.
