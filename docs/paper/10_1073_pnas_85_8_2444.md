# Improved tools for biological sequence comparison.

## Why this mattered

Pearson and Lipman’s 1988 FASTA paper mattered because it helped make sequence database searching a practical scientific instrument rather than a specialized computational exercise. As DNA and protein databases were growing rapidly, exact matching was no longer enough: researchers needed fast ways to detect biologically meaningful similarity despite substitutions, insertions, deletions, and local conservation. FASTA advanced this by combining speed with improved sensitivity, especially through its ability to join multiple local regions of similarity and to search translated DNA databases with protein queries. That made homology detection, gene annotation, and functional inference feasible at database scale.

The paper also clarified that sequence comparison was not just about producing an alignment, but about evaluating whether a score was meaningful. RDF2’s shuffling-based significance testing addressed a central problem in biological inference: similar-looking sequences can arise from biased composition or chance. LFASTA’s display of multiple local similarities likewise supported a richer view of sequence relationships, including repeated domains, periodic structure, and mosaic similarity. Together, these tools helped establish the modern workflow in which computational sequence search generates experimentally useful hypotheses about ancestry, structure, and function.

Its influence is visible in the tools and discoveries that followed. FASTA became one of the foundational database-search methods of molecular biology, alongside and preceding the later dominance of BLAST. The paradigm it helped consolidate was that biological knowledge could be extracted systematically from large sequence archives by statistically informed local similarity search. That shift underwrote later breakthroughs in genome annotation, comparative genomics, protein family classification, domain discovery, and evolutionary reconstruction: once new sequences could be rapidly connected to known ones, sequencing projects became not merely data-producing efforts but engines for biological interpretation.

## Abstract

We have developed three computer programs for comparisons of protein and DNA sequences. They can be used to search sequence data bases, evaluate similarity scores, and identify periodic structures based on local sequence similarity. The FASTA program is a more sensitive derivative of the FASTP program, which can be used to search protein or DNA sequence data bases and can compare a protein sequence to a DNA sequence data base by translating the DNA data base as it is searched. FASTA includes an additional step in the calculation of the initial pairwise similarity score that allows multiple regions of similarity to be joined to increase the score of related sequences. The RDF2 program can be used to evaluate the significance of similarity scores using a shuffling method that preserves local sequence composition. The LFASTA program can display all the regions of local similarity between two sequences with scores greater than a threshold, using the same scoring parameters and a similar alignment algorithm; these local similarities can be displayed as a "graphic matrix" plot or as individual alignments. In addition, these programs have been generalized to allow comparison of DNA or protein sequences based on a variety of alternative scoring matrices.
