# Simple Combinations of Lineage-Determining Transcription Factors Prime cis-Regulatory Elements Required for Macrophage and B Cell Identities

## Why this mattered

This paper helped shift enhancer biology from descriptive chromatin maps toward a mechanistic model of cell identity. Heinz and colleagues showed that macrophage and B-cell regulatory landscapes were not arbitrary collections of active marks, but were specified by small combinations of lineage-determining transcription factors. In macrophages, PU.1 collaborated with factors such as C/EBP and AP-1; in B cells, PU.1 occupied a different regulatory landscape with B-cell factors such as E2A and EBF. The key claim was that these factors “prime” distal cis-regulatory elements by opening chromatin and establishing H3K4me1-marked enhancer-like regions.

What became newly possible was a general way to read cell identity from the noncoding genome. If lineage factors define enhancer repertoires, then genome-wide maps of H3K4me1, chromatin accessibility, and transcription-factor motifs can be used to infer the regulatory logic of a cell type, not merely catalogue its expressed genes. The paper also explained why broadly expressed or signal-responsive factors can have cell-specific effects: receptors such as LXR are guided to regulatory elements already made accessible by lineage factors, so the same signal can produce different transcriptional programs in different cells.

Its influence is visible across later work on enhancer priming, pioneer and lineage-determining factors, super-enhancers, cell-type-specific GWAS interpretation, and single-cell chromatin profiling. The paper made enhancers central to the explanation of cellular identity and immune-cell responsiveness, and it helped establish a framework in which noncoding regulatory elements are treated as the operational substrate through which developmental history, transcription-factor combinations, and environmental signals are integrated.
