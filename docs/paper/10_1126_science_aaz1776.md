# The GTEx Consortium atlas of genetic regulatory effects across human tissues

## Why this mattered

Before GTEx v8, human genetics had become very good at finding trait-associated variants, but much less good at explaining what many of them did. This paper helped change the default interpretation of the noncoding genome: regulatory variation was not an occasional annotation attached to disease loci, but a pervasive, measurable layer of human biology across tissues. By analyzing 15,201 RNA-seq samples from 49 tissues of 838 donors, the consortium showed that genetic effects on expression and splicing could be mapped at near-genome scale, with regulatory associations detectable for almost all genes and with many effects depending on tissue context. That made tissue specificity, allelic heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and cell-type composition central rather than secondary features of variant interpretation.

The practical shift was that disease genetics could now be connected to molecular mechanism through a common reference atlas. GWAS signals that had previously ended at broad loci could be tested against tissue-specific eQTL and splicing-QTL maps, helping researchers nominate target genes, relevant tissues, and plausible regulatory mechanisms. This did not solve causal inference by itself, and GTEx remained limited by sample composition, postmortem tissues, bulk RNA-seq, and uneven tissue power. But it made regulatory genomics into a routinely usable substrate for interpreting complex traits, rather than a set of isolated case studies.

Subsequent work in statistical fine-mapping, transcriptome-wide association studies, colocalization, single-cell regulatory genetics, and disease-specific functional genomics built directly on this change. GTEx v8 became a reference frame against which new datasets could be compared: single-cell studies could ask which cell types explained bulk-tissue signals, large biobanks could prioritize regulatory mechanisms behind associations, and functional screens could test candidate variants and genes emerging from atlas-based inference. In that sense, the paper mattered less as a single discovery about one disease than as infrastructure for a new mode of genetics: moving from “where in the genome is the association?” toward “in which tissue, through which regulatory program, and affecting which gene does it act?”

## Abstract

The Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project was established to characterize genetic effects on the transcriptome across human tissues and to link these regulatory mechanisms to trait and disease associations. Here, we present analyses of the version 8 data, examining 15,201 RNA-sequencing samples from 49 tissues of 838 postmortem donors. We comprehensively characterize genetic associations for gene expression and splicing in cis and trans, showing that regulatory associations are found for almost all genes, and describe the underlying molecular mechanisms and their contribution to allelic heterogeneity and pleiotropy of complex traits. Leveraging the large diversity of tissues, we provide insights into the tissue specificity of genetic effects and show that cell type composition is a key factor in understanding gene regulatory mechanisms in human tissues.
