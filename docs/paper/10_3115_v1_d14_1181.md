# Convolutional Neural Networks for Sentence Classification

## Why this mattered

Kim’s paper mattered because it showed that strong sentence representations did not require elaborate feature engineering or task-specific linguistic pipelines. A shallow convolutional network, applied over pre-trained word vectors, could match or exceed state-of-the-art results across sentiment, subjectivity, question classification, and related sentence-level benchmarks with comparatively little tuning. This made neural text classification look practical rather than exotic: pre-trained distributional word representations could be treated as a general substrate, and a simple architecture could learn useful local n-gram-like patterns directly from data.

The paper also clarified an important recipe for transfer learning in NLP before the transformer era. Its comparison of static word vectors, fine-tuned vectors, and a combined “multi-channel” setup made concrete the tradeoff between preserving general semantic information and adapting representations to a target task. That idea became central to later neural NLP: start from broad pretraining, then adapt to downstream tasks, while managing the risk of overwriting useful general knowledge.

In retrospect, the model was not the final architecture for language understanding, but it helped normalize the move from sparse lexical features to learned neural representations for ordinary NLP benchmarks. It formed part of the bridge from word2vec-era transfer to later sequence models, attention mechanisms, and large pretrained language models. After this paper, sentence classification became a domain where compact neural baselines were expected to be competitive, and where architecture design increasingly focused on how best to exploit pretrained representations rather than how to hand-build task features.

## Abstract

We report on a series of experiments with convolutional neural networks (CNN) trained on top of pre-trained word vectors for sentence-level classification tasks. We show that a simple CNN with little hyperparameter tuning and static vectors achieves excellent results on multiple benchmarks. Learning task-specific vectors through fine-tuning offers further gains in performance. We additionally propose a simple modification to the architecture to allow for the use of both task-specific and static vectors. The CNN models discussed herein improve upon the state of the art on 4 out of 7 tasks, which include sentiment analysis and question classification.
