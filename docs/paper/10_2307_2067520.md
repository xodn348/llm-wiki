# In a Different Voice: Psychological Theory and Women's Development.

## Why this mattered

Gilligan’s *In a Different Voice* mattered because it challenged a central assumption in dominant developmental psychology: that moral maturity could be adequately modeled from male-centered samples and then generalized as universal. Against theories such as Lawrence Kohlberg’s stage model, Gilligan argued that women’s moral reasoning had often been misread as deficient because it emphasized responsibility, relationship, care, and contextual judgment rather than abstract rights and rule-based justice. The paradigm shift was not simply adding women to an existing framework; it exposed how supposedly neutral psychological theory could encode gendered assumptions.

After this intervention, it became newly possible to treat “care” as a serious moral orientation rather than as immaturity, dependency, or failure to reach principled reasoning. The work helped open feminist psychology, feminist ethics, and care ethics as major research programs, influencing later debates in moral development, education, legal theory, political philosophy, nursing ethics, and social policy. Subsequent breakthroughs did not all accept Gilligan’s empirical claims unchanged; many refined, contested, or complicated the gender binary in her framing. But the durable shift was methodological and conceptual: developmental theory could no longer plausibly ignore whose experience had been used to define maturity, autonomy, and moral voice.
