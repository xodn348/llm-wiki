# Significance tests and goodness of fit in the analysis of covariance structures.

## Why this mattered

Bentler and Bonett’s paper mattered because it changed covariance-structure modeling from a near-binary exercise in chi-square rejection into a comparative framework for judging model adequacy. Before this, the dominant large-sample goodness-of-fit test asked whether a proposed factor, path, or structural equation model could be rejected against an unrestricted covariance model. Bentler and Bonett showed why that standard was scientifically incomplete: with large samples, even substantively useful models could be rejected for trivial discrepancies, while with small samples multiple weak models could survive. Their key move was to introduce a structured null or baseline model, so that fit could be evaluated incrementally: not only “is this model exact?” but “how much does this model improve over a minimally constrained alternative?”

That shift made it possible to compare covariance-structure models as scientific approximations rather than treating them only as literal null hypotheses. The paper’s normed and nonnormed fit indices provided early formal tools for quantifying improvement in fit across hierarchical models, helping researchers distinguish models that were statistically rejectable from models that were substantively informative. This became foundational for structural equation modeling practice, where fit indices such as the NFI and the later Tucker-Lewis-style nonnormed family helped normalize the idea that model evaluation should combine statistical testing, comparison to baselines, and theoretical judgment.

Its influence also lies in the path it opened for later SEM methodology. Subsequent work on comparative fit indices, approximate fit, residual-based diagnostics, and practical model evaluation built on the same recognition: complex social, psychological, and behavioral theories rarely imply perfectly fitting covariance structures, so methodology must assess degrees and sources of misfit. Bentler and Bonett did not abandon hypothesis testing; they reframed it inside a broader evidential system. That reframing helped make SEM a widely usable paradigm for theory testing across the behavioral sciences rather than a fragile procedure dominated by sample-size-sensitive chi-square decisions.

## Abstract

Factor analysis, path analysis, structural equation modeling, and related multivariate statistical methods are based on maximum likelihood or generalized least squares estimation developed for covariance structure models. Large-sample theory provides a chi-square goodness-of-fit test for comparing a model against a general alternative model based on correlated variables. This model comparison is insufficient for model evaluation: In large samples virtually any model tends to be rejected as inadequate, and in small samples various competing models, if evaluated, might be equally acceptable. A general null model based on modified independence among variables is proposed to provide an additional reference point for the statistical and scientific evaluation of covariance structure models. Use of the null model in the context of a procedure that sequentially evaluates the statistical necessity of various sets of parameters places statistical methods in covariance structure analysis into a more complete framework. The concepts of ideal models and pseudo chi-square tests are introduced, and their roles in hypothesis testing are developed. The importance of supplementing statistical evaluation with incremental fit indices associated with the comparison of hierarchical models is also emphasized. Normed and nonnormed fit indices are developed and illustrated.
