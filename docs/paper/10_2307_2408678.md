# Confidence Limits on Phylogenies: An Approach Using the Bootstrap

## Why this mattered

Felsenstein’s 1985 paper made statistical uncertainty a routine part of phylogenetic inference. Before it, phylogenetic trees were often presented as single best estimates, with limited, ad hoc ways to distinguish strongly supported groupings from artifacts of finite or noisy character data. By applying the nonparametric bootstrap to phylogenies, Felsenstein gave systematists a practical way to ask which clades would recur if the observed characters were resampled. The result was not a new tree-building method, but a general framework for attaching confidence measures to inferred branches.

This changed what phylogenetics could claim. Bootstrap proportions made it possible to compare support across different parts of a tree, to identify unstable relationships, and to separate a preferred topology from the evidence for each of its internal branches. That mattered especially as molecular sequence data expanded in the late 1980s and 1990s: larger datasets produced more trees, but also sharper questions about sampling error, model choice, and conflicting signal. Felsenstein’s approach became a standard diagnostic across parsimony, distance, likelihood, and later Bayesian-era phylogenetics.

The paper also helped shift evolutionary biology toward explicitly computational and statistical tree inference. Subsequent breakthroughs in maximum-likelihood phylogenetics, model-based molecular evolution, genome-scale systematics, and comparative biology all depended on treating phylogenies as estimates with measurable uncertainty rather than fixed diagrams. Even where later methods replaced or supplemented bootstrap support with posterior probabilities, likelihood-ratio tests, or coalescent-based measures, the central expectation remained: a phylogenetic hypothesis should report not only what relationship is inferred, but how strongly the data support it.
