# An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change

## Why this mattered

Nelson and Winter’s *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* mattered because it offered a coherent alternative to the equilibrium-centered, optimizing-firm framework that dominated postwar economics. Instead of treating firms as identical maximizers with full information, it modeled them as heterogeneous organizations guided by routines, bounded rationality, search, imitation, and selection. This made economic change itself, not just movement toward equilibrium, a central object of theory.

The paradigm shift was to make innovation, organizational learning, technological trajectories, and industry evolution analytically tractable without forcing them into static optimization. After this work, economists and management scholars could study why firms differ persistently, how routines store knowledge, how markets select among competing practices, and why technological progress is path-dependent. It helped open the way for modern evolutionary economics, neo-Schumpeterian innovation studies, capabilities theory, and later work on absorptive capacity, dynamic capabilities, national innovation systems, and agent-based models of industrial change.

Its influence also came from showing that “Darwinian” economic reasoning need not be metaphor alone. Variation, selection, and retention could be formalized in models of firm behavior and market competition while remaining historically sensitive to institutions and technological regimes. Subsequent breakthroughs in innovation economics and strategy built on this shift: firms came to be understood less as production functions and more as evolving repositories of routines, knowledge, and capabilities.
