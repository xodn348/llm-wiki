# Boron nitride substrates for high-quality graphene electronics

## Why this mattered

Before this work, graphene electronics were largely constrained by the disorder introduced by the standard SiO₂ substrate: trapped charges, surface roughness, and dangling bonds masked much of graphene’s intrinsic transport behavior. Dean and colleagues showed that atomically flat, chemically inert hexagonal boron nitride could serve as a much cleaner supporting substrate, producing graphene devices with substantially higher mobility and reduced charge inhomogeneity. The key shift was not a new graphene growth method, but a new materials platform: graphene could now be studied and engineered in an environment closer to its ideal two-dimensional limit.

This made possible a generation of high-quality graphene heterostructures in which substrate disorder was no longer the dominant experimental limitation. The boron nitride platform helped enable clearer observation of delicate quantum transport phenomena, including improved fractional quantum Hall physics, ballistic transport, and later moiré superlattice effects when graphene and hBN were crystallographically aligned. More broadly, the paper helped establish the now-standard paradigm of van der Waals heterostructures: building electronic materials by stacking clean, atomically thin crystals with chosen interfaces rather than relying only on conventional epitaxial growth or lithographic patterning.

Its influence extended beyond graphene itself. By demonstrating that device performance could be transformed through the choice and assembly of adjacent two-dimensional layers, the work helped redirect the field from “graphene on a wafer” toward engineered layered quantum materials. Subsequent breakthroughs in encapsulated graphene, transition-metal dichalcogenide devices, and ultimately twisted and moiré materials all depended on the same central lesson: in two-dimensional electronics, the interface is not a passive support, but a primary design parameter.
