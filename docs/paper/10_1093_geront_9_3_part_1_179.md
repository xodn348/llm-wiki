# Assessment of Older People: Self-Maintaining and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living

## Why this mattered

Lawton and Brody’s 1969 paper mattered because it made “independence” measurable at a level more precise than survival, diagnosis, or need for institutional care. Earlier geriatric assessment had already used basic activities of daily living, but [Lawton and Brody](https://academic.oup.com/gerontologist/article-abstract/9/3_Part_1/179/552574) separated **physical self-maintenance** from **instrumental self-maintenance**: not only bathing, dressing, toileting, and feeding, but using the telephone, shopping, preparing food, housekeeping, doing laundry, traveling, managing medications, and handling finances. That distinction converted everyday competence into a structured clinical and research variable.

The paradigm shift was that aging could now be studied through functional capacity rather than only disease categories. The IADL scale made it possible to detect earlier and subtler losses of autonomy, especially among older adults still living in the community. It also gave clinicians, social workers, epidemiologists, and policymakers a common language for matching services to need, tracking decline, and evaluating interventions. A person’s ability to manage money or medication could become evidence, not anecdote.

Subsequent breakthroughs in geriatrics, dementia research, rehabilitation, long-term care planning, and population aging studies all depended on this move toward standardized functional assessment. IADL impairment became a key outcome in studies of cognitive decline and frailty, a practical measure for care eligibility and support planning, and a bridge between biomedical status and lived independence. The paper’s unusually high citation count reflects that it did not merely add another questionnaire; it helped define functional aging as a central object of measurement.
