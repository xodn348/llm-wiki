# Evidence for High-Energy Extraterrestrial Neutrinos at the IceCube Detector

## Why this mattered

This paper marked the first strong evidence that IceCube had detected a population of high-energy neutrinos not produced in Earth’s atmosphere. Before it, neutrino astronomy beyond the Sun and Supernova 1987A was largely aspirational: detectors had set limits, and models predicted cosmic neutrinos from accelerators such as active galactic nuclei, gamma-ray bursts, or star-forming galaxies, but no diffuse high-energy extraterrestrial flux had been established. The 28-event sample changed that status. By showing an excess over atmospheric backgrounds with energies, directions, and event properties consistent with a new astrophysical component, the paper opened a new observational channel on the high-energy universe.

The paradigm shift was not that IceCube had identified the sources of the neutrinos; it had not. The result mattered because it demonstrated that the universe could be studied through TeV–PeV neutrinos in practice, not only in principle. Neutrinos travel nearly undeflected by magnetic fields and can escape dense environments that absorb photons, so a confirmed astrophysical flux made it possible to probe cosmic accelerators in a way complementary to gamma-ray and cosmic-ray astronomy. It also forced source-population models to confront real event rates, spectra, and flavor expectations rather than upper limits alone.

Subsequent breakthroughs built directly on this foundation. IceCube’s later measurements refined the diffuse astrophysical neutrino spectrum, and the 2017 association of the high-energy neutrino IceCube-170922A with the flaring blazar TXS 0506+056 supplied a concrete example of multi-messenger neutrino astronomy. The 2013 Science paper was therefore a threshold result: it did not solve the source problem, but it made the source problem experimentally tractable and established high-energy neutrinos as a durable messenger for studying the most energetic nonthermal processes in the cosmos.

## Abstract

We report on results of an all-sky search for high-energy neutrino events interacting within the IceCube neutrino detector conducted between May 2010 and May 2012. The search follows up on the previous detection of two PeV neutrino events, with improved sensitivity and extended energy coverage down to about 30 TeV. Twenty-six additional events were observed, substantially more than expected from atmospheric backgrounds. Combined, both searches reject a purely atmospheric origin for the 28 events at the 4σ level. These 28 events, which include the highest energy neutrinos ever observed, have flavors, directions, and energies inconsistent with those expected from the atmospheric muon and neutrino backgrounds. These properties are, however, consistent with generic predictions for an additional component of extraterrestrial origin.
