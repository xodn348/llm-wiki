# Flexible triboelectric generator

## Why this mattered

[Fan, Tian, and Wang’s 2012 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nanoen.2012.01.004) reframed contact electrification from a nuisance of static charge into a usable energy-conversion mechanism. The device was deliberately simple: two polymer sheets with different triboelectric tendencies, metal electrodes, and mechanical deformation that produced frictional charge separation and current through an external circuit. That made mechanical energy harvesting possible with soft, lightweight, low-cost materials rather than relying only on electromagnetic, piezoelectric, or electrochemical architectures.

The paradigm shift was that everyday, irregular motion could now be treated as an electrical resource. Bending, pressing, vibration, touch, walking, air flow, and water motion all became plausible inputs for thin generators that could be integrated into flexible surfaces. This directly opened the field of triboelectric nanogenerators, later formalized as TENGs, and helped shift “self-powered systems” from a slogan toward practical device classes: wearable power sources, active mechanical sensors, electronic skin, transparent pressure sensors, implantable or biomechanical harvesters, and hybrid energy systems.

Subsequent breakthroughs built on the same core mechanism rather than replacing it. Within a year, reviews reported large gains in output density and new operating modes such as contact-separation, sliding, and single-electrode configurations; later work extended the idea to [transparent self-powered pressure sensors](https://research.nottingham.edu.cn/en/publications/transparent-triboelectric-nanogenerators-and-self-powered-pressur/) and flexible power sources for wearable electronics. The paper mattered because it gave a neglected physical effect a scalable device grammar: choose triboelectric materials, engineer surfaces and motion, collect electrostatic induction, and integrate the result where conventional generators were too rigid, heavy, or inefficient.
