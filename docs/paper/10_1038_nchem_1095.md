# Single-atom catalysis of CO oxidation using Pt1/FeOx

## Why this mattered

Before Qiao and co-workers’ 2011 [Nature Chemistry paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nchem.1095), heterogeneous catalysis largely treated ever-smaller metal nanoparticles as the practical lower limit for supported metal catalysts. This work made a sharper claim: isolated Pt atoms anchored on iron oxide could be prepared, imaged, spectroscopically characterized, and used as an active CO oxidation catalyst. The point was not simply better dispersion of platinum, but a new catalytic unit: a single supported metal atom whose activity depended on its coordination to the oxide support.

That shifted single atoms from a limiting case or surface defect into a designable catalyst class. It made possible a program of maximizing precious-metal atom economy while studying active sites with unusually well-defined nuclearity. The paper also helped reframe metal-support interactions: the support was no longer just a carrier that stabilized small particles, but part of the active site that fixed the atom’s charge, coordination, stability, and reaction pathway.

Subsequent work in single-atom catalysis built directly on this opening, extending the idea to other metals, oxide and carbon supports, electrocatalysis, photocatalysis, selective hydrogenation, and atom-trapping syntheses. Later breakthroughs also exposed the complications implicit in the original result: single atoms can restructure, agglomerate, or require neighboring support atoms and vacancies to function. Even so, Pt1/FeOx became a canonical demonstration that heterogeneous catalysis could be designed at the one-atom limit, bridging nanoparticle catalysis, surface science, and molecular single-site chemistry.
