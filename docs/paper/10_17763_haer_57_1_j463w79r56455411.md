# Knowledge and Teaching:Foundations of the New Reform

## Why this mattered

Shulman’s 1987 article mattered because it shifted the theory of teaching away from two inadequate poles: generic teaching skill on one side and subject-matter expertise on the other. Its central contribution was to name and organize the “knowledge base” of teaching, especially *pedagogical content knowledge*: the distinctive understanding teachers use to transform subject matter into forms learners can grasp. This reframed teaching as a form of professional reasoning, not merely delivery, personality, classroom management, or the mechanical application of method.

That shift made new kinds of research and reform possible. If teaching depended on specialized forms of knowledge, then teacher education could no longer be justified as either general pedagogy plus subject coursework or apprenticeship alone. Researchers could study how teachers represent ideas, anticipate misconceptions, choose examples, sequence explanations, and reflect on student understanding. Policymakers could also evaluate teacher quality less as a checklist of behaviors and more as evidence of judgment, comprehension, and disciplined adaptation.

The paper became foundational for later work on teacher expertise, subject-specific teacher education, professional standards, and instructional improvement. Its influence is visible in subsequent frameworks that treat teaching knowledge as situated at the intersection of content, learners, curriculum, assessment, and context, including later extensions such as technological pedagogical content knowledge. More broadly, Shulman helped make teaching intellectually legible as a profession: a field with its own forms of knowledge, reasoning, and evidence, rather than a craft whose most important judgments were invisible to research and reform.

## Abstract

Lee S. Shulman builds his foundation for teaching reform on an idea of teaching that emphasizes comprehension and reasoning, transformation and reflection. "This emphasis is justified," he writes, "by the resoluteness with which research and policy have so blatantly ignored those aspects of teaching in the past." To articulate and justify this conception, Shulman responds to four questions: What are the sources of the knowledge base for teaching?In what terms can these sources be conceptualized? What are the processes of pedagogical reasoning and action? and What are the implications for teaching policy and educational reform? The answers — informed by philosophy, psychology, and a growing body of casework based on young and experienced practitioners — go far beyond current reform assumptions and initiatives. The outcome for educational practitioners, scholars, and policymakers is a major redirection in how teaching is to be understood and teachers are to be trained and evaluated. This article was selected for the November 1986 special issue on "Teachers, Teaching,and Teacher Education," but appears here because of the exigencies of publishing.
