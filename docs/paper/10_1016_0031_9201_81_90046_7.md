# Preliminary reference Earth model

## Why this mattered

Dziewoński and Anderson’s *Preliminary Reference Earth Model* mattered because it gave seismology a common quantitative Earth: a self-consistent, radially layered reference model for seismic velocities, density, attenuation, and elastic parameters from the crust through the inner core. Earlier models captured parts of this structure, but PREM became the standard baseline against which travel times, normal modes, free oscillations, mantle discontinuities, and core properties could be compared. Its importance was not that it made Earth simple, but that it made deviations from a shared simple model scientifically legible.

After PREM, seismologists could describe anomalies as departures from a widely accepted reference rather than as artifacts of incompatible starting assumptions. That made global seismic tomography, mantle convection studies, core anisotropy work, and refined estimates of Earth’s mass distribution and moment of inertia more comparable and cumulative. In practice, PREM helped turn seismic observations into a map-making discipline: once the average one-dimensional Earth was fixed well enough, researchers could focus on the three-dimensional heterogeneity that records subduction, plumes, thermal structure, compositional variation, and deep Earth dynamics.

The model also became a bridge between observational seismology and geodynamics. By tying seismic wave speeds, density, attenuation, and discontinuities into one reference framework, it gave later work a disciplined way to connect earthquake waveforms and normal modes to mineral physics, mantle phase transitions, and core structure. Its “preliminary” label proved modest: PREM became one of the durable coordinate systems of deep-Earth science, not because it ended debate, but because it made sharper debates possible.
