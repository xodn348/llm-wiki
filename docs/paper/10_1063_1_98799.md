# Organic electroluminescent diodes

## Why this mattered

Tang and VanSlyke’s 1987 paper mattered because it turned organic electroluminescence from a laboratory curiosity into a practical device architecture. Earlier organic light emission generally required high voltages and produced limited efficiency, but this work showed that carefully chosen, vapor-deposited organic thin films could form a low-voltage diode with separate charge-transport and emission functions. The double-layer structure, transparent ITO anode, and Mg:Ag cathode made balanced injection of holes and electrons possible, confining recombination near the organic interface and producing bright green emission below 10 V. That combination reframed organic materials as viable semiconductor components, not merely fluorescent substances.

The paradigm shift was architectural as much as material. By separating hole transport from electron transport and locating emission at an engineered interface, the paper introduced the basic logic of the modern OLED: stacked organic layers with tailored electronic and optical roles. This made it newly plausible to design thin, bright, efficient, large-area light-emitting devices by molecular selection and layer engineering rather than by adapting inorganic LED concepts directly. The reported 1% external quantum efficiency, 1.5 lm/W luminous efficiency, and brightness above 1000 cd/m² were not just incremental improvements; they crossed a threshold at which organic displays and lighting became technologically credible.

Subsequent OLED breakthroughs built directly on this template. Later work improved color purity, operational lifetime, full-color patterning, phosphorescent emitters, small-molecule and polymer materials, and active-matrix backplanes, but the central device idea remained recognizably Tang and VanSlyke’s: organic multilayers engineered for injection, transport, recombination, and emission. The paper therefore stands at the origin of practical OLED technology, connecting molecular organic electronics to later flat-panel displays, smartphone screens, televisions, and solid-state lighting.

## Abstract

A novel electroluminescent device is constructed using organic materials as the emitting elements. The diode has a double-layer structure of organic thin films, prepared by vapor deposition. Efficient injection of holes and electrons is provided from an indium-tin-oxide anode and an alloyed Mg:Ag cathode. Electron-hole recombination and green electroluminescent emission are confined near the organic interface region. High external quantum efficiency (1% photon/electron), luminous efficiency (1.5 lm/W), and brightness (&amp;gt;1000 cd/m2) are achievable at a driving voltage below 10 V.
