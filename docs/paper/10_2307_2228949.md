# A Theory of the Allocation of Time

## Why this mattered

Becker’s paper mattered because it made time a central object of economic choice rather than a background constraint. Earlier consumer theory largely treated households as choosing among market goods with given prices and income; Becker reframed households as producers of “commodities” such as meals, health, child-rearing, education, and leisure, each requiring both market goods and time. This shifted the unit of analysis from expenditure alone to the joint allocation of money and hours, making it possible to analyze nonmarket activity with the same optimizing framework used for market behavior.

The paradigm shift was especially powerful because it assigned an opportunity cost to time. A wage was not just labor-market income; it became the implicit price of household time, changing the relative cost of cooking, commuting, schooling, parenting, searching for information, or consuming leisure-intensive goods. This opened a path for formal models of labor supply, household production, fertility, marriage, education, commuting, health behavior, and the economics of the family, many of which Becker and later economists developed into major research programs.

Its later influence came from showing how apparently “noneconomic” domains could be made analytically tractable without treating preferences or institutions as ad hoc explanations. Human capital theory, household economics, fertility models, time-use research, and modern labor economics all drew on the paper’s core move: outcomes depend not only on prices and income, but on the technologies by which people combine scarce time with goods to produce valuable activities. That made the household a site of production and allocation, not merely consumption, and helped extend microeconomic analysis into domains that became central to late twentieth-century social science.
