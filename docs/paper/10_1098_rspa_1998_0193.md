# The empirical mode decomposition and the Hilbert spectrum for nonlinear and non-stationary time series analysis

## Why this mattered

Before Huang et al., much signal analysis treated time series by projecting them onto fixed bases, especially Fourier or wavelet components. That worked well when the relevant oscillations were approximately linear, stationary, or globally describable by chosen basis functions, but it could blur transient structure or introduce harmonics that were artifacts of the representation. The paradigm shift in the [1998 paper](https://jeti.uni-freiburg.de/studenten_seminar/stud_sem_WS_08_09/Huang_etal98.pdf) was to make the decomposition adaptive: empirical mode decomposition separated data into intrinsic mode functions defined by local extrema and local time scales, then applied Hilbert spectral analysis to obtain instantaneous frequency and energy over time.

This made it newly practical to ask physically sharper questions of nonlinear and non-stationary records: when does energy move between scales, how does frequency vary within a cycle, and which oscillatory components are genuine rather than imposed by the analyst’s basis. The paper demonstrated this on both canonical nonlinear systems and observational data, including waves, turbulence-like signals, altimetry, and earthquakes. Its importance was not simply a new transform, but a change in stance: complicated measured data could be analyzed from the inside out, with the data itself defining the modes.

The resulting Hilbert-Huang transform became a foundation for later work across geophysics, ocean engineering, seismology, biomedical signal processing, climate analysis, and machinery diagnostics. Subsequent methods such as ensemble empirical mode decomposition addressed known weaknesses, especially mode mixing, while multivariate and noise-assisted variants extended the idea to more complex data. Even where later methods replaced or refined EMD, the 1998 paper established the central ambition: time-frequency analysis for real-world signals should be local, adaptive, and capable of representing nonlinearity without forcing the data into stationary templates.
