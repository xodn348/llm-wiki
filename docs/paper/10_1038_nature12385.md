# Van der Waals heterostructures

## Why this mattered

[Geim and Grigorieva’s 2013 paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12385) mattered because it recast graphene research as a broader materials-engineering program. The central idea was not merely to isolate more atomically thin crystals, but to stack them in chosen sequences, using van der Waals bonding to bypass the lattice-matching constraints that governed conventional semiconductor heterostructures. That made “designer” solids plausible: graphene, hBN, MoS2, WSe2 and related layers could be treated as modular components with atomically sharp interfaces rather than as materials that had to be grown as one chemically compatible crystal.

The paradigm shift was that dimensionality, stacking order, dielectric environment, tunnelling barriers, and interlayer coupling became controllable design variables. After this, researchers could build devices in which one layer supplied high mobility, another insulation or encapsulation, another optical activity, and another strong spin-orbit or correlated-electron physics. The paper did not report a single definitive device breakthrough; its importance was in naming and organizing a new experimental platform, at a moment when early graphene/hBN and vertical tunnelling structures had shown that manual assembly could produce clean, functional heterostructures.

Many later breakthroughs followed directly from this platform logic. Moire physics and “twistronics,” including correlated insulating states and superconductivity in magic-angle graphene superlattices, depended on the ability to assemble atomically thin layers with controlled orientation. So did later work on interlayer excitons, valley-selective optical phenomena, 2D tunnelling devices, proximity-induced spin-orbit effects, and semiconductor moire systems. In retrospect, the paper marked the transition from discovering individual two-dimensional materials to engineering artificial quantum materials one layer at a time.
