# Antiinflammatory Therapy with Canakinumab for Atherosclerotic Disease

## Why this mattered

CANTOS mattered because it separated two ideas that had long been experimentally intertwined: atherosclerosis as a lipid-storage disease and atherosclerosis as an inflammatory disease. Statins and other lipid-lowering therapies had already shown that reducing LDL cholesterol prevents cardiovascular events, but this left open whether inflammation was merely a marker of vascular risk or a causal, druggable pathway. By enrolling patients with prior myocardial infarction and persistent elevation of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, then showing that interleukin-1β inhibition reduced recurrent cardiovascular events without lowering lipid levels, the trial provided the first large randomized proof that targeted anti-inflammatory therapy could modify atherothrombotic risk independently of cholesterol.

The result did not make canakinumab a routine cardiovascular drug: the benefit was modest, dose-specific, expensive to deliver, and accompanied by a higher incidence of fatal infection, with no significant reduction in all-cause mortality. Its importance was therefore less as an immediate therapeutic replacement than as a causal demonstration. After CANTOS, residual inflammatory risk became a clinically meaningful concept alongside residual lipid risk, and hsCRP was strengthened as more than a prognostic biomarker. The paper helped make it plausible to design cardiovascular trials around immune pathways, inflammatory phenotyping, and event reduction in patients already receiving contemporary secondary-prevention therapy.

Subsequent work built directly on this shift. Trials of broader, cheaper anti-inflammatory approaches, especially low-dose colchicine in coronary disease, extended the idea that dampening innate immune activity could reduce ischemic events, though with different mechanisms and safety profiles. At the same time, negative or mixed studies of other anti-inflammatory strategies clarified that the lesson was not simply “less inflammation is better,” but that pathway, patient selection, timing, and infection risk matter. In that sense, CANTOS marked a transition from inflammation as a background feature of atherosclerosis to inflammation as a testable therapeutic axis, opening a line of cardiovascular medicine in which immune modulation could be judged by the same outcome-trial standards as lipid lowering and antithrombotic therapy.

## Abstract

BACKGROUND: Experimental and clinical data suggest that reducing inflammation without affecting lipid levels may reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease. Yet, the inflammatory hypothesis of atherothrombosis has remained unproved. METHODS: We conducted a randomized, double-blind trial of canakinumab, a therapeutic monoclonal antibody targeting interleukin-1β, involving 10,061 patients with previous myocardial infarction and a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein level of 2 mg or more per liter. The trial compared three doses of canakinumab (50 mg, 150 mg, and 300 mg, administered subcutaneously every 3 months) with placebo. The primary efficacy end point was nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death. RESULTS: At 48 months, the median reduction from baseline in the high-sensitivity C-reactive protein level was 26 percentage points greater in the group that received the 50-mg dose of canakinumab, 37 percentage points greater in the 150-mg group, and 41 percentage points greater in the 300-mg group than in the placebo group. Canakinumab did not reduce lipid levels from baseline. At a median follow-up of 3.7 years, the incidence rate for the primary end point was 4.50 events per 100 person-years in the placebo group, 4.11 events per 100 person-years in the 50-mg group, 3.86 events per 100 person-years in the 150-mg group, and 3.90 events per 100 person-years in the 300-mg group. The hazard ratios as compared with placebo were as follows: in the 50-mg group, 0.93 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.80 to 1.07; P=0.30); in the 150-mg group, 0.85 (95% CI, 0.74 to 0.98; P=0.021); and in the 300-mg group, 0.86 (95% CI, 0.75 to 0.99; P=0.031). The 150-mg dose, but not the other doses, met the prespecified multiplicity-adjusted threshold for statistical significance for the primary end point and the secondary end point that additionally included hospitalization for unstable angina that led to urgent revascularization (hazard ratio vs. placebo, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.73 to 0.95; P=0.005). Canakinumab was associated with a higher incidence of fatal infection than was placebo. There was no significant difference in all-cause mortality (hazard ratio for all canakinumab doses vs. placebo, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.83 to 1.06; P=0.31). CONCLUSIONS: Antiinflammatory therapy targeting the interleukin-1β innate immunity pathway with canakinumab at a dose of 150 mg every 3 months led to a significantly lower rate of recurrent cardiovascular events than placebo, independent of lipid-level lowering. (Funded by Novartis; CANTOS ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT01327846 .).
