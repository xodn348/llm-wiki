# Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow

## Why this mattered

Lorenz’s paper made a decisive break with the older intuition that deterministic laws implied long-term practical predictability. By showing that a small, deterministic system of nonlinear differential equations could produce bounded, nonperiodic, highly unstable trajectories, it separated determinism from forecastability. The result mattered especially because it came from an atmospheric-science problem: the limits of weather prediction were not merely due to inadequate instruments or computers, but could arise intrinsically from the mathematics of the flow itself.

The paper also made chaotic dynamics experimentally accessible. Lorenz’s three-variable convection model was simple enough to compute and visualize, yet rich enough to exhibit what became known as a strange attractor: ordered structure without periodic repetition. This gave researchers a concrete object around which to build the modern theory of chaos, including sensitive dependence on initial conditions, attractors, fractal geometry, and the qualitative study of nonlinear systems across physics, biology, chemistry, engineering, and economics.

Subsequent breakthroughs in dynamical systems, nonlinear science, and complexity theory drew directly on the conceptual opening Lorenz created. His work helped shift scientific attention from solving equations exactly to understanding the geometry and stability of their trajectories, and from seeking indefinitely precise predictions to quantifying horizons of predictability. In that sense, “Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow” did not just add a result to meteorology; it changed what scientists thought deterministic models could mean.

## Abstract

Finite systems of deterministic ordinary nonlinear differential equations may be designed to represent forced dissipative hydrodynamic flow. Solutions of these equations can be identified with trajectories in phase space. For those systems with bounded solutions, it is found that nonperiodic solutions are ordinarily unstable with respect to small modifications, so that slightly differing initial states can evolve into considerably different states. Systems with bounded solutions are shown to possess bounded numerical solutions. A simple system representing cellular convection is solved numerically. All of the solutions are found to be unstable, and almost all of them are nonperiodic. The feasibility of very-long-range weather prediction is examined in the light of these results.
