# ESTIMATING<i>F</i>-STATISTICS FOR THE ANALYSIS OF POPULATION STRUCTURE

## Why this mattered

Weir and Cockerham’s 1984 paper mattered because it turned Wright’s influential but difficult-to-estimate *F*-statistics into a practical statistical toolkit for empirical population genetics. Earlier work had established *F*<sub>IS</sub>, *F*<sub>ST</sub>, and *F*<sub>IT</sub> as central measures of inbreeding, population subdivision, and hierarchical genetic structure, but applying them consistently to finite samples, multiple alleles, and real survey data was not straightforward. By deriving estimators through an analysis-of-variance framework, Weir and Cockerham made it possible to estimate population structure from genetic marker data with explicit attention to sampling, allele frequencies, and variance components.

The paradigm shift was methodological: population structure became something that could be compared quantitatively across loci, populations, and studies rather than described only qualitatively or with ad hoc summaries. Their estimator of *F*<sub>ST</sub>, often denoted θ, became a standard measure of genetic differentiation, enabling rigorous inference about gene flow, drift, inbreeding, local adaptation, and the boundaries of populations. This was especially important as molecular marker datasets expanded through the late twentieth century, because the paper supplied a durable bridge between classical population-genetic theory and increasingly data-rich empirical genetics.

Its influence is visible in later breakthroughs across evolutionary biology, conservation genetics, human population genetics, and genomics. Genome-wide scans for differentiation, estimates of population stratification, conservation assessments of fragmented populations, and many tests for locus-specific adaptation rely either directly on Weir and Cockerham’s estimators or on methods built to refine, generalize, or critique them. The paper did not merely introduce another statistic; it helped define the statistical language in which modern population structure is still measured.
