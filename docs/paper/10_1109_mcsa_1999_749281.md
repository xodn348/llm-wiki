# Ad-hoc on-demand distance vector routing

## Why this mattered

AODV mattered because it helped define routing for mobile ad hoc networks as a problem of *reactive discovery* rather than continuous global maintenance. Earlier distance-vector approaches inherited from fixed networks depended heavily on periodic advertisements, which were costly in wireless settings where bandwidth, power, and topology stability were scarce. Perkins and Royer showed that routes could instead be created only when needed, while still preserving a central correctness property: loop freedom, maintained through destination sequence numbers. That combination made ad hoc routing look less like a weakened version of wired-network routing and more like a distinct design space suited to self-starting, infrastructureless mobile systems.

The paper made newly plausible a class of networks in which mobile devices could organize communication without base stations, preconfigured routers, or centralized control. Its importance was not only the specific protocol, but the architectural claim that dynamic wireless networks could scale by avoiding unnecessary control traffic and by repairing routes locally as links changed. This became a foundation for later work in MANETs, wireless mesh networks, disaster-recovery communications, military and sensor-network routing, and eventually broader thinking about decentralized, intermittently connected systems.

AODV also became a reference point against which later protocols were evaluated. Subsequent breakthroughs often refined, specialized, or challenged its assumptions: geographic routing reduced dependence on route discovery floods, opportunistic and delay-tolerant networking addressed partitions and extreme mobility, and mesh standards adapted on-demand ideas to more stable community or enterprise deployments. Even where AODV itself was not the final answer, the paper gave the field a durable vocabulary: route requests, route replies, sequence-number freshness, reactive control overhead, and loop-free operation under mobility.

## Abstract

An ad-hoc network is the cooperative engagement of a collection of mobile nodes without the required intervention of any centralized access point or existing infrastructure. We present Ad-hoc On Demand Distance Vector Routing (AODV), a novel algorithm for the operation of such ad-hoc networks. Each mobile host operates as a specialized router, and routes are obtained as needed (i.e., on-demand) with little or no reliance on periodic advertisements. Our new routing algorithm is quite suitable for a dynamic self starting network, as required by users wishing to utilize ad-hoc networks. AODV provides loop-free routes even while repairing broken links. Because the protocol does not require global periodic routing advertisements, the demand on the overall bandwidth available to the mobile nodes is substantially less than in those protocols that do necessitate such advertisements. Nevertheless we can still maintain most of the advantages of basic distance vector routing mechanisms. We show that our algorithm scales to large populations of mobile nodes wishing to form ad-hoc networks. We also include an evaluation methodology and simulation results to verify the operation of our algorithm.
