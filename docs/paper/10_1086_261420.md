# Increasing Returns and Long-Run Growth

## Why this mattered

Romer’s 1986 paper mattered because it helped turn long-run growth from something largely explained by forces outside the model into something economists could analyze as an outcome of economic decisions. In the Solow tradition, sustained growth depended on exogenous technological progress: the central engine was recognized but not explained. Romer instead treated knowledge as a productive input whose accumulation could generate increasing returns at the aggregate level, while preserving a competitive-equilibrium structure. That move made it possible to model persistent growth without relying on an unexplained external technology trend.

The paradigm shift was not simply that “ideas matter,” but that knowledge could have spillovers: individual firms might face ordinary private returns, while the economy as a whole benefits from a growing stock of non-rival knowledge. This created a formal route by which investment, scale, history, and policy could affect long-run growth rates rather than only transition paths. It also made plausible, within a disciplined model, outcomes that older diminishing-returns frameworks tended to rule out: amplification of small shocks, divergence across economies, and persistent advantages for larger or more knowledge-intensive economies.

The paper became a foundation for endogenous growth theory and set up later breakthroughs, including Romer’s own 1990 model of technological change with intentional research and development, as well as the broader literature on human capital, innovation, knowledge spillovers, and scale effects. After this work, growth theory could ask more specific questions about institutions, markets for ideas, education, R&D incentives, intellectual property, and international diffusion of technology. Its lasting importance lies in shifting growth economics toward explaining the production and accumulation of knowledge itself, rather than treating technological progress as a residual.

## Abstract

This paper presents a fully specified model of long-run growth in which knowledge is assumed to be an input in production that has increasing marginal productivity. It is essentially a competitive equilibrium model with endogenous technological change. In contrast to models based on diminishing returns, growth rates can be increasing over time, the effects of small disturbances can be amplified by the actions of private agents, and large countries may always grow faster than small countries. Long-run evidence is offered in support of the empirical relevance of these possibilities.
