# Evidence based medicine: what it is and what it isn't

## Why this mattered

Sackett and colleagues’ 1996 BMJ article mattered because it gave a compact, defensible definition to a movement that was already changing clinical training and practice: evidence based medicine was “the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence” in caring for individual patients. Its paradigm shift was not simply that clinicians should read research, but that clinical authority should be systematically joined to external evidence from patient-centered research and to the circumstances and preferences of the patient. That framing directly challenged a model in which expert opinion, pathophysiologic reasoning, and local custom could dominate even when controlled studies or systematic reviews pointed elsewhere.

After this paper, it became newly possible to teach, audit, and institutionalize clinical decision-making as a reproducible discipline: formulate answerable clinical questions, search for the best available evidence, critically appraise it, and apply it to a particular patient. The article also clarified what evidence based medicine was *not*: it was not “cookbook” medicine, cost-cutting by another name, or a replacement for clinical expertise. That defensive clarification was crucial, because it allowed EBM to spread through medical education, guideline development, journal formats, clinical epidemiology, and health technology assessment without being reduced to blind rule-following.

Its later influence is visible in the rise of systematic reviews, the Cochrane Collaboration’s expanding role, evidence-graded clinical guidelines, comparative effectiveness research, and modern quality-improvement programs. More broadly, the paper helped make medicine compatible with a continuously updating knowledge system: randomized trials, meta-analyses, diagnostic accuracy studies, and risk prediction tools could become part of routine care rather than remote academic products. Subsequent breakthroughs in guideline methodology, shared decision-making, and implementation science all built on the central settlement this article popularized: good medicine requires both rigorous evidence and expert judgment applied to individual patients.
