# Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment: RNA Ligands to Bacteriophage T4 DNA Polymerase

## Why this mattered

Tuerk and Gold’s paper mattered because it turned molecular recognition into an iterative search problem. Instead of discovering nucleic acid–protein interactions one natural sequence at a time, they showed that a large randomized RNA population could be subjected to repeated cycles of binding, recovery, amplification, and reselection until high-affinity ligands dominated the pool. The key conceptual move was that selection and amplification could make rare functional molecules experimentally visible. In this case, the procedure recovered both the natural bacteriophage T4 mRNA sequence recognized by T4 DNA polymerase and a distinct sequence with comparable binding affinity, demonstrating that functional binding solutions could be found without simply copying biology.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as biological. The paper introduced what became known as SELEX, making it possible to evolve nucleic acid ligands in vitro against defined targets. This created the modern aptamer field: RNA or DNA molecules could be selected for binding to proteins, small molecules, cells, and other targets, then used as probes, inhibitors, diagnostics, or therapeutic leads. The work also helped establish a broader principle that large molecular libraries could be searched by Darwinian enrichment outside living organisms, linking molecular biology, combinatorial chemistry, and directed evolution.

Its influence is visible in later breakthroughs in aptamer therapeutics, biosensors, ribozyme and RNA-structure studies, and selection-based biotechnology. The paper did not merely add a new assay for one viral polymerase interaction; it made high-affinity molecular recognition programmable by experiment. After SELEX, researchers could ask not only what nucleic acids already bind in nature, but what nucleic acids could be made to bind under imposed selective conditions.

## Abstract

High-affinity nucleic acid ligands for a protein were isolated by a procedure that depends on alternate cycles of ligand selection from pools of variant sequences and amplification of the bound species. Multiple rounds exponentially enrich the population for the highest affinity species that can be clonally isolated and characterized. In particular one eight-base region of an RNA that interacts with the T4 DNA polymerase was chosen and randomized. Two different sequences were selected by this procedure from the calculated pool of 65,536 species. One is the wild-type sequence found in the bacteriophage mRNA; one is varied from wild type at four positions. The binding constants of these two RNA's to T4 DNA polymerase are equivalent. These protocols with minimal modification can yield high-affinity ligands for any protein that binds nucleic acids as part of its function; high-affinity ligands could conceivably be developed for any target molecule.
