# STUDIES ON THE CHEMICAL NATURE OF THE SUBSTANCE INDUCING TRANSFORMATION OF PNEUMOCOCCAL TYPES

## Why this mattered

Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty’s paper made heredity chemically tractable. Before 1944, bacterial transformation was known from Griffith’s experiments, but the identity of the “transforming principle” remained uncertain, and proteins were still widely regarded as the more plausible carriers of biological specificity. By purifying the active material from Type III pneumococci and showing that it retained transforming activity while lacking demonstrable protein, lipid, or capsular polysaccharide, the paper shifted the question from whether cells could acquire heritable traits from an extract to what molecule carried that heritable information. Its central claim was careful rather than overstated: within the limits of available chemical, enzymatic, serological, and physical tests, the active substance consisted principally, if not solely, of highly polymerized DNA.

What changed was not only an interpretation of pneumococcal variation, but the experimental program available to biology. The paper showed that a defined chemical fraction could produce predictable, type-specific, serially transmissible changes in cell structure and function. That made it possible to study inheritance as the transfer and expression of a molecule rather than as an opaque property of whole cells or chromosomes. Even though acceptance was gradual, the work gave later researchers a concrete target: DNA could be isolated, degraded, measured, and tested for biological activity.

The paper became a foundation for the molecular biology that followed. Hershey and Chase’s 1952 bacteriophage experiments strengthened the case that DNA, not protein, was genetic material in another system, and Watson and Crick’s 1953 structure of DNA supplied a physical mechanism for replication and information storage. In retrospect, Avery, MacLeod, and McCarty turned “gene” from an abstract unit of inheritance into something that could be investigated as chemistry, enabling the later breakthroughs in DNA structure, coding, recombination, sequencing, and genetic engineering.

## Abstract

1. From Type III pneumococci a biologically active fraction has been isolated in highly purified form which in exceedingly minute amounts is capable under appropriate cultural conditions of inducing the transformation of unencapsulated R variants of Pneumococcus Type II into fully encapsulated cells of the same specific type as that of the heat-killed microorganisms from which the inducing material was recovered. 2. Methods for the isolation and purification of the active transforming material are described. 3. The data obtained by chemical, enzymatic, and serological analyses together with the results of preliminary studies by electrophoresis, ultracentrifugation, and ultraviolet spectroscopy indicate that, within the limits of the methods, the active fraction contains no demonstrable protein, unbound lipid, or serologically reactive polysaccharide and consists principally, if not solely, of a highly polymerized, viscous form of desoxyribonucleic acid. 4. Evidence is presented that the chemically induced alterations in cellular structure and function are predictable, type-specific, and transmissible in series. The various hypotheses that have been advanced concerning the nature of these changes are reviewed.
