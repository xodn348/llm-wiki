# Two-dimensional atomic crystals

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it showed that strictly two-dimensional crystals were not merely theoretical constructs or unstable surface curiosities, but isolable, ambient-stable materials. Before this work, atomically thin layers were widely expected to be difficult or impossible to sustain as free-standing crystals because thermal fluctuations should destabilize ideal 2D long-range order. Novoselov and colleagues demonstrated that single atomic planes could be mechanically extracted from layered bulk materials, remain continuous over macroscopic distances, and retain high crystalline quality without encapsulation or substrate protection.

The shift was broader than graphene alone. By preparing monolayers of graphite, boron nitride, transition-metal dichalcogenides, and complex oxides, the paper established “two-dimensional atomic crystals” as a general materials class rather than a one-material anomaly. This made it possible to study matter in a regime where every atom is effectively a surface atom, enabling direct control by substrates, adsorbates, strain, gates, and stacking. It opened an experimental route to electronic, optical, mechanical, and chemical behavior that could not be accessed in ordinary bulk crystals.

Subsequent breakthroughs grew directly from this change in what could be made and measured. Graphene soon became the flagship example, with unusual charge transport and relativistic-like quasiparticle physics recognized in the same period, contributing to the 2010 Nobel Prize awarded to Geim and Novoselov. But the longer-term impact was the creation of a 2D-materials platform: insulating hexagonal boron nitride, semiconducting dichalcogenides, magnetic and superconducting monolayers, and later van der Waals heterostructures assembled layer by layer. The paper’s central contribution was therefore not just a preparation technique, but a new experimental paradigm for building and probing crystals one atomic plane at a time.

## Abstract

We report free-standing atomic crystals that are strictly 2D and can be viewed as individual atomic planes pulled out of bulk crystals or as unrolled single-wall nanotubes. By using micromechanical cleavage, we have prepared and studied a variety of 2D crystals including single layers of boron nitride, graphite, several dichalcogenides, and complex oxides. These atomically thin sheets (essentially gigantic 2D molecules unprotected from the immediate environment) are stable under ambient conditions, exhibit high crystal quality, and are continuous on a macroscopic scale.
