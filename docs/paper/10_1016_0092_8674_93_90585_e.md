# A novel gene containing a trinucleotide repeat that is expanded and unstable on Huntington's disease chromosomes

## Why this mattered

This paper transformed Huntington’s disease from a genetically mapped disorder into a molecularly defined one. After a decade of linkage work near chromosome 4p16.3, it identified the disease-associated mutation as an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat in a newly described gene, then called *IT15* and later named *HTT*. That shifted the field from tracking inheritance through nearby markers to testing the causal mutation itself, making direct molecular diagnosis, presymptomatic testing, and more accurate family counseling possible.

The finding also changed how researchers understood inherited neurodegeneration. Huntington’s disease was shown to belong to a class of repeat-expansion disorders in which mutation size, instability across generations, and clinical severity could be mechanistically connected. The expanded CAG repeat implied an elongated polyglutamine tract in huntingtin, helping establish the polyglutamine disease paradigm later applied across spinocerebellar ataxias and related disorders.

Once the gene and mutation were known, the central experimental problem became tractable: researchers could make cellular and animal models, study mutant huntingtin toxicity, and follow the consequences of repeat length in defined systems. Subsequent breakthroughs in aggregation biology, transcriptional dysregulation, proteostasis, somatic repeat expansion, antisense oligonucleotides, RNA-targeting strategies, and allele-selective therapies all depended on this molecular foothold. The paper mattered because it converted Huntington’s disease from a clinical-genetic mystery into a targetable molecular disease.
