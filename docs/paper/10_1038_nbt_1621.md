# Transcript assembly and quantification by RNA-Seq reveals unannotated transcripts and isoform switching during cell differentiation

## Why this mattered

Before this [paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nbt.1621), RNA-seq was already displacing microarrays as a way to measure gene expression, but much analysis still treated genes as the main unit. Trapnell and colleagues made transcript-resolution analysis practical: their Cufflinks framework could assemble transcripts from spliced RNA-seq alignments, estimate isoform abundance, and compare expression across conditions. That shifted RNA-seq from a read-counting technology into a tool for reconstructing and quantifying the expressed transcriptome, including isoforms absent from existing annotations.

The conceptual advance was that differentiation could be studied not only as genes turning up or down, but as cells changing which transcript isoforms they use. In the muscle differentiation system analyzed in the paper, unannotated transcripts and isoform switching showed that regulatory change was substantially richer than gene-level summaries implied. This helped normalize the view that alternative splicing, promoter choice, and transcript structure are central layers of cell-state regulation, not secondary complications to be averaged away.

Its influence carried into later transcriptomics: Cufflinks and related tools helped define standard RNA-seq workflows, enabled annotation refinement from population-scale sequencing, and set the stage for later methods focused on transcript-level differential expression, isoform usage, long-read transcript discovery, and single-cell trajectory analysis. The paper mattered because it made the transcriptome a measurable, dynamic object rather than a static annotation attached to the genome.
