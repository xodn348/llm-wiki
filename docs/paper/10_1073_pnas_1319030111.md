# Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics

## Why this mattered

Freeman et al. mattered because it shifted the burden of proof in undergraduate STEM teaching. Before this paper, “active learning” was often treated as an instructional preference or a collection of local innovations, while lecture remained the default comparison condition. By aggregating 225 studies, the authors made the case at a field-wide scale: active learning produced higher exam and concept-inventory performance, while traditional lecturing nearly doubled the odds of failure. The paper therefore reframed the question from whether active learning could work in selected classrooms to why lecture should remain the default when a broad empirical record favored more participatory methods.

The paradigm shift was methodological as well as pedagogical. The paper gave STEM education researchers, departments, and professional societies a quantitative reference point for treating teaching practice as an evidence-based intervention rather than an inherited academic norm. After it, large-enrollment course redesigns, discipline-based education research, and institutional teaching initiatives could cite a consolidated empirical foundation rather than isolated classroom studies. It also made new kinds of accountability possible: failure rates, concept-inventory gains, and equity-relevant course outcomes could be evaluated as consequences of instructional design, not simply as reflections of student preparation.

Its influence can be seen in later work that moved beyond asking whether active learning improves average performance toward asking how, for whom, and under what conditions it works best. Subsequent research on structured classroom interaction, peer instruction, inclusive teaching, course-based undergraduate research experiences, and equity gaps in gateway STEM courses built on the paper’s central implication: the organization of classroom time is a causal lever in student success. In that sense, Freeman et al. did not discover active learning, but it helped make lecture-centered STEM instruction scientifically contestable at scale.

## Abstract

To test the hypothesis that lecturing maximizes learning and course performance, we metaanalyzed 225 studies that reported data on examination scores or failure rates when comparing student performance in undergraduate science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) courses under traditional lecturing versus active learning. The effect sizes indicate that on average, student performance on examinations and concept inventories increased by 0.47 SDs under active learning (n = 158 studies), and that the odds ratio for failing was 1.95 under traditional lecturing (n = 67 studies). These results indicate that average examination scores improved by about 6% in active learning sections, and that students in classes with traditional lecturing were 1.5 times more likely to fail than were students in classes with active learning. Heterogeneity analyses indicated that both results hold across the STEM disciplines, that active learning increases scores on concept inventories more than on course examinations, and that active learning appears effective across all class sizes--although the greatest effects are in small (n ≤ 50) classes. Trim and fill analyses and fail-safe n calculations suggest that the results are not due to publication bias. The results also appear robust to variation in the methodological rigor of the included studies, based on the quality of controls over student quality and instructor identity. This is the largest and most comprehensive metaanalysis of undergraduate STEM education published to date. The results raise questions about the continued use of traditional lecturing as a control in research studies, and support active learning as the preferred, empirically validated teaching practice in regular classrooms.
