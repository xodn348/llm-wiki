# Mendelian Randomization Analysis With Multiple Genetic Variants Using Summarized Data

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, Mendelian randomization was largely constrained by access to individual-level cohort data and by single-variant or small-instrument analyses. Burgess, Butterworth, and Thompson showed that the regression coefficients routinely published by genome-wide association studies could be combined across multiple uncorrelated variants to estimate causal effects with efficiency comparable to two-stage least squares on individual-level data. That made MR scalable: investigators could use public GWAS summary statistics rather than negotiate access to raw genotypes and phenotypes for every exposure-outcome question.

The paradigm shift was methodological and infrastructural. By formalizing inverse-variance weighted and likelihood-based MR with summarized data, the paper turned GWAS catalogues into reusable causal-inference resources. Its cautions were equally important: linkage disequilibrium could overstate precision, weak instruments required attention, and summarized data limited the ability to check MR assumptions. Those constraints shaped the next decade of MR methods, including sensitivity analyses for pleiotropy, stronger instrument-selection conventions, and routine LD pruning or LD-aware modeling.

Subsequent breakthroughs such as two-sample MR pipelines, MR-Base, automated phenome-wide MR scans, and methods like MR-Egger and weighted-median estimation depended on the same core possibility: causal analysis from published variant-exposure and variant-outcome associations. The paper did not make genetic instruments automatically valid, but it made large-scale, reproducible, hypothesis-generating causal epidemiology technically feasible. In that sense, it helped move MR from bespoke cohort analysis into a summary-statistics era, where the limiting factor became not only data access but the credibility of assumptions about instrument validity, pleiotropy, and shared genetic architecture.

## Abstract

Genome-wide association studies, which typically report regression coefficients summarizing the associations of many genetic variants with various traits, are potentially a powerful source of data for Mendelian randomization investigations. We demonstrate how such coefficients from multiple variants can be combined in a Mendelian randomization analysis to estimate the causal effect of a risk factor on an outcome. The bias and efficiency of estimates based on summarized data are compared to those based on individual-level data in simulation studies. We investigate the impact of gene-gene interactions, linkage disequilibrium, and 'weak instruments' on these estimates. Both an inverse-variance weighted average of variant-specific associations and a likelihood-based approach for summarized data give similar estimates and precision to the two-stage least squares method for individual-level data, even when there are gene-gene interactions. However, these summarized data methods overstate precision when variants are in linkage disequilibrium. If the P-value in a linear regression of the risk factor for each variant is less than 1×10⁻⁵, then weak instrument bias will be small. We use these methods to estimate the causal association of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) on coronary artery disease using published data on five genetic variants. A 30% reduction in LDL-C is estimated to reduce coronary artery disease risk by 67% (95% CI: 54% to 76%). We conclude that Mendelian randomization investigations using summarized data from uncorrelated variants are similarly efficient to those using individual-level data, although the necessary assumptions cannot be so fully assessed.
