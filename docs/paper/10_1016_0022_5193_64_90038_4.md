# The genetical evolution of social behaviour. I

## Why this mattered

Hamilton’s paper changed evolutionary biology by giving altruism a rigorous genetic accounting. Before it, costly social behavior was often treated as an anomaly or explained loosely through “good of the species” reasoning. Hamilton showed that a gene could increase in frequency even when it reduced its bearer’s direct reproduction, provided it sufficiently increased the reproduction of genetic relatives. This reframed adaptation around **inclusive fitness**: direct reproduction plus effects on others, weighted by genetic relatedness.

What became newly possible was a quantitative theory of social evolution. Behaviors such as helping, alarm calling, brood care, and the extreme sterility of workers in eusocial insects could now be analyzed as consequences of gene transmission rather than exceptions to natural selection. The paper made relatedness a central variable and supplied the logic later summarized as **Hamilton’s rule**, \( rb > c \): altruism is favored when the benefit to recipients, discounted by relatedness, exceeds the cost to the actor.

Its influence ran through much of late twentieth-century evolutionary theory. It helped displace group-selection explanations, shaped the rise of kin selection and sociobiology, and provided a foundation for later work on sex ratios, parent-offspring conflict, reciprocal altruism, cooperation, and the Price equation’s formal treatment of selection. The paper mattered not because it solved every problem of social behavior, but because it made social traits mathematically tractable within Darwinian genetics.
