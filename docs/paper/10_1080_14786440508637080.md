# LXXIX. <i>The scattering of α and β particles by matter and the structure of the atom</i>

## Why this mattered

Rutherford’s 1911 paper mattered because it overturned the then-plausible picture of the atom as a diffuse distribution of positive charge with embedded electrons. By showing that the observed large-angle scattering of α particles could be explained by a very small, massive, centrally concentrated charge, Rutherford made the atom mostly empty space with a dense center. The result did not yet provide a complete stable atomic model, but it decisively changed the problem: atomic structure was no longer a matter of arranging charge throughout a volume, but of explaining electrons around a central charge.

The paper also made scattering a quantitative probe of invisible structure. Rutherford connected measurable deflections to the magnitude and concentration of atomic charge, turning α-particle bombardment into a way to infer what could not be directly seen. That method became foundational: Bohr’s 1913 model required Rutherford’s nuclear atom, Moseley’s work linked nuclear charge to atomic number, and later nuclear and particle physics repeatedly used scattering experiments to reveal internal structure. In that sense, the paper did not merely propose a new atom; it introduced a durable experimental logic for discovering subatomic architecture.
