# Mapping and quantifying mammalian transcriptomes by RNA-Seq

## Why this mattered

Before this work, transcriptome-wide expression measurement was dominated by microarrays and tag-based assays, which were powerful but constrained by probe design, limited dynamic range, and weaker access to unannotated transcripts or splice structure. Mortazavi, Williams, McCue, Schaeffer, and Wold showed that deep sequencing of RNA could instead make transcriptomics a digital counting problem: reads mapped across the genome could quantify known genes, reveal previously unannotated transcription, and directly detect splice-crossing reads. Their use of tens of millions of mapped short reads from mouse tissues, with RNA standards showing detection across five orders of magnitude, helped establish RNA-Seq as a quantitative replacement for array-era expression profiling.

The paradigm shift was not simply better measurement; it was a change in what counted as observable. After this paper, mammalian transcriptomes could be surveyed without being limited to a predefined microarray design, allowing expression level, transcript boundaries, tissue specificity, and splicing evidence to be extracted from the same experiment. That made transcriptomics more extensible: improvements in sequencing depth, read length, alignment, and statistical modeling could immediately translate into better biological resolution.

Its influence is visible in much of modern functional genomics. RNA-Seq became the standard substrate for differential expression analysis, transcript reconstruction, isoform quantification, cancer expression atlases, developmental time courses, perturbation screens, and eventually single-cell RNA sequencing. Later methods such as Cufflinks/StringTie-style transcript assembly, RSEM/kallisto/salmon-style quantification, and large consortia projects built on the premise demonstrated here: that the transcriptome could be mapped and measured directly by sequencing, at genome scale, with enough quantitative fidelity to make it a foundational assay rather than a specialist technique.
