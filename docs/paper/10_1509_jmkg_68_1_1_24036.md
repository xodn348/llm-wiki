# Evolving to a New Dominant Logic for Marketing

## Why this mattered

Vargo and Lusch’s paper mattered because it gave marketing a coherent alternative to the goods-dominant view inherited from classical economics: the idea that firms make value, embed it in products, and transfer it through transactions. Its paradigm shift was to treat service, not goods, as the fundamental basis of exchange. Goods became distribution mechanisms for service, value became something realized in use rather than contained in output, and customers became participants in value creation rather than endpoints of a production chain. This reframed marketing from a discipline centered on exchange events and tangible products into one centered on resources, processes, relationships, knowledge, and ongoing interaction.

After this paper, it became possible to integrate several previously separate research streams: services marketing, relationship marketing, resource-based views of the firm, customer experience, networks, and value co-creation. The paper supplied a vocabulary and theoretical center of gravity for asking different questions: how actors integrate resources, how value emerges in use, how firms can only offer value propositions, and how markets are shaped through interaction rather than merely served through distribution. That shift helped move marketing theory beyond the product-versus-service distinction and made service-dominant logic a platform for later work on ecosystems, platforms, customer engagement, institutional arrangements, and co-creation.

Its influence is visible in the subsequent expansion from firm-customer dyads to broader service ecosystems. Later breakthroughs in service-dominant logic generalized the original argument: value is co-created by multiple actors, coordinated through institutions, and embedded in networks rather than isolated transactions. This made the paper paradigm-shifting not because it introduced a single technique, but because it changed the unit of analysis and the assumed source of value in marketing. For a field long organized around products, channels, and exchange, it made relational, experiential, and systemic accounts of markets intellectually central rather than peripheral.

## Abstract

Marketing inherited a model of exchange from economics, which had a dominant logic based on the exchange of “goods,” which usually are manufactured output. The dominant logic focused on tangible resources, embedded value, and transactions. Over the past several decades, new perspectives have emerged that have a revised logic focused on intangible resources, the cocreation of value, and relationships. The authors believe that the new perspectives are converging to form a new dominant logic for marketing, one in which service provision rather than goods is fundamental to economic exchange. The authors explore this evolving logic and the corresponding shift in perspective for marketing scholars, marketing practitioners, and marketing educators.
