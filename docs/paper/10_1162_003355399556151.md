# A Theory of Fairness, Competition, and Cooperation

## Why this mattered

Fehr and Schmidt’s paper mattered because it gave economists a compact, tractable way to model a fact that standard self-interest models handled poorly: people often reject unfairness, punish defectors, and sustain cooperation even when doing so is materially costly. Its central move was not to deny strategic rationality, but to change the utility function. By representing some agents as disliking inequitable outcomes, especially disadvantageous inequality, the paper made fairness preferences usable inside ordinary game-theoretic equilibrium analysis. This helped move behavioral economics from a collection of anomalies toward a framework that could generate precise comparative predictions.

The paradigm shift was that “fairness” became an economically disciplined explanatory variable rather than an informal appeal to morality or culture. The model showed why the same population could look selfish in competitive markets, fair-minded in bilateral bargaining, and cooperative under punishment institutions: equilibrium behavior depends on the environment and on the mix of selfish and inequality-averse types. That made it newly possible to analyze institutions by asking when fair types discipline outcomes, when selfish types dominate, and when competition erases or amplifies social preferences.

The paper became a foundation for later work on social preferences, reciprocity, punishment, public-goods provision, contract theory, labor-market experiments, and institutional design. Alongside related models such as Bolton and Ockenfels’s ERC framework and subsequent experimental work on strong reciprocity and peer punishment, it helped establish that preferences over relative payoffs could be incorporated into mainstream economic theory without abandoning formal rigor. Its lasting influence lies in making fairness and cooperation analytically portable across bargaining, markets, organizations, and collective-action problems.

## Abstract

There is strong evidence that people exploit their bargaining power in competitive markets but not in bilateral bargaining situations. There is also strong evidence that people exploit free-riding opportunities in voluntary cooperation games. Yet, when they are given the opportunity to punish free riders, stable cooperation is maintained, although punishment is costly for those who punish. This paper asks whether there is a simple common principle that can explain this puzzling evidence. We show that if some people care about equity the puzzles can be resolved. It turns out that the economic environment determines whether the fair types or the selfish types dominate equilibrium behavior
