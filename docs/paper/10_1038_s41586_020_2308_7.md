# The mutational constraint spectrum quantified from variation in 141,456 humans

## Why this mattered

Before this work, human genetics had many catalogs of variants, but lacked a population-scale, quantitatively calibrated map of which genes humans can and cannot tolerate losing. Karczewski and colleagues turned rare loss-of-function variation across 141,456 sequenced individuals into a genome-wide measure of mutational constraint, separating genes that accumulate inactivating variants from those conspicuously depleted of them. The paradigm shift was that “gene essentiality” could now be inferred directly from natural human variation, not only from model organisms, family studies, or cell screens.

This made it newly possible to use absence as evidence. If a gene had far fewer observed loss-of-function variants than expected under a mutation model, that depletion became a practical signal that disruption of the gene was likely deleterious. The paper’s constraint metrics, especially the loss-of-function observed/expected framework and LOEUF ranking, gave researchers a way to prioritize candidate disease genes, interpret rare variants in clinical sequencing, and distinguish plausible pathogenic disruptions from variants in genes that appear broadly tolerant of inactivation.

Its influence carried into the next phase of rare-disease discovery, biobank-scale genetics, and therapeutic target evaluation. Later studies could ask not only whether a variant was rare, but whether it occurred in a gene where such variants should almost never be seen. Conversely, naturally occurring human “knockouts” in tolerant genes helped sharpen expectations about safety and druggability. In that sense, gnomAD’s constraint spectrum became a reference layer for modern genomics: a population-derived baseline against which disease cohorts, functional genomics experiments, and gene-editing or therapeutic hypotheses could be interpreted.

## Abstract

Abstract Genetic variants that inactivate protein-coding genes are a powerful source of information about the phenotypic consequences of gene disruption: genes that are crucial for the function of an organism will be depleted of such variants in natural populations, whereas non-essential genes will tolerate their accumulation. However, predicted loss-of-function variants are enriched for annotation errors, and tend to be found at extremely low frequencies, so their analysis requires careful variant annotation and very large sample sizes 1 . Here we describe the aggregation of 125,748 exomes and 15,708 genomes from human sequencing studies into the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). We identify 443,769 high-confidence predicted loss-of-function variants in this cohort after filtering for artefacts caused by sequencing and annotation errors. Using an improved model of human mutation rates, we classify human protein-coding genes along a spectrum that represents tolerance to inactivation, validate this classification using data from model organisms and engineered human cells, and show that it can be used to improve the power of gene discovery for both common and rare diseases.
