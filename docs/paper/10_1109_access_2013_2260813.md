# Millimeter Wave Mobile Communications for 5G Cellular: It Will Work!

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it directly challenged a central assumption in cellular engineering: that millimeter-wave bands were too lossy, too easily blocked, and too propagation-hostile for mobile access networks. By reporting urban measurements at 28 and 38 GHz and showing that directional, steerable antennas could recover usable links through beamforming and reflected paths, Rappaport and coauthors reframed mmWave from a niche backhaul or short-range technology into a plausible cellular access layer. The title’s claim, “It Will Work,” was deliberately forceful: the paper did not prove that mmWave cellular was already deployable at scale, but it supplied evidence that the propagation problem was engineering-constrained rather than physically prohibitive.

The paradigm shift was that spectrum above traditional cellular bands became a serious design space for mainstream mobile broadband. After this paper, 5G research could treat very wide mmWave channels, dense small cells, highly directional beamforming, and new channel models as parts of one coherent cellular architecture rather than separate laboratory topics. This helped make possible a wave of subsequent work on hybrid analog-digital beamforming, massive MIMO arrays, beam training, blockage mitigation, and mmWave channel modeling, all of which became central to early 5G standardization and deployment.

Its influence also lay in timing. Around 2013, mobile data demand was rising while sub-6 GHz spectrum was increasingly crowded; the paper gave industry and academia a credible empirical basis for looking upward in frequency instead of only improving spectral efficiency in legacy bands. Later 5G systems did not adopt mmWave as a universal replacement for lower bands, and practical deployments exposed limits in coverage, mobility, indoor penetration, and economics. But the paper changed the feasible frontier: it made mmWave cellular a legitimate pillar of 5G research and helped turn an underused spectrum region into a standardized, commercially deployed option for high-capacity wireless access.

## Abstract

The global bandwidth shortage facing wireless carriers has motivated the exploration of the underutilized millimeter wave (mm-wave) frequency spectrum for future broadband cellular communication networks. There is, however, little knowledge about cellular mm-wave propagation in densely populated indoor and outdoor environments. Obtaining this information is vital for the design and operation of future fifth generation cellular networks that use the mm-wave spectrum. In this paper, we present the motivation for new mm-wave cellular systems, methodology, and hardware for measurements and offer a variety of measurement results that show 28 and 38 GHz frequencies can be used when employing steerable directional antennas at base stations and mobile devices.
