# Identification of the Cystic Fibrosis Gene: Chromosome Walking and Jumping

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it showed that a human disease gene could be found without knowing the defective protein or its biochemical function. Cystic fibrosis had been mapped to chromosome 7, but the disease mechanism was still obscure. By using chromosome walking, chromosome jumping, and cDNA hybridization across a large genomic interval, Rommens and colleagues helped turn positional cloning from a theoretical strategy into a practical route to disease-gene discovery.

The immediate consequence was the identification of the CF gene, later known as *CFTR*, and the opening of cystic fibrosis to molecular diagnosis. Carrier testing, prenatal diagnosis, mutation catalogs, genotype-phenotype studies, and population-level screening all became possible because the disease could now be read at the DNA sequence level rather than inferred only from symptoms or linkage markers.

Its longer-term importance was broader than cystic fibrosis. The work helped establish the paradigm that inherited disease could be attacked through maps, markers, genomic clones, and transcripts before function was understood. That approach shaped the next decade of human genetics, contributed to the logic of the Human Genome Project, and set the stage for later mechanistic and therapeutic breakthroughs, including understanding CFTR as an ion channel and developing mutation-targeted CFTR modulator drugs.

## Abstract

An understanding of the basic defect in the inherited disorder cystic fibrosis requires cloning of the cystic fibrosis gene and definition of its protein product. In the absence of direct functional information, chromosomal map position is a guide for locating the gene. Chromosome walking and jumping and complementary DNA hybridization were used to isolate DNA sequences, encompassing more than 500,000 base pairs, from the cystic fibrosis region on the long arm of human chromosome 7. Several transcribed sequences and conserved segments were identified in this cloned region. One of these corresponds to the cystic fibrosis gene and spans approximately 250,000 base pairs of genomic DNA.
