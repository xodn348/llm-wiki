# Institutional Ecology, `Translations' and Boundary Objects: Amateurs and Professionals in Berkeley's Museum of Vertebrate Zoology, 1907-39

## Why this mattered

Star and Griesemer’s paper mattered because it shifted explanations of scientific cooperation away from a single actor’s successful “translation” of others’ interests and toward a more plural, infrastructural account of how coordination actually holds together. In their study of Berkeley’s Museum of Vertebrate Zoology, amateurs, professional zoologists, patrons, trappers, administrators, and curators did not need to share one unified meaning of “California fauna” in order to work productively. What made cooperation possible was a set of practices and artifacts, including standardized collecting methods, specimens, field notes, maps, repositories, and forms, that were flexible enough to serve different local purposes yet stable enough to support collective scientific work.

The paper’s central concept, the “boundary object,” became a durable alternative to models that treated consensus as the necessary basis of knowledge production. It made it possible to analyze scientific institutions as ecologies of partially aligned worlds, where ambiguity, translation, classification, and standardization are not peripheral problems but core mechanisms of collaboration. This was especially important for understanding museums, databases, classifications, standards, forms, protocols, and other seemingly mundane infrastructures as active participants in making science portable, comparable, and cumulative.

Subsequent work in science and technology studies, organization studies, information studies, design, infrastructure studies, and human-computer interaction repeatedly built on this insight. Boundary objects helped explain how interdisciplinary teams, distributed databases, clinical records, biodiversity collections, software platforms, and policy-science interfaces can function without requiring total agreement among participants. The breakthrough was not simply naming a useful concept; it was showing that heterogeneous cooperation is often achieved through material and procedural arrangements that preserve difference while enabling shared work.

## Abstract

Scientific work is heterogeneous, requiring many different actors and viewpoints. It also requires cooperation. The two create tension between divergent viewpoints and the need for generalizable findings. We present a model of how one group of actors managed this tension. It draws on the work of amateurs, professionals, administrators and others connected to the Museum of Vertebrate Zoology at the University of California, Berkeley, during its early years. Extending the Latour-Callon model of interessement, two major activities are central for translating between viewpoints: standardization of methods, and the development of `boundary objects'. Boundary objects are both adaptable to different viewpoints and robust enough to maintain identity across them. We distinguish four types of boundary objects: repositories, ideal types, coincident boundaries and standardized forms.
