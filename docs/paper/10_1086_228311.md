# Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness

## Why this mattered

Granovetter’s 1985 article mattered because it gave economic sociology a compact alternative to two dominant explanations of economic action: the undersocialized actor of neoclassical economics and the oversocialized actor of some sociological accounts. Its central move was to argue that market behavior in modern industrial society is not detached from social life, nor simply determined by norms, but embedded in ongoing networks of concrete social relations. That reframed trust, opportunism, price, contract, and organizational form as outcomes shaped by relational structure, not as matters explainable only by individual incentives or generalized culture.

The paradigm shift was methodological as well as theoretical. After this paper, researchers could study markets as social structures: networks of firms, careers, credit, ethnic business communities, supply chains, and interorganizational alliances became legitimate objects for explaining economic outcomes. Granovetter’s critique of Oliver Williamson’s markets-and-hierarchies program was especially important because it showed that the choice between market exchange and hierarchy could not be understood only through transaction costs and opportunism; social ties could generate trust, information, and constraint in ways that formal governance alone did not capture.

Its influence can be seen in the consolidation of “new economic sociology” and in later work on social capital, structural holes, network governance, institutional entrepreneurship, diffusion, entrepreneurship, labor markets, and platform or innovation ecosystems. The paper did not claim that networks make economic action harmonious or efficient; it explicitly treated social relations as capable of producing trust, malfeasance, exclusion, and disorder. That balance made “embeddedness” durable: it became a bridge concept linking economics, sociology, organization theory, and management research without reducing one discipline’s questions to another’s assumptions.

## Abstract

How behavior and institutions are affected by social relations is one of the classic questions of social theory. This paper concerns the extent to which economic action is embedded in structures of social relations, in modern industrial society. Although the usual neoclasical accounts provide an undersocialized or atomized-actor explanation of such action, reformist economists who attempt to bring social structure back in do so in the way criticized by Dennis Wrong. Under-and oversocialized accounts are paradoxically similar in their neglect of ongoing structures of social relations, and a sophisticated account of economic action must consider its embeddedness in such structures. The argument in illustrated by a critique of Oliver Williamson's markets and hierarchies research program.
