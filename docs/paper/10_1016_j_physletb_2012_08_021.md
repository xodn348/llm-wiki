# Observation of a new boson at a mass of 125 GeV with the CMS experiment at the LHC

## Why this mattered

The CMS paper, published alongside the ATLAS discovery result in 2012, marked the experimental observation of a new boson near 125 GeV whose production and decay channels were consistent with the long-sought Higgs boson. Its importance was not merely the addition of another particle to the Standard Model, but the empirical confirmation of the mechanism that gives masses to the electroweak gauge bosons while preserving gauge symmetry. After decades in which the Higgs field had been a central but unverified component of particle physics, the result turned the electroweak theory from a highly successful framework with a missing cornerstone into a experimentally completed theory at accessible energies.

The discovery changed what could be asked experimentally. Before 2012, searches were primarily existential: whether a Higgs-like particle existed, and at what mass. After this paper, the program shifted to precision measurement: determining the boson’s spin, parity, couplings to fermions and vector bosons, decay rates, production modes, and possible deviations from Standard Model predictions. The 125 GeV mass also had deep consequences, placing the observed particle in a range compatible with Standard Model extrapolations while sharpening questions about vacuum stability, naturalness, and the absence of clear new physics at the TeV scale.

Subsequent breakthroughs built directly on this transition from discovery to characterization. LHC Run 1 and Run 2 measurements established that the new boson was spin-0 and increasingly Higgs-like, while later observations of decays and production involving fermions, including tau leptons, bottom quarks, and top-associated production, confirmed that the field couples to matter broadly as the Standard Model predicts. The paper therefore opened the modern Higgs era: a precision frontier in which the Higgs boson became both a triumph of quantum field theory and a sensitive probe for physics beyond it.
