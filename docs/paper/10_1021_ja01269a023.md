# Adsorption of Gases in Multimolecular Layers

## Why this mattered

Before Brunauer, Emmett, and Teller, adsorption theory was dominated by monolayer pictures such as Langmuir’s: useful, but poorly matched to many real gas-solid isotherms near saturation. The 1938 BET paper reframed physical adsorption as a multilayer equilibrium process and turned that picture into a usable equation, linking measured adsorption curves to the amount required to form a complete first layer. That made surface area experimentally accessible rather than merely inferential: once the monolayer capacity was estimated, the area could be calculated from the molecular cross-section of the adsorbed gas. In practice, this converted gas adsorption into a standard metrology tool for powders, catalysts, carbons, oxides, and porous solids. [Original paper](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/ja01269a023)

The paradigm shift was not just a better isotherm; it was a bridge from thermodynamic adsorption measurements to material structure. BET analysis made “specific surface area” a routine, comparable quantity, allowing chemists and engineers to connect catalytic activity, sintering, porosity, particle size, and preparation conditions to a number obtained by a reproducible experiment. This helped move surface chemistry from qualitative descriptions of “active” solids toward quantitative design and characterization.

Its influence persists because many later breakthroughs depended on measuring and optimizing internal surface: heterogeneous catalysts, activated carbons, zeolites, mesoporous silicas, battery and fuel-cell materials, and metal-organic frameworks all rely on surface area and porosity as central descriptors. Modern adsorption science has gone beyond the simplifying assumptions of BET, especially for microporous or heterogeneous materials, but the method remains the historical baseline against which more detailed pore-size, surface-energy, and molecular-simulation approaches are compared.
