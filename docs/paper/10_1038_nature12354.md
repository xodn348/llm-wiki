# Ultrasensitive fluorescent proteins for imaging neuronal activity

## Why this mattered

Before [this paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12354), genetically encoded calcium indicators promised cell-type-specific, chronic, non-invasive readout of neural activity, but their sensitivity often lagged behind synthetic dyes. Chen and colleagues’ GCaMP6 sensors changed that balance. By combining structure-guided mutagenesis with neuron-based screening, they produced fluorescent proteins sensitive enough to detect single action potentials in mouse cortical neurons and activity in small compartments such as dendritic spines, while also working across zebrafish, flies, and mice in vivo.

The practical shift was from imaging selected activity under favorable conditions to routinely recording large populations of genetically defined neurons over repeated sessions. Because GCaMP6 could be delivered genetically and retained over chronic timescales, it made neural activity imaging more compatible with behaving animals, circuit-level experiments, and longitudinal studies of the same cells. This helped move systems neuroscience toward optical population recording as a standard experimental mode rather than a specialized technical achievement.

Subsequent advances in two-photon microscopy, light-sheet imaging, miniature microscopes, virtual-reality behavior, and large-scale brain mapping all benefited from this sensor jump. Later indicators, including newer GCaMP and jGCaMP variants, improved speed, brightness, targeting, and spectral range, but the 2013 GCaMP6 paper established the modern expectation that genetically encoded sensors could be sensitive enough for high-resolution functional neuroscience.
