# Information Theory and Statistical Mechanics

## Why this mattered

Jaynes’s paper reframed statistical mechanics from a theory requiring special physical postulates about long-run dynamical behavior into an inference problem: given macroscopic constraints, choose the probability distribution that adds the least unwarranted information. That shift made entropy a general rule of reasoning, not only a thermodynamic state function. The canonical machinery of equilibrium statistical mechanics, especially the partition function and Boltzmann weights, could then be derived as maximum-entropy estimates constrained by known quantities such as mean energy, rather than justified by assumptions like ergodicity or equal a priori probabilities.

What became newly possible was a unified way to construct probability distributions whenever knowledge is incomplete but constraints are known. The maximum-entropy principle gave scientists a constructive method for assigning distributions in physics and beyond: not by guessing microscopic ignorance away, but by making the most noncommittal estimate consistent with the available facts. This clarified the boundary between the physical part of a problem, enumerating states and their properties, and the inferential part, assigning probabilities over those states.

The paper’s influence extended well beyond equilibrium statistical mechanics. It helped make entropy maximization a general language for inverse problems, Bayesian inference, image reconstruction, spectral estimation, ecology, economics, and machine learning. Later developments such as maximum-entropy models, exponential-family modeling, variational inference, and modern probabilistic approaches to physical systems all drew on the same central insight: constrained uncertainty is not a defect to be patched over, but a principled basis for prediction.

## Abstract

Information theory provides a constructive criterion for setting up probability distributions on the basis of partial knowledge, and leads to a type of statistical inference which is called the maximum-entropy estimate. It is the least biased estimate possible on the given information; i.e., it is maximally noncommittal with regard to missing information. If one considers statistical mechanics as a form of statistical inference rather than as a physical theory, it is found that the usual computational rules, starting with the determination of the partition function, are an immediate consequence of the maximum-entropy principle. In the resulting "subjective statistical mechanics," the usual rules are thus justified independently of any physical argument, and in particular independently of experimental verification; whether or not the results agree with experiment, they still represent the best estimates that could have been made on the basis of the information available.It is concluded that statistical mechanics need not be regarded as a physical theory dependent for its validity on the truth of additional assumptions not contained in the laws of mechanics (such as ergodicity, metric transitivity, equal a priori probabilities, etc.). Furthermore, it is possible to maintain a sharp distinction between its physical and statistical aspects. The former consists only of the correct enumeration of the states of a system and their properties; the latter is a straightforward example of statistical inference.
