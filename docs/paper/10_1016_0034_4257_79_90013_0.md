# Red and photographic infrared linear combinations for monitoring vegetation

## Why this mattered

Tucker’s paper mattered because it helped turn vegetation remote sensing from visual interpretation of color-infrared imagery into a quantitative biophysical measurement problem. Using field spectrometer data, Tucker compared red, photographic infrared, and several red-infrared combinations against measured grass-canopy properties such as biomass, leaf water content, and chlorophyll content. The key result was not simply that vegetation looked different in infrared, which was already known, but that normalized red-infrared combinations were especially sensitive to green leaf area and photosynthetically active biomass. That made vegetation condition expressible as a compact, repeatable index rather than as a site-specific photographic judgment.

The paradigm shift was that plant productivity could now be monitored synoptically and repeatedly from multispectral sensors. The red/near-infrared contrast exploited a basic plant-physiology signal: chlorophyll absorbs strongly in red wavelengths while healthy leaf structure reflects strongly in near infrared. Tucker’s analysis gave empirical support for using that contrast as a general monitoring tool, helping establish the normalized vegetation-index tradition that later became central to NDVI-based remote sensing. After this, satellites such as Landsat, NOAA AVHRR, and later MODIS could be used not only to map land cover, but to track greenness, drought, phenology, crop condition, rangeland productivity, deforestation, and large-scale carbon-cycle dynamics over time.
