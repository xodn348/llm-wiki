# Induction of Pluripotent Stem Cells from Mouse Embryonic and Adult Fibroblast Cultures by Defined Factors

## Why this mattered

Takahashi and Yamanaka’s 2006 paper changed the central assumption that pluripotency was a state obtainable only from embryos, germ cells, or nuclear transfer. By introducing a small defined set of transcription factors into mouse fibroblasts, they showed that differentiated somatic cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells, or iPS cells. The result made pluripotency look less like an irreversible developmental origin and more like a controllable molecular state.

What became newly possible was the derivation of patient- or disease-specific pluripotent cells without using embryos and without requiring oocytes. This opened a practical route to modeling genetic disease in vitro, screening drugs in relevant cell types, and studying early developmental programs from accessible adult tissues. The original cells were not yet equivalent in every respect to embryonic stem cells, and the use of integrating retroviruses and oncogenic factors such as `c-Myc` carried obvious limitations, but the conceptual proof was decisive.

The paper directly set up the rapid 2007 extension of iPS reprogramming to human cells, followed by safer non-integrating methods, improved differentiation protocols, organoid systems, and later gene-editing workflows using patient-derived pluripotent lines. Its influence also reached beyond stem-cell biology: it helped establish cellular identity as experimentally reprogrammable, shaping later work in direct lineage conversion, regenerative medicine, and disease modeling. This is why the paper is treated as paradigm-shifting rather than merely technical: it replaced a boundary in developmental biology with a method.
