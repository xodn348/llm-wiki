# Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses: The PRISMA Statement

## Why this mattered

PRISMA mattered because it turned systematic review reporting from an uneven craft practice into a standardized, auditable form of scientific communication. Building on the earlier QUOROM statement, Moher, Liberati, Tetzlaff, and Altman gave authors a 27-item checklist and flow diagram for describing how evidence was identified, screened, assessed, and synthesized. The paradigm shift was not a new statistical method, but a reporting infrastructure: readers, reviewers, guideline panels, and clinicians could now see where studies entered or left the review process, how eligibility decisions were made, and whether risks of bias and synthesis methods were reported clearly enough to judge the result.

After PRISMA, systematic reviews became more reproducible, comparable, and easier to evaluate across medicine and public health. It helped make transparent evidence synthesis a baseline expectation for clinical guidelines, health technology assessment, and evidence-based medicine, especially as meta-analyses increasingly influenced policy and standards of care. By specifying what must be reported rather than merely recommending that reviews be “systematic,” PRISMA made omissions visible: unclear search strategies, unexplained exclusions, unreported bias assessment, and selective outcome synthesis became identifiable weaknesses rather than hidden methodological variation.

Its later importance is visible in the ecosystem it helped enable: protocol registration, living systematic reviews, network meta-analysis reporting extensions, and increasingly automated evidence-surveillance tools all depend on structured, explicit review methods. Subsequent PRISMA extensions and the 2020 update reflect the same core insight of the 2009 statement: when research synthesis becomes a central layer of scientific knowledge, the methods of finding, filtering, and combining studies must themselves be reported with enough precision to be inspected, replicated, and trusted.
