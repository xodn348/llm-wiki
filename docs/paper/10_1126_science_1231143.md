# Multiplex Genome Engineering Using CRISPR/Cas Systems

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, programmable genome editing in mammalian cells largely depended on protein-engineered nucleases such as zinc-finger nucleases and TALENs, where each new target required substantial protein design and validation. Cong et al. helped establish a different paradigm: genomic targeting could be specified by a short RNA sequence while the Cas9 protein remained essentially reusable. By showing RNA-guided cleavage at endogenous human and mouse loci, the paper made genome engineering look less like bespoke molecular construction and more like a programmable platform.

Its most important contribution was not merely that CRISPR/Cas9 could cut DNA, but that it could be adapted into a flexible engineering system. The authors demonstrated editing in mammalian cells, conversion of Cas9 into a nickase to bias repair toward homology-directed outcomes with reduced mutagenic activity, and multiplex targeting by encoding multiple guides in one CRISPR array. Together, these features made it plausible to edit, perturb, or repair several genomic sites in parallel, greatly lowering the practical barrier to functional genomics.

The paper appeared at the moment when CRISPR was shifting from a bacterial immune mechanism into a general genome-editing technology. Alongside contemporaneous 2013 demonstrations, it helped launch the rapid expansion of CRISPR-based methods: genome-wide knockout and activation screens, disease-model generation, pooled perturbation experiments, base and prime editing, epigenome editing, and therapeutic editing strategies. Later tools often changed the enzyme, repair chemistry, delivery method, or regulatory architecture, but they inherited the central insight validated here: genome engineering could be made scalable by separating the targeting logic into easily designed guide RNAs.

## Abstract

Functional elucidation of causal genetic variants and elements requires precise genome editing technologies. The type II prokaryotic CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats)/Cas adaptive immune system has been shown to facilitate RNA-guided site-specific DNA cleavage. We engineered two different type II CRISPR/Cas systems and demonstrate that Cas9 nucleases can be directed by short RNAs to induce precise cleavage at endogenous genomic loci in human and mouse cells. Cas9 can also be converted into a nicking enzyme to facilitate homology-directed repair with minimal mutagenic activity. Lastly, multiple guide sequences can be encoded into a single CRISPR array to enable simultaneous editing of several sites within the mammalian genome, demonstrating easy programmability and wide applicability of the RNA-guided nuclease technology.
