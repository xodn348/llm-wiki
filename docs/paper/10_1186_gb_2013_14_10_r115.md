# DNA methylation age of human tissues and cell types

## Why this mattered

Horvath’s 2013 paper turned DNA methylation from a catalog of age-associated molecular changes into a quantitative biological clock. Its central shift was not merely that some CpG sites correlated with age, but that a single 353-CpG predictor trained across thousands of samples could estimate chronological age across many human tissues and cell types. That made “biological age” operational: researchers could assign a reproducible methylation age to blood, brain, liver, cancer tissue, cultured cells, stem cells, and even chimpanzee tissues, then ask where methylation age diverged from calendar age.

This mattered because it created a new measurable phenotype: epigenetic age acceleration. Aging research had long relied on organism-level outcomes, tissue pathology, or narrow biomarkers, but Horvath’s clock made it possible to compare aging rates across tissues, diseases, genotypes, exposures, and interventions using a common molecular scale. The finding that embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells had near-zero methylation age also linked the clock to developmental state and cellular reprogramming, while the strong age acceleration observed across cancers gave cancer biology a new way to quantify epigenetic dysregulation.

The paper became a foundation for later epigenetic clocks and “biological age” biomarkers, including clocks trained to predict mortality, morbidity, immune aging, inflammation, and lifestyle or environmental risk. It also helped make aging-intervention studies more experimentally tractable: instead of waiting for lifespan or late disease outcomes, researchers could test whether interventions shift molecular aging signatures. Subsequent clocks refined the target, but Horvath’s multi-tissue clock supplied the paradigm: aging could be read, compared, and statistically modeled from the epigenome across much of the human body.

## Abstract

Abstract Background It is not yet known whether DNA methylation levels can be used to accurately predict age across a broad spectrum of human tissues and cell types, nor whether the resulting age prediction is a biologically meaningful measure. Results I developed a multi-tissue predictor of age that allows one to estimate the DNA methylation age of most tissues and cell types. The predictor, which is freely available, was developed using 8,000 samples from 82 Illumina DNA methylation array datasets, encompassing 51 healthy tissues and cell types. I found that DNA methylation age has the following properties: first, it is close to zero for embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells; second, it correlates with cell passage number; third, it gives rise to a highly heritable measure of age acceleration; and, fourth, it is applicable to chimpanzee tissues. Analysis of 6,000 cancer samples from 32 datasets showed that all of the considered 20 cancer types exhibit significant age acceleration, with an average of 36 years. Low age-acceleration of cancer tissue is associated with a high number of somatic mutations and TP53 mutations, while mutations in steroid receptors greatly accelerate DNA methylation age in breast cancer. Finally, I characterize the 353 CpG sites that together form an aging clock in terms of chromatin states and tissue variance. Conclusions I propose that DNA methylation age measures the cumulative effect of an epigenetic maintenance system. This novel epigenetic clock can be used to address a host of questions in developmental biology, cancer and aging research.
