# The Determination of Pore Volume and Area Distributions in Porous Substances. I. Computations from Nitrogen Isotherms

## Why this mattered

Before Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda’s [1951 paper](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/ja01145a126), gas adsorption measurements could indicate that a solid was porous, but converting a nitrogen isotherm into a quantitative distribution of pore volumes and surface areas was not yet a routine analytical operation. The BJH method changed that by linking capillary condensation, the Kelvin equation, and adsorbed-film corrections into a practical computation. It made the internal architecture of mesoporous solids experimentally legible from a standard nitrogen sorption experiment.

The paradigm shift was methodological: pore structure became something that could be tabulated, compared, optimized, and engineered rather than inferred qualitatively. This was especially important for catalysts, adsorbents, ceramics, carbons, and later ordered mesoporous materials, where performance depends not only on total surface area but on how pore volume is distributed across sizes. The method helped make “pore size distribution” a standard materials descriptor, alongside BET surface area, in laboratories studying heterogeneous catalysis, separations, and porous inorganic solids.

Its assumptions were simplified, especially the treatment of pores as idealized geometries and the reliance on classical capillary condensation, so later approaches such as density-functional-theory-based adsorption models refined or replaced BJH for many systems. But those later advances inherited the problem that Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda made central: extracting physically meaningful pore architecture from adsorption isotherms. Its lasting importance lies less in being the final theory of porous materials than in establishing a calculable bridge between adsorption data and nanoscale structure.
