# Physics-informed neural networks: A deep learning framework for solving forward and inverse problems involving nonlinear partial differential equations

## Why this mattered

Raissi, Perdikaris, and Karniadakis’s paper mattered because it made a simple but powerful reframing widely usable: a neural network could be trained not only to fit observations, but also to satisfy a governing differential equation through its loss function. This moved deep learning for scientific computing away from being merely a surrogate trained on simulation data and toward a method that could embed known physical structure directly into optimization. The result was especially important for regimes where data are sparse, noisy, or expensive, because the PDE residual, boundary conditions, and initial conditions could act as a form of scientific regularization.

The paradigm shift was that forward solution, inverse parameter estimation, and model discovery could be treated in one differentiable framework. Instead of building separate numerical solvers, adjoint methods, and inverse-problem pipelines, PINNs used automatic differentiation to connect observations, unknown coefficients, and nonlinear PDE constraints inside a single trainable model. This did not replace classical finite element, finite volume, or spectral methods, but it opened a new computational design space: mesh-free approximation, data assimilation from partial measurements, and inverse inference in settings where conventional discretization or sensor coverage was difficult.

Its influence is visible in the subsequent wave of scientific machine learning: variational PINNs, Bayesian PINNs, domain-decomposed and adaptive PINNs, operator-learning hybrids such as physics-informed DeepONets, and applications across fluids, materials, geophysics, biology, and control. Later work also clarified the limitations of the original formulation, including optimization stiffness, poor scaling to multiscale dynamics, and difficulty with sharp gradients or chaotic systems. Even those critiques underscored the paper’s importance: it gave the field a common language and benchmark for combining mechanistic equations with neural networks, making “physics-informed” learning a central paradigm rather than a niche technique.
