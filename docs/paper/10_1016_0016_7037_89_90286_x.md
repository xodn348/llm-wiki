# Abundances of the elements: Meteoritic and solar

## Why this mattered

Anders and Grevesse did not introduce a new physical law; they made a shared abundance scale reliable enough to become infrastructure. By critically combining CI chondrite measurements with solar photospheric and coronal data, they turned “solar-system composition” into a standardized reference table, with stated uncertainties and explicit exceptions. The key shift was methodological: meteorites and the Sun could be treated as mutually checking archives of the primordial solar nebula, rather than as separate abundance traditions.

That made many later comparisons quantitative. Planetary scientists could normalize rocks, meteorites, and differentiated bodies to CI abundances; astrophysicists could use a common solar mixture for stellar models, opacity calculations, nucleosynthesis yields, and Galactic chemical-evolution work. The paper’s conclusion that most well-determined meteoritic and solar abundances agreed closely, while a few elements such as Fe, Mn, Ge, Pb, and W remained discrepant, gave researchers both a baseline and a map of where better measurements mattered.

Its influence is also visible in later revisions of the solar abundance scale. The widely used Anders-Grevesse composition became the benchmark against which 1990s and 2000s updates were measured, including the lower C, N, O, and Ne abundances that helped create the modern “solar abundance problem” in helioseismology. In that sense, the paper mattered because it made the chemical composition of the Solar System a precise, portable standard: a table that could be challenged, refined, and used to connect meteorites, the Sun, stellar evolution, and planet formation.
