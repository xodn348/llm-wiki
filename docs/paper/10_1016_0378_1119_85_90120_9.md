# Improved M13 phage cloning vectors and host strains: nucleotide sequences of the M13mpl8 and pUC19 vectors

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it turned molecular cloning from a bespoke construction problem into a standardized, sequence-defined workflow. Yanisch-Perron, Vieira, and Messing did not merely report another vector: they provided improved M13 and pUC systems, compatible host strains such as JM109/JM110, and complete nucleotide sequence information for M13mp18 and pUC19, making restriction maps, primer sites, blue-white screening, and insert recovery predictable rather than empirical ([Gene, 1985](https://gwern.net/doc/genetics/1985-yanischperron.pdf); [Addgene record](https://www.addgene.org/browse/article/7543/)). The practical shift was that ordinary laboratories could clone, screen, propagate, sequence, and manipulate DNA fragments with a compact, high-copy, well-characterized toolkit.

Its deeper importance was infrastructural. M13 vectors made single-stranded DNA templates and universal-primer sequencing routine; pUC19 made high-copy plasmid cloning with lacZα disruption and a dense multiple cloning site a default laboratory idiom. Together with matched host strains engineered to reduce restriction, recombination, methylation, and background problems, the system lowered the friction of gene isolation, deletion mapping, site-directed mutagenesis, and Sanger-era sequencing. Many later breakthroughs in genomics, recombinant protein engineering, PCR-era subcloning, and synthetic biology depended less on this specific plasmid alone than on the paradigm it exemplified: small, fully mapped, modular DNA vehicles that made genetic construction reproducible, teachable, and portable across laboratories.
