# A Quantum Theory of the Scattering of X-rays by Light Elements

## Why this mattered

Compton’s paper mattered because it turned X-ray scattering into a direct test of whether light quanta carried momentum, not only energy. Classical wave theory could describe Thomson scattering without changing the radiation’s wavelength, but Compton showed that for light elements the scattered wavelength should increase by an angle-dependent amount, independent of the incident wavelength: the Compton shift. By treating the collision as one between an X-ray quantum and an electron, with conservation of energy and momentum, the paper made the photon a mechanically intelligible object rather than just a bookkeeping device for emission and absorption.

The result sharply strengthened the case for wave-particle duality. Einstein had argued in 1905 that light behaved in localized quanta in the photoelectric effect, but Compton’s experiment-theory agreement showed that even freely propagating radiation could exchange momentum in particle-like collisions. This helped move quantum theory beyond Planck’s oscillator hypothesis and Einstein’s light quantum into a broader physical framework in which radiation and matter both had dual aspects.

The paper also opened practical and conceptual paths for later physics. Recoil electrons, high-energy photon scattering, and wavelength-shift measurements became standard tools in atomic, nuclear, and particle physics. Historically, Compton scattering became one of the decisive empirical pillars behind the photon concept, feeding into the rapid consolidation of quantum mechanics in the mid-1920s and later into relativistic quantum theories of light-matter interaction, including quantum electrodynamics.

## Abstract

A quantum theory of the scattering of X-rays and $\ensuremath{\gamma}$-rays by light elements.---The hypothesis is suggested that when an X-ray quantum is scattered it spends all of its energy and momentum upon some particular electron. This electron in turn scatters the ray in some definite direction. The change in momentum of the X-ray quantum due to the change in its direction of propagation results in a recoil of the scattering electron. The energy in the scattered quantum is thus less than the energy in the primary quantum by the kinetic energy of recoil of the scattering electron. The corresponding increase in the wave-length of the scattered beam is ${\ensuremath{\lambda}}_{\ensuremath{\theta}}\ensuremath{-}{\ensuremath{\lambda}}_{0}=(\frac{2h}{\mathrm{mc}}) {sin}^{2} \frac{1}{2}\ensuremath{\theta}=0.0484 {sin}^{2} \frac{1}{2}\ensuremath{\theta}$, where $h$ is the Planck constant, $m$ is the mass of the scattering electron, $c$ is the velocity of light, and $\ensuremath{\theta}$ is the angle between the incident and the scattered ray. Hence the increase is independent of the wave-length. The distribution of the scattered radiation is found, by an indirect and not quite rigid method, to be concentrated in the forward direction according to a definite law (Eq. 27). The total energy removed from the primary beam comes out less than that given by the classical Thomson theory in the ratio $\frac{1}{(1+2\ensuremath{\alpha})}$, where $\ensuremath{\alpha}=\frac{h}{\mathrm{mc}{\ensuremath{\lambda}}_{0}}=\frac{0.0242}{{\ensuremath{\lambda}}_{0}}$. Of this energy a fraction $\frac{(1+\ensuremath{\alpha})}{(1+2\ensuremath{\alpha})}$ reappears as scattered radiation, while the remainder is truly absorbed and transformed into kinetic energy of recoil of the scattering electrons. Hence, if ${\ensuremath{\sigma}}_{0}$ is the scattering absorption coefficient according to the classical theory, the coefficient according to this theory is $\ensuremath{\sigma}=\frac{{\ensuremath{\sigma}}_{0}}{(1+2\ensuremath{\alpha})}={\ensuremath{\sigma}}_{s}+{\ensuremath{\sigma}}_{a}$, where ${\ensuremath{\sigma}}_{s}$ is the true scattering coefficient [$\frac{(1+\ensuremath{\alpha})\ensuremath{\sigma}}{{(1+2\ensuremath{\alpha})}^{2}}$], and ${\ensuremath{\sigma}}_{a}$ is the coefficient of absorption due to scattering [$\frac{\ensuremath{\alpha}\ensuremath{\sigma}}{{(1+2\ensuremath{\alpha})}^{2}}$]. Unpublished experimental results are given which show that for graphite and the Mo-K radiation the scattered radiation is longer than the primary, the observed difference (${\ensuremath{\lambda}}_{\frac{\ensuremath{\pi}}{2}}\ensuremath{-}{\ensuremath{\lambda}}_{0}=.022$) being close to the computed value.024. In the case of scattered $\ensuremath{\gamma}$-rays, the wave-length has been found to vary with $\ensuremath{\theta}$ in agreement with the theory, increasing from.022 A (primary) to.068 A ($\ensuremath{\theta}={135}^{\ensuremath{\circ}}$). Also the velocity of secondary $\ensuremath{\beta}$-rays excited in light elements by $\ensuremath{\gamma}$-rays agrees with the suggestion that they are recoil electrons. As for the predicted variation of absorption with $\ensuremath{\lambda}$, Hewlett's results for carbon for wave-lengths below 0.5 A are in excellent agreement with this theory; also the predicted concentration in the forward direction is shown to be in agreement with the experimental results, both for X-rays and $\ensuremath{\gamma}$-rays. This remarkable agreement between experiment and theory indicates clearly that scattering is a quantum phenomenon and can be explained without introducing any new hypothesis as to the size of the electron or any new constants; also that a radiation quantum carries with it momentum as well as energy.
