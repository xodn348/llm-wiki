# The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (beta version)

## Why this mattered

ICHD-3 beta mattered because it made headache medicine more reproducible. Headache disorders are common, disabling, and often symptom-defined rather than biomarker-defined; without shared diagnostic rules, clinical trials, epidemiology, and routine care could not reliably be compared. The 2013 beta edition updated the International Headache Society framework into a detailed, testable classification for migraine, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, secondary headaches, cranial neuropathies, and facial pains, while explicitly inviting field testing before the final edition.

Its paradigm shift was not a single biological discovery but a standard-setting move: it treated headache diagnoses as operational entities with criteria that could travel across clinics, countries, trials, and health systems. That made it newly possible to enroll more comparable patient populations, measure burden more consistently, separate primary from secondary headache syndromes with greater precision, and align headache classification with the developing ICD-11 framework.

The downstream importance was substantial. The beta classification became the working language for much of the next decade’s migraine and headache research, including studies of chronic migraine, medication-overuse headache, trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, and rare secondary headache disorders. It also helped create the diagnostic stability needed for later therapeutic breakthroughs, especially targeted migraine trials such as CGRP-pathway monoclonal antibodies and gepants, where precise case definitions were essential for regulatory trials, meta-analyses, and translation into clinical practice.
