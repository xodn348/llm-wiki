# Theory of Superconductivity

## Why this mattered

Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer’s 1957 paper turned superconductivity from a collection of striking phenomenological facts into a microscopic quantum theory. Before it, the Meissner effect, zero resistance, isotope effect, energy gap, and thermodynamic transition were known but not unified from first principles. The BCS theory supplied the missing mechanism: an effective attraction between electrons mediated by lattice vibrations can overcome screened Coulomb repulsion near the Fermi surface, producing a many-electron ground state built from correlated pairs of opposite spin and momentum. The central shift was conceptual as much as technical: superconductivity was no longer an exotic perfect-conductor state, but a coherent macroscopic quantum phase of the electronic system.

The paper made previously disconnected measurements calculable within one framework. It derived an energy gap, a second-order phase transition, the temperature dependence of specific heat and penetration depth, and a microscopic basis for the isotope effect. It also explained why superconductivity is robustly collective: the relevant state is not a gas of independent electrons, but a paired condensate whose excitations differ qualitatively from those of the normal metal. This gave experimentalists precise targets and gave theorists a controlled model in which broken symmetry, quasiparticles, and collective quantum order could be treated quantitatively.

Its influence extended well beyond conventional superconductors. BCS theory became a template for modern many-body physics: spontaneous symmetry breaking in Fermi systems, Nambu-Gor’kov methods, Bogoliubov quasiparticles, and pairing instabilities in systems ranging from superfluid helium-3 and ultracold atomic gases to nuclear matter and dense astrophysical matter. Later breakthroughs, including Josephson tunneling, flux quantization, Ginzburg-Landau microscopic derivations, and the search for unconventional and high-temperature superconductivity, all developed in dialogue with the BCS framework, whether by extending it, modifying its pairing mechanism, or identifying where its assumptions fail.

## Abstract

A theory of superconductivity is presented, based on the fact that the interaction between electrons resulting from virtual exchange of phonons is attractive when the energy difference between the electrons states involved is less than the phonon energy, $\ensuremath{\hbar}\ensuremath{\omega}$. It is favorable to form a superconducting phase when this attractive interaction dominates the repulsive screened Coulomb interaction. The normal phase is described by the Bloch individual-particle model. The ground state of a superconductor, formed from a linear combination of normal state configurations in which electrons are virtually excited in pairs of opposite spin and momentum, is lower in energy than the normal state by amount proportional to an average ${(\ensuremath{\hbar}\ensuremath{\omega})}^{2}$, consistent with the isotope effect. A mutually orthogonal set of excited states in one-to-one correspondence with those of the normal phase is obtained by specifying occupation of certain Bloch states and by using the rest to form a linear combination of virtual pair configurations. The theory yields a second-order phase transition and a Meissner effect in the form suggested by Pippard. Calculated values of specific heats and penetration depths and their temperature variation are in good agreement with experiment. There is an energy gap for individual-particle excitations which decreases from about $3.5k{T}_{c}$ at $T=0\ifmmode^\circ\else\textdegree\fi{}$K to zero at ${T}_{c}$. Tables of matrix elements of single-particle operators between the excited-state superconducting wave functions, useful for perturbation expansions and calculations of transition probabilities, are given.
