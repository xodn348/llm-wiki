# Enzymatic Amplification of β-Globin Genomic Sequences and Restriction Site Analysis for Diagnosis of Sickle Cell Anemia

## Why this mattered

This paper marked one of the first demonstrations that polymerase chain reaction (PCR) could turn a specific genomic sequence from scarce clinical DNA into an abundant, analyzable target. Before this, detecting a single inherited mutation usually depended on comparatively large DNA samples, Southern blotting, radioactive probes, and slower workflows. Saiki and colleagues showed that primer-directed enzymatic amplification could increase β-globin target copies by roughly 220,000-fold, making it possible to genotype the sickle-cell mutation from much smaller samples in less than a day.

The paradigm shift was not merely faster sickle-cell diagnosis. The paper showed that a defined region of the human genome could be selectively copied, then interrogated with conventional molecular tools such as restriction enzymes and allele-specific probes. That changed the practical unit of genetic analysis: researchers no longer had to begin with abundant DNA or clone a fragment before studying it. A chosen locus could be made experimentally accessible on demand.

This capability became foundational for modern molecular biology. PCR soon enabled rapid genetic testing, prenatal diagnosis, forensic DNA analysis, infectious-disease detection, ancient-DNA studies, mutation screening, and eventually many sequencing workflows. Later breakthroughs, including high-throughput genotyping, pathogen diagnostics, and next-generation sequencing library preparation, all depended in part on the same conceptual move demonstrated here: amplifying a precise nucleic-acid target until it became easy to detect, compare, and interpret.

## Abstract

Two new methods were used to establish a rapid and highly sensitive prenatal diagnostic test for sickle cell anemia. The first involves the primer-mediated enzymatic amplification of specific beta-globin target sequences in genomic DNA, resulting in the exponential increase (220,000 times) of target DNA copies. In the second technique, the presence of the beta A and beta S alleles is determined by restriction endonuclease digestion of an end-labeled oligonucleotide probe hybridized in solution to the amplified beta-globin sequences. The beta-globin genotype can be determined in less than 1 day on samples containing significantly less than 1 microgram of genomic DNA.
