# A method for obtaining digital signatures and public-key cryptosystems

## Why this mattered

Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman made public-key cryptography concrete. Diffie and Hellman had introduced the paradigm in 1976, but RSA gave a practical, elegantly specified construction for both encryption and digital signatures: publish a key, keep a related inverse secret, and rely on the apparent hardness of factoring a large composite number. This changed cryptography from a system organized around shared secrets and secure key couriers into one where strangers could communicate securely using public directories of keys.

The signature half was just as important as the encryption half. RSA showed how a digital message could be bound to an identity in a way that anyone could verify but only the private-key holder could produce. That made cryptographic authentication, nonrepudiation, software distribution, certificate authorities, secure email, and electronic payments conceptually and technically plausible at internet scale. The paper’s examples, “electronic mail” and “electronic funds transfer,” were not incidental applications; they pointed toward the infrastructure of networked trust.

Its deeper legacy is that it helped define modern cryptography as a field built around public problems with hidden trapdoors and explicit computational assumptions. Later systems changed the underlying hardness assumptions, improved efficiency, and refined security definitions, but they inherited the RSA paper’s central architecture: public keys, private keys, signatures, and security reducible to problems believed infeasible at scale. In that sense, RSA became both a working cryptosystem and a template for subsequent breakthroughs in secure protocols, public-key infrastructure, and the cryptographic foundations of the web.

## Abstract

An encryption method is presented with the novel property that publicly revealing an encryption key does not thereby reveal the corresponding decryption key. This has two important consequences: Couriers or other secure means are not needed to transmit keys, since a message can be enciphered using an encryption key publicly revealed by the intended recipient. Only he can decipher the message, since only he knows the corresponding decryption key. A message can be “signed” using a privately held decryption key. Anyone can verify this signature using the corresponding publicly revealed encryption key. Signatures cannot be forged, and a signer cannot later deny the validity of his signature. This has obvious applications in “electronic mail” and “electronic funds transfer” systems. A message is encrypted by representing it as a number M, raising M to a publicly specified power e, and then taking the remainder when the result is divided by the publicly specified product, n , of two large secret prime numbers p and q. Decryption is similar; only a different, secret, power d is used, where e * d = 1(mod (p - 1) * (q - 1)). The security of the system rests in part on the difficulty of factoring the published divisor, n .
