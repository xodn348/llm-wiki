# Molecular cloning of mouse PSP mRNA

## Why this mattered

Madsen and Hjorth’s paper mattered because it converted PSP from a parotid-gland protein phenotype into a defined molecular object. By cloning and sequencing overlapping cDNAs for the dominant parotid mRNA, they established the nucleotide sequence, inferred the secreted-protein architecture including a signal peptide, and supplied probes that could distinguish PSP gene structure and expression. That was the key shift: questions about tissue restriction and coordinated production with salivary amylase could now be asked at the level of mRNA abundance, genomic organization, and regulatory DNA rather than only protein gels or physiology.

The work also made mouse PSP a tractable model for salivary-gland-specific gene regulation. Because PSP and amylase were both abundant and apparently restricted to the parotid gland, the cloned PSP sequence gave investigators a concrete entry point for comparing regulatory logic between two highly expressed secretory genes in the same tissue. Later studies of developmental control, post-transcriptional regulation, PSP promoter/minigene constructs, and salivary-gland expression in transgenic mice depended on exactly this kind of clone: a sequence-verified cDNA tied to the native protein and usable as a molecular handle.

In retrospect, the paper’s importance was enabling rather than singularly transformative. It did not by itself reveal a universal regulatory mechanism, but it helped move salivary biology into the molecular genetics era and anchored what later became the PSP/BPIFA2 lineage of secreted mucosal proteins. That connection linked a highly tissue-specific mouse parotid product to broader questions about epithelial secretion, oral innate defense, and the evolution of BPI-fold-containing proteins.

## Abstract

PSP is the most abundant translation product of mouse parotid glands where its production is co-ordinated with that of salivary amylase. The synthesis of these two proteins apparently is restricted to this tissue. In order to enable us to study common regulatory elements in the genes of the two proteins, double stranded cDNA, synthesized for parotid gland poly (A)+ RNA, was cloned. DNA sequencing of three clones complementary to the most abundant messenger indicated overlap and resulted in a total sequence of 867 nucleotides. Translation of this sequence revealed that at one end the amino acid sequence was the same as the N-terminal sequence of PSP. The sequence contains 60 nucleotides coding for part of or the complete signal peptide, 645 nucleotides coding for the PSP protein, and 162 nucleotides that apparently are not translated. Southern blot analysis suggests a simple structure for the PSP gene in mouse and man.
