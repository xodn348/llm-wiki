# An Analysis of Transformations

## Why this mattered

Box and Cox turned transformation from an informal diagnostic art into an object of statistical inference. Before this paper, analysts commonly used logs, square roots, reciprocals, and related transformations to make residuals look more normal, stabilize variance, or linearize relationships, but the choice was often justified by convention or graphical judgment. The paper’s central move was to treat the transformation parameter itself as something estimable, using likelihood to compare a family of power transformations while fitting the linear model. That made explicit a powerful idea: the scale on which a model is simple is not fixed in advance, but can be learned from the data under stated assumptions.

This mattered because it extended the practical reach of linear modeling without abandoning its inferential machinery. By separating the roles of normality, constant variance, and additivity, Box and Cox gave analysts a disciplined way to ask why a transformation helped, rather than merely whether it improved appearance. The resulting Box-Cox transformation became a standard part of regression diagnostics, experimental analysis, econometrics, biostatistics, and industrial statistics because it provided a bridge between rigid Gaussian linear models and the irregular, skewed, heteroscedastic measurements common in real data.

Its longer-term significance lies in how it anticipated later model-building philosophies. The paper did not replace linear models with a fully new class of flexible models, but it made model form itself partly inferential, a theme that runs through generalized linear models, variance-stabilizing transformations, transformation models, semiparametric regression, and modern preprocessing pipelines. In that sense, its paradigm shift was methodological as much as technical: it showed that assumptions about measurement scale could be estimated, tested, and discussed within the same probabilistic framework as the regression parameters.

## Abstract

Summary In the analysis of data it is often assumed that observations y 1, y 2, …, yn are independently normally distributed with constant variance and with expectations specified by a model linear in a set of parameters θ. In this paper we make the less restrictive assumption that such a normal, homoscedastic, linear model is appropriate after some suitable transformation has been applied to the y's. Inferences about the transformation and about the parameters of the linear model are made by computing the likelihood function and the relevant posterior distribution. The contributions of normality, homoscedasticity and additivity to the transformation are separated. The relation of the present methods to earlier procedures for finding transformations is discussed. The methods are illustrated with examples.
