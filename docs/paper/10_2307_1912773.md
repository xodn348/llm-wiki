# Autoregressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity with Estimates of the Variance of United Kingdom Inflation

## Why this mattered

Engle’s 1982 paper changed econometrics by making volatility itself an object of statistical modeling rather than a nuisance summarized by a constant error variance. In the ARCH framework, a series can be uncorrelated in levels while still exhibiting predictable variation in its conditional variance, so large shocks tend to be followed by periods in which future shocks are more uncertain. This formalized a pattern long observed in macroeconomic and financial data, volatility clustering, within a likelihood-based regression model that could be estimated, tested, and compared using standard econometric tools.

The new possibility was conditional variance forecasting. After ARCH, economists could ask not only what the expected value of inflation, returns, or interest rates was, but how uncertain that forecast was given recent history. Engle’s application to United Kingdom inflation showed that the variance of inflation rose sharply during the turbulent 1970s, giving empirical content to the idea that macroeconomic instability changes through time. The paper also supplied a practical diagnostic: if squared residuals are autocorrelated, the constant-variance assumption is suspect.

Its larger impact was to open the modern econometrics of time-varying risk. Bollerslev’s GARCH model generalized ARCH in 1986 and became a workhorse for financial volatility, option pricing, risk management, and macro-finance. Later stochastic volatility models, realized-volatility methods, volatility spillover models, and Value-at-Risk systems all inherited the central shift Engle made: uncertainty is dynamic, measurable, and forecastable, not merely an error term around the mean.

## Abstract

Traditional econometric models assume a constant one-period forecast variance. To generalize this implausible assumption, a new class of stochastic processes called autoregressive conditional heteroscedastic (ARCH) processes are introduced in this paper. These are mean zero, serially uncorrelated processes with nonconstant variances conditional on the past, but constant unconditional variances. For such processes, the recent past gives information about the one-period forecast variance. A regression model is then introduced with disturbances following an ARCH process. Maximum likelihood estimators are described and a simple scoring iteration formulated. Ordinary least squares maintains its optimality properties in this set-up, but maximum likelihood is more efficient. The relative efficiency is calculated and can be infinite. To test whether the disturbances follow an ARCH process, the Lagrange multiplier procedure is employed. The test is based simply on the autocorrelation of the squared OLS residuals. This model is used to estimate the means and variances of inflation in the U.K. The ARCH effect is found to be significant and the estimated variances increase substantially during the chaotic seventies.
