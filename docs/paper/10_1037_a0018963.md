# Construal-level theory of psychological distance.

## Why this mattered

Trope and Liberman’s 2010 review mattered because it unified several phenomena that had often been studied separately: thinking about the future, distant places, other people, unlikely events, and counterfactual possibilities. The paper’s central move was to treat these as dimensions of a single construct, *psychological distance*, all anchored in the self, here, and now. That reframing made abstraction itself a general cognitive mechanism rather than a topic-specific effect: distant objects are represented in terms of gist, goals, and invariant features, while near objects are represented in terms of contextual, concrete, and incidental details.

This shifted research from asking only how people think about time, space, probability, or social perspective in isolation to asking how movement along any distance dimension changes prediction, preference, moral judgment, planning, and action. It made newly possible a common experimental language for linking diverse effects: why future choices emphasize desirability over feasibility, why socially distant others are stereotyped or moralized more abstractly, why unlikely events are represented schematically, and why counterfactuals can reshape meaning by moving experience away from the immediate present.

The paper also helped set the agenda for later work on abstraction in judgment and decision-making, self-control, consumer behavior, moral psychology, political cognition, and communication. Its influence came not from introducing every component de novo, but from showing that a broad family of cognitive and motivational effects could be explained by a shared architecture of distance and construal. Subsequent breakthroughs could therefore treat psychological distance as a design variable: changing perceived distance could systematically alter how people imagine, evaluate, and act on the same object or event.

## Abstract

People are capable of thinking about the future, the past, remote locations, another person's perspective, and counterfactual alternatives. Without denying the uniqueness of each process, it is proposed that they constitute different forms of traversing psychological distance. Psychological distance is egocentric: Its reference point is the self in the here and now, and the different ways in which an object might be removed from that point-in time, in space, in social distance, and in hypotheticality-constitute different distance dimensions. Transcending the self in the here and now entails mental construal, and the farther removed an object is from direct experience, the higher (more abstract) the level of construal of that object. Supporting this analysis, research shows (a) that the various distances are cognitively related to each other, (b) that they similarly influence and are influenced by level of mental construal, and (c) that they similarly affect prediction, preference, and action.
