# Kinetics of Phase Change. I General Theory

## Why this mattered

Avrami’s paper mattered because it turned phase transformation from a largely descriptive subject into a quantitative kinetic theory. Before this work, transformations such as crystallization, precipitation, recrystallization, and solid-state phase change were often discussed in terms of empirical time-temperature curves. Avrami instead modeled transformation as the coupled result of nucleation, growth, and impingement: new-phase grains begin from activated “germ nuclei,” grow through the parent phase, and progressively consume both untransformed material and inactive nuclei. This made the transformed fraction a calculable function of time rather than just an observed sigmoid curve.

The paradigm shift was the separation between real transformed volume and “extended” growth that would occur if growing regions did not overlap. That idea made impingement mathematically tractable and led to the family of relations now associated with Johnson-Mehl-Avrami-Kolmogorov kinetics, often summarized by the Avrami equation. After this paper and its sequels, experimentalists could infer mechanisms from transformation curves: whether nucleation was site-saturated or continuing, whether growth was effectively one-, two-, or three-dimensional, and whether a system lay in an isokinetic regime where the same kinetic form persisted across temperature or composition.

Its influence reached well beyond the particular assumptions of the 1939 paper. The framework became a standard language for interpreting crystallization in metals, polymers, glasses, thin films, and biological or soft-matter systems where a new phase emerges by nucleation and growth. Later breakthroughs in materials processing, heat treatment, precipitation hardening, polymer crystallization, and transformation diagrams all depended on the same conceptual move: microstructural evolution could be linked to measurable macroscopic kinetics through a small number of physically interpretable parameters.

## Abstract

The theory of the kinetics of phase change is developed with the experimentally supported assumptions that the new phase is nucleated by germ nuclei which already exist in the old phase, and whose number can be altered by previous treatment. The density of germ nuclei diminishes through activation of some of them to become growth nuclei for grains of the new phase, and ingestion of others by these growing grains. The quantitative relations between the density of germ nuclei, growth nuclei, and transformed volume are derived and expressed in terms of a characteristic time scale for any given substance and process. The geometry and kinetics of a crystal aggregate are studied from this point of view, and it is shown that there is strong evidence of the existence, for any given substance, of an isokinetic range of temperatures and concentrations in which the characteristic kinetics of phase change remains the same. The determination of phase reaction kinetics is shown to depend upon the solution of a functional equation of a certain type. Some of the general properties of temperature-time and transformation-time curves, respectively, are described and explained.
