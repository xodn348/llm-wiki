# Quantum Computing in the NISQ era and beyond

## Why this mattered

Preskill’s paper mattered because it gave the field a durable name and conceptual frame for the awkward but crucial interval between laboratory demonstrations and fully fault-tolerant quantum computers. “NISQ” clarified that near-term machines with tens to hundreds of noisy qubits should not be judged by the standards of future error-corrected devices, nor dismissed as mere prototypes. The paper reframed them as a distinct technological regime: large enough to probe quantum dynamics beyond straightforward classical simulation in some settings, but too noisy and shallow to run long algorithms such as Shor’s factoring algorithm at useful scale.

That framing changed what became scientifically legitimate to pursue. After this paper, near-term quantum research increasingly centered on questions suited to noisy devices: quantum supremacy or quantum advantage experiments, variational quantum algorithms, analog and digital quantum simulation, error mitigation, benchmarking, and hardware-aware circuit design. It also sharpened expectations. Preskill argued that NISQ devices might become valuable scientific instruments, especially for many-body physics, while warning that practical commercial impact was uncertain and that noise would remain the central obstacle.

The paper’s influence is visible in subsequent milestones and debates. Google’s 2019 random-circuit sampling experiment, later demonstrations of larger superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom, and photonic systems, and the rapid growth of error-mitigation and quantum-simulation programs all fit the NISQ framework. At the same time, the paper helped keep attention on the longer arc: NISQ was not the endpoint, but a bridge toward fault-tolerant quantum computing. Its paradigm shift was therefore partly technical and partly sociological: it gave researchers, funders, and engineers a shared map for what near-term quantum computers could plausibly teach us, and what they could not yet do.

## Abstract

Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) technology will be available in the near future. Quantum computers with 50-100 qubits may be able to perform tasks which surpass the capabilities of today's classical digital computers, but noise in quantum gates will limit the size of quantum circuits that can be executed reliably. NISQ devices will be useful tools for exploring many-body quantum physics, and may have other useful applications, but the 100-qubit quantum computer will not change the world right away - we should regard it as a significant step toward the more powerful quantum technologies of the future. Quantum technologists should continue to strive for more accurate quantum gates and, eventually, fully fault-tolerant quantum computing.
