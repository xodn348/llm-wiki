# Dynamic mode decomposition of numerical and experimental data

## Why this mattered

Schmid’s 2010 paper mattered because it turned modal analysis from something primarily tied to known governing equations, linearized operators, or carefully posed stability problems into a practical data-driven procedure for extracting coherent spatiotemporal structures from snapshots. In fluid mechanics, this was a significant shift: numerical simulations and experiments were producing large time-resolved datasets, but the dominant tools either emphasized energetic structures, as in proper orthogonal decomposition, or required an explicit linear operator. Dynamic mode decomposition instead associated spatial modes with single frequencies and growth or decay rates, making it possible to identify dynamically meaningful patterns directly from measured or simulated flow fields.

The paper also made a bridge between classical stability theory and modern data analysis. By interpreting DMD modes as a generalization of global stability modes, Schmid gave experimental and computational researchers a way to study instability mechanisms, oscillatory wakes, shear-layer dynamics, and localized flow phenomena without first deriving or discretizing the full evolution operator. This was especially important for complex flows where the equations may be known but the relevant reduced dynamics are hidden in high-dimensional data, or where only measurements are available.

Its longer-term importance was that DMD became one of the foundational methods of equation-free reduced-order modeling and data-driven dynamical systems. Later work connected DMD more explicitly to Koopman operator theory, extended it with sparsity, control inputs, streaming updates, noise-aware variants, and nonlinear observables, and applied it far beyond fluid mechanics. The paper helped establish the now-standard idea that large scientific datasets can be decomposed not only by variance or correlation, but by approximating the dynamics that generated them.

## Abstract

The description of coherent features of fluid flow is essential to our understanding of fluid-dynamical and transport processes. A method is introduced that is able to extract dynamic information from flow fields that are either generated by a (direct) numerical simulation or visualized/measured in a physical experiment. The extracted dynamic modes, which can be interpreted as a generalization of global stability modes, can be used to describe the underlying physical mechanisms captured in the data sequence or to project large-scale problems onto a dynamical system of significantly fewer degrees of freedom. The concentration on subdomains of the flow field where relevant dynamics is expected allows the dissection of a complex flow into regions of localized instability phenomena and further illustrates the flexibility of the method, as does the description of the dynamics within a spatial framework. Demonstrations of the method are presented consisting of a plane channel flow, flow over a two-dimensional cavity, wake flow behind a flexible membrane and a jet passing between two cylinders.
