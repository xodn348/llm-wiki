# The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money.

## Why this mattered

*The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* mattered because it broke with the classical view that economies naturally tend toward full employment if wages and prices adjust. Keynes argued that aggregate demand could settle at a level too low to employ all available labor, making prolonged involuntary unemployment not an anomaly but a possible equilibrium outcome. That shift reframed depression-era unemployment from a failure of workers, wages, or temporary market friction into a systemic macroeconomic problem.

The work made modern demand-management policy intellectually possible. If output and employment depend on effective demand, then fiscal policy, public investment, and monetary conditions become tools for stabilizing the economy rather than external distortions of an otherwise self-correcting system. This helped establish macroeconomics as a distinct field with its own objects of analysis: consumption, investment, liquidity preference, expectations, the multiplier, and the relation between interest rates and employment.

Its later influence ran through both adoption and criticism. Postwar Keynesian economics, national income accounting, stabilization policy, and large-scale macroeconomic modeling all drew on its framework, while monetarism, new classical macroeconomics, and new Keynesian economics developed partly by challenging, formalizing, or modifying Keynes’s claims. The book’s lasting importance is that it changed the central question of economic theory: not only how markets allocate resources, but why entire economies can fail to use them.
