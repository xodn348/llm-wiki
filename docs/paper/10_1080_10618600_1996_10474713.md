# R: A Language for Data Analysis and Graphics

## Why this mattered

Ihaka and Gentleman’s 1996 paper mattered because it reframed statistical software as a programmable, portable, extensible language environment rather than a closed collection of procedures. R deliberately joined the statistical orientation of S with Scheme-influenced lexical scoping, making it natural to express analyses as functions, compose models and graphics interactively, and carry code across systems. The paper’s technical claims about portability, memory management, efficiency, and scoping were not incidental implementation details; they were what made statistical computing more like open scientific infrastructure.

The paradigm shift was that new statistical methods could be distributed as code in the same language analysts used every day. After R, a method did not have to wait for incorporation into commercial software to become usable: researchers could publish packages, examples, simulations, diagnostics, and graphics together. That helped turn statistics, bioinformatics, econometrics, ecology, epidemiology, and later data science into fields where computational methods circulated through reusable software artifacts, not just equations and prose.

Its later importance is visible in ecosystems that built directly on this model: CRAN, Bioconductor, reproducible-analysis tools such as R Markdown, and major graphics and data-analysis frameworks such as ggplot2 and the tidyverse. Those later breakthroughs were not all in the 1996 paper, but they depended on the paper’s core bet: a statistical language with strong interactive graphics, functional programming features, and open portability could become a medium for scientific exchange. In that sense, R helped establish the modern norm that a statistical paper’s impact is amplified when its method is immediately executable, inspectable, and extensible by a community. ## Abstract

Abstract In this article we discuss our experience designing and implementing a statistical computing language. In developing this new language, we sought to combine what we felt were useful features from two existing computer languages. We feel that the new language provides advantages in the areas of portability, computational efficiency, memory management, and scoping.
