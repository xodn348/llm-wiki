# To Err is Human: Building a Safer Health System

## Why this mattered

Hendee’s 2001 article mattered less as a source of new empirical data than as part of the rapid clinical uptake of *To Err is Human*, the Institute of Medicine report that reframed medical injury as a systems problem rather than a matter of individual negligence. Its paradigm shift was to make preventable harm a measurable property of health-care systems: errors could be counted, analyzed, and reduced through redesigned processes, reporting cultures, checklists, team training, device safeguards, and institutional accountability.

For interventional radiology and vascular intervention, this framing was especially consequential because procedural medicine combines complex technology, time pressure, handoffs, imaging interpretation, medication use, and invasive technique. After this shift, safety work could move beyond retrospective blame toward prospective risk reduction: credentialing standards, quality-improvement registries, morbidity review, simulation-based training, timeout procedures, dose monitoring, and structured complication reporting all became more intellectually and institutionally defensible.

The paper also connects to later breakthroughs in patient-safety science: high-reliability organization theory in hospitals, surgical safety checklists, human-factors engineering, computerized order entry, disclosure-and-learning systems, and national quality metrics. Its lasting importance is that it helped normalize the idea that safer care is engineered, not merely exhorted; excellence in medicine would increasingly be judged not only by technical success, but by whether the surrounding system made avoidable harm less likely.
