# Giant Magnetoresistance of (001)Fe/(001)Cr Magnetic Superlattices

## Why this mattered

Baibich et al. showed that magnetoresistance could be engineered as a large, multilayer quantum-transport effect rather than treated as a small material property. In Fe/Cr superlattices grown by molecular-beam epitaxy, they found that a magnetic field could nearly halve the resistivity at low temperature when the Cr spacer was thin. Their interpretation was decisive: electron transport depended on spin-dependent transmission between ferromagnetic Fe layers through nonmagnetic Cr, so the relative magnetic alignment of layers could control electrical resistance.

That result helped establish **spintronics**: devices could use the electron’s spin, not only its charge, as an information-bearing degree of freedom. Before this work, magnetic fields affected resistance in known ways, but the effect was generally too small for a new device paradigm. Giant magnetoresistance made magnetic configuration electrically readable with high contrast, turning layered magnetic materials into practical sensors and memory-relevant structures. It also showed the power of atomically controlled artificial materials: by choosing layer thickness and interfaces, one could design electronic behavior not found in bulk compounds.

The immediate technological consequence was magnetic read heads for hard drives, where GMR enabled much more sensitive detection of tiny magnetic domains and helped drive the rapid increase in storage density in the 1990s. Conceptually, the paper sits at the start of a line leading to spin valves, tunnel magnetoresistance, magnetic random-access memory, and modern spin-dependent transport research. Together with the independent 1988 discovery of GMR in Fe/Cr multilayers by Peter Grünberg’s group, it became the experimental foundation recognized by the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg.

## Abstract

We have studied the magnetoresistance of (001)Fe/(001)Cr superlattices prepared by molecularbeam epitaxy. A huge magnetoresistance is found in superlattices with thin Cr layers: For example, with ${t}_{\mathrm{Cr}}=9$ \AA{}, at $T=4.2$ K, the resistivity is lowered by almost a factor of 2 in a magnetic field of 2 T. We ascribe this giant magnetoresistance to spin-dependent transmission of the conduction electrons between Fe layers through Cr layers.
