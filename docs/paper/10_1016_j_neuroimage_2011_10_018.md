# Spurious but systematic correlations in functional connectivity MRI networks arise from subject motion

## Why this mattered

[Power et al.](https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/sites.wustl.edu/dist/7/1080/files/2017/12/Power-Barnes-Snyder-Schlaggar-Petersen-2012-23f46mt.pdf) changed resting-state fMRI from a field that treated head motion mainly as a nuisance to be minimized into one that had to treat motion as a structured source of false network biology. The crucial point was not simply that movement was bad; it was that small, ordinary movements surviving realignment and motion regression could systematically inflate short-distance correlations and attenuate longer-distance correlations. That meant apparent developmental, aging, or clinical differences in functional networks could be artifacts of who moved more.

The paper made quality control a central analytic object. By proposing frame-by-frame measures of suspect data and demonstrating “scrubbing,” it helped turn resting-state connectomics into a more auditable enterprise: investigators could ask whether a network result survived motion censoring, motion matching, and distance-dependent artifact checks. This was especially important because rs-fcMRI’s appeal lay in studying children, older adults, and clinical populations, precisely the groups likely to differ in motion.

Its longer-term importance was methodological and disciplinary. Later large-scale connectomics, developmental neuroimaging, psychiatric biomarker work, and individual-difference mapping all depended on more rigorous denoising and transparent motion control. The paper did not end the artifact problem, but it reset the burden of proof: functional connectivity findings now had to show that they were not merely reproducing the geometry of head motion.
