# Technical Change and the Aggregate Production Function

## Why this mattered

Solow’s 1957 paper made technical change measurable at the level of the whole economy. Its central move was to separate growth caused by increases in measured inputs, capital and labor, from growth caused by shifts in the production function itself. Under competitive factor pricing and constant returns assumptions, Solow showed that the unexplained residual in aggregate output growth could be interpreted as a measure of technical change. This transformed “technology” from a broad qualitative force into an empirical object: the now-famous Solow residual.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as substantive. Before this paper, economists could discuss productivity and mechanization, but lacked a disciplined accounting framework for asking how much growth came from factor accumulation versus improved efficiency. Solow’s framework made it newly possible to decompose historical growth, compare productivity performance across periods and countries, and treat technological progress as a central variable in macroeconomics rather than a background condition. His finding that a large share of U.S. growth could not be explained by capital deepening alone helped redirect growth theory away from simple accumulation stories.

The paper also set the agenda for later breakthroughs. Growth accounting became a standard empirical tool, feeding into work on productivity measurement, development economics, and business-cycle analysis. At the same time, the residual’s importance exposed a major limitation of the neoclassical model: technical progress was measured but not explained. That gap helped motivate endogenous growth theory, including later models by Romer, Lucas, and others, which tried to explain innovation, knowledge accumulation, human capital, and increasing returns from within the economic system rather than treating technological change as exogenous.
