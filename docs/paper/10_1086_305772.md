# Maps of Dust Infrared Emission for Use in Estimation of Reddening and Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation Foregrounds

## Why this mattered

Before Schlegel, Finkbeiner, and Davis, Galactic dust extinction was a limiting nuisance for precision work over large areas of the sky: astronomers could correct for reddening in particular directions, but there was no high-resolution, full-sky, uniformly calibrated dust column map suitable for survey-scale cosmology. The paper changed that by combining IRAS resolution with COBE/DIRBE calibration, removing zodiacal light and scan artifacts, and using far-infrared color to correct for dust temperature. The resulting “SFD” map turned thermal dust emission into a practical all-sky reddening estimator, especially powerful at high Galactic latitude where extragalactic surveys operate.

Its importance was not just that it made a better dust map, but that it made dust correction routine. Large optical and infrared surveys could now deredden photometry consistently across thousands of square degrees, enabling cleaner galaxy catalogs, quasar selection, stellar population studies, supernova measurements, and large-scale-structure analyses. In CMB work, it also provided a concrete spatial template for Galactic dust foregrounds, helping separate cosmological anisotropy from emission by the Milky Way. That foreground problem became central to later precision cosmology, including WMAP, Planck, and searches for primordial B-mode polarization.

The map was later refined and recalibrated, notably because its reddening estimates have known limitations in high-extinction regions, variable dust properties, and small-scale systematics. But its paradigm shift was durable: it established that far-infrared emission could be processed into a full-sky, survey-ready model of Galactic dust, turning an irregular foreground into a quantified correction layer. For roughly two decades, SFD was the default extinction reference against which newer 2D and 3D dust maps were compared.

## Abstract

We present a full-sky 100 km map that is a reprocessed composite of the COBE/DIRBE and IRAS/ ISSA maps, with the zodiacal foreground and conrmed point sources removed. Before using the ISSA maps, we remove the remaining artifacts from the IRAS scan pattern. Using the DIRBE 100 and 240 km data, we have constructed a map of the dust temperature so that the 100 km map may be converted to a map proportional to dust column density. The dust temperature varies from 17 to 21 K, which is modest but does modify the estimate of the dust column by a factor of 5. The result of these manipulations is a map with DIRBE quality calibration and IRAS resolution. A wealth of lamentary detail is apparent on many di erent scales at all Galactic latitudes. In high-latitude regions, the dust map correlates well with maps of H I emission, but deviations are coherent in the sky and are especially conspicuous in regions of saturation of H I emission toward denser clouds and of formation of in molecular clouds. In contrast, H 2 high-velocity H I clouds are decient in dust emission, as expected.
