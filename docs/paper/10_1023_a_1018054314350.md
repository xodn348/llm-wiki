# Bagging Predictors

## Why this mattered

Breiman’s *Bagging Predictors* mattered because it turned model instability from a weakness into a reusable source of predictive power. The paper showed that for learning methods whose fitted predictors change substantially under small perturbations of the training set, repeatedly resampling the data, fitting many predictors, and aggregating their outputs could reduce variance and improve accuracy. This was a conceptual shift away from treating a single learned model as the final object of interest: the predictor could instead be an ensemble produced by a stochastic procedure.

The immediate importance was practical. Bagging made high-variance methods such as decision trees more reliable without requiring a new domain-specific model or strong distributional assumptions. It gave machine learning a simple, general recipe for stabilizing flexible learners, especially in settings where overfitting and sampling noise limited performance. The paper also helped establish empirical resampling as a central tool in predictive modeling, extending the bootstrap’s role from uncertainty estimation into direct algorithmic improvement.

Its longer-term influence runs through much of modern ensemble learning. Random forests can be understood as a major descendant of bagging: they combine bootstrap aggregation with additional randomization in feature selection to decorrelate trees and further improve performance. More broadly, bagging helped legitimize the idea that many moderately accurate, diverse models can outperform a single carefully chosen model, a principle later echoed across ensemble methods, model averaging, and large-scale predictive systems.
