# Sequential deposition as a route to high-performance perovskite-sensitized solar cells

## Why this mattered

Before this work, organometal halide perovskites had already shown striking photovoltaic promise, but the field was still constrained by unreliable film formation: one-step deposition could produce uncontrolled precipitation, large morphological variation, and wide device-to-device performance spreads. [Burschka et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12340) shifted attention from perovskite merely as a new light absorber to perovskite as a processable semiconductor whose performance depended critically on crystallization control. By first loading PbI₂ into nanoporous TiO₂ and then converting it with CH₃NH₃I, the paper made morphology a controllable variable and reported solid-state mesoscopic cells with reproducible performance and roughly 15% power conversion efficiency.

That mattered because it changed what researchers could reasonably attempt. Perovskite photovoltaics no longer looked like a fragile extension of dye-sensitized solar cells; they became a rapidly optimizable thin-film materials platform. The sequential route gave laboratories a practical way to decouple precursor infiltration, conversion, and film growth, making it easier to study interfaces, transport layers, additives, annealing, and compositional tuning without every experiment being dominated by stochastic film quality.

Many later breakthroughs did not simply copy this exact mesoporous architecture, but they inherited its central lesson: high-efficiency perovskite solar cells are as much a triumph of deposition chemistry and crystallization engineering as of band-gap selection. The same logic underlies later advances in vapor-deposited, planar, mixed-halide, mixed-cation, passivated, and tandem perovskite devices. In that sense, the paper helped move the field from proof-of-concept sensitizers toward a reproducible device-engineering discipline.
