# The Cancer Genome Atlas Pan-Cancer analysis project

## Why this mattered

Before this project, cancer genomics was still largely organized around organ sites: breast cancer, lung cancer, glioblastoma, ovarian cancer. The Pan-Cancer initiative reframed TCGA’s growing datasets as a cross-tumor resource, asking whether molecular features could define clinically meaningful relationships that cut across tissue boundaries. That was the paradigm shift: cancer classification could be driven not only by where a tumor arose, but by shared DNA, RNA, protein, and epigenetic aberrations.

What became newly possible was systematic comparison across tumor lineages at scale. Instead of treating each TCGA disease project as a separate atlas, the paper established the rationale for harmonized, multi-platform analysis across the first 12 tumor types. This made it possible to identify recurrent pathways, subtype similarities, and candidate therapeutic matches that would be invisible or underpowered within a single cancer type.

Its importance lies less in a single result than in the research program it formalized. Later pan-cancer studies, basket-trial logic, molecular tumor boards, and tissue-agnostic approvals all depended on the same conceptual move: a genomic alteration or pathway state can sometimes be more actionable than the tumor’s anatomical label. This paper helped make that shift explicit within one of the central public cancer-genomics efforts of the era.

## Abstract

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Research Network has profiled and analyzed large numbers of human tumors to discover molecular aberrations at the DNA, RNA, protein and epigenetic levels. The resulting rich data provide a major opportunity to develop an integrated picture of commonalities, differences and emergent themes across tumor lineages. The Pan-Cancer initiative compares the first 12 tumor types profiled by TCGA. Analysis of the molecular aberrations and their functional roles across tumor types will teach us how to extend therapies effective in one cancer type to others with a similar genomic profile.
