# Induced Pluripotent Stem Cell Lines Derived from Human Somatic Cells

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it helped establish that human cellular identity was not fixed in the way developmental biology had long assumed. Building on somatic cell nuclear transfer and the 2006 mouse iPSC work, Yu and colleagues showed that defined transcriptional regulators could convert human somatic cells into pluripotent stem cell lines with core properties of human embryonic stem cells: self-renewal, telomerase activity, normal karyotypes, embryonic-stem-cell marker expression, and differentiation into derivatives of all three germ layers. The key shift was conceptual and practical: pluripotency could be induced in human cells without making or destroying embryos and without requiring an oocyte.

What became newly possible was the systematic creation of patient- or disease-specific pluripotent cell lines. Instead of relying mainly on scarce embryonic stem cell lines or difficult nuclear-transfer approaches, researchers could in principle take an accessible adult cell, reprogram it, and then differentiate it into disease-relevant cell types. That opened a path to human models of disorders that were otherwise hard to study directly, especially neurological, cardiac, metabolic, and developmental diseases. It also gave drug discovery a new substrate: genetically matched human cells carrying a patient’s disease background.

The paper also helped launch the broader iPSC era alongside the contemporaneous 2007 human reprogramming work from Yamanaka’s group. Later breakthroughs refined nearly every technical limitation visible in the abstract: reducing or eliminating integrating viral vectors, improving reprogramming efficiency, directing differentiation into specific mature cell types, building organoids, and combining iPSCs with genome editing. Its lasting importance is that it turned pluripotency from a rare embryological state into an experimentally programmable one, reshaping regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and human developmental biology.

## Abstract

Somatic cell nuclear transfer allows trans-acting factors present in the mammalian oocyte to reprogram somatic cell nuclei to an undifferentiated state. We show that four factors (OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, and LIN28) are sufficient to reprogram human somatic cells to pluripotent stem cells that exhibit the essential characteristics of embryonic stem (ES) cells. These induced pluripotent human stem cells have normal karyotypes, express telomerase activity, express cell surface markers and genes that characterize human ES cells, and maintain the developmental potential to differentiate into advanced derivatives of all three primary germ layers. Such induced pluripotent human cell lines should be useful in the production of new disease models and in drug development, as well as for applications in transplantation medicine, once technical limitations (for example, mutation through viral integration) are eliminated.
