# Observation of a new particle in the search for the Standard Model Higgs boson with the ATLAS detector at the LHC

## Why this mattered

This paper reported ATLAS’s observation of a new boson with a mass near 126 GeV in LHC proton-proton collision data, with local significance reaching the discovery threshold in channels including two photons and four leptons. Its importance was not only that it found a new particle, but that the particle appeared exactly where the Standard Model’s electroweak-symmetry-breaking mechanism required a scalar boson to be visible if that framework was correct. Together with the parallel CMS result, it converted the Higgs boson from a theoretically required but unobserved element of the Standard Model into an experimentally established object.

The paradigm shift was that electroweak symmetry breaking became a precision experimental subject rather than primarily a consistency condition of the theory. After this result, physicists could measure the new particle’s spin, parity, couplings to fermions and vector bosons, production modes, decay rates, and self-interaction prospects, asking whether it was the Standard Model Higgs boson or the first sign of a larger scalar sector. The paper therefore opened a new program: Higgs physics as a central probe of physics beyond the Standard Model.

Subsequent breakthroughs built directly on this foundation: increasingly precise ATLAS and CMS measurements confirmed that the particle’s observed properties are broadly consistent with a Standard Model Higgs boson, while searches for deviations became a major route toward testing supersymmetry, composite Higgs models, extended Higgs sectors, and other new-physics scenarios. The discovery also completed the experimentally observed particle content of the Standard Model, sharpening the remaining open questions rather than ending the field: dark matter, neutrino masses, the hierarchy problem, and the matter-antimatter asymmetry all became more sharply framed against a now-complete electroweak theory.
