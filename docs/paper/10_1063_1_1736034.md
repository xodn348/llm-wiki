# Detailed Balance Limit of Efficiency of<i>p-n</i>Junction Solar Cells

## Why this mattered

Shockley and Queisser turned solar-cell efficiency from an empirical engineering target into a thermodynamic limit problem. By applying detailed balance to an ideal *p-n* junction, they showed that even a perfect single-junction cell under ordinary sunlight could not convert all absorbed photons into electrical work: losses from sub-bandgap transmission, excess photon energy thermalization, and unavoidable radiative recombination set a hard ceiling. The result was not merely the famous ~30% limit near a 1.1 eV band gap under their assumptions, but a new way to ask what “ideal” photovoltaic performance means.

The paper made it possible to separate material imperfection from fundamental loss. After 1961, researchers could evaluate real devices against a benchmark that was independent of any particular semiconductor technology, while also seeing exactly which assumptions would have to be relaxed to exceed it. This framing clarified why improving crystal quality, contacts, and nonradiative recombination mattered for approaching the limit, and why single-junction silicon, gallium arsenide, and later thin-film cells should be judged partly by how close they came to detailed-balance behavior.

Its influence also lies in defining the escape routes. Tandem and multijunction cells, optical concentration, hot-carrier concepts, multiple-exciton generation, and other advanced photovoltaic strategies can be understood as attempts to change the constraints that Shockley and Queisser made explicit, especially the one-band-gap and one-absorber assumptions. In that sense, the paper did for photovoltaics what Carnot-style reasoning did for heat engines: it gave the field a durable theoretical standard, a vocabulary for losses, and a map of where genuinely new device physics would be required.

## Abstract

In order to find an upper theoretical limit for the efficiency of p-n junction solar energy converters, a limiting efficiency, called the detailed balance limit of efficiency, has been calculated for an ideal case in which the only recombination mechanism of hole-electron pairs is radiative as required by the principle of detailed balance. The efficiency is also calculated for the case in which radiative recombination is only a fixed fraction fc of the total recombination, the rest being nonradiative. Efficiencies at the matched loads have been calculated with band gap and fc as parameters, the sun and cell being assumed to be blackbodies with temperatures of 6000°K and 300°K, respectively. The maximum efficiency is found to be 30% for an energy gap of 1.1 ev and fc = 1. Actual junctions do not obey the predicted current-voltage relationship, and reasons for the difference and its relevance to efficiency are discussed.
