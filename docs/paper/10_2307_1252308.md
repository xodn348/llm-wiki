# The Commitment-Trust Theory of Relationship Marketing

## Why this mattered

Morgan and Hunt’s paper mattered because it gave relationship marketing a coherent theoretical core at a moment when marketing was moving beyond discrete transactions. Earlier work had documented channels, services, alliances, and buyer-seller relationships, but the field lacked a parsimonious explanation for why some relational exchanges endured and produced cooperation while others did not. By placing **commitment** and **trust** as key mediating variables, the paper reframed relationship marketing from a loose managerial orientation into a testable theory of exchange behavior.

The shift was paradigmatic because it changed what marketers could model. Instead of treating outcomes such as cooperation, acquiescence, conflict, or propensity to leave as direct products of economic incentives or power, Morgan and Hunt argued that these outcomes operated through relational states. That made it possible to study relationship marketing with the tools of causal modeling: antecedents such as shared values, communication, opportunistic behavior, relationship benefits, and termination costs could be linked to commitment and trust, which in turn explained downstream behavior.

The paper helped anchor later work in customer relationship management, service-dominant logic, channel governance, business-to-business marketing, and brand/community research. Its influence came not from introducing the words “commitment” or “trust,” but from making them central explanatory mechanisms in marketing theory. After this paper, relationship quality could be treated as an empirical construct rather than a managerial slogan, enabling subsequent studies to measure, compare, and refine the relational foundations of loyalty, cooperation, and long-term exchange.

## Abstract

Relationship marketing—establishing, developing, and maintaining successful relational exchanges—constitutes a major shift in marketing theory and practice. After conceptualizing relationship marketing and discussing its ten forms, the authors (1) theorize that successful relationship marketing requires relationship commitment and trust, (2) model relationship commitment and trust as key mediating variables, (3) test this key mediating variable model using data from automobile tire retailers, and (4) compare their model with a rival that does not allow relationship commitment and trust to function as mediating variables. Given the favorable test results for the key mediating variable model, suggestions for further explicating and testing it are offered.
