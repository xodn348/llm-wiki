# Experimental observation of the quantum Hall effect and Berry's phase in graphene

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it turned graphene from a remarkable isolated atomic sheet into a clean experimental platform for relativistic condensed-matter physics. Zhang, Tan, Störmer, and Kim observed an anomalous integer quantum Hall sequence in which the Hall plateaus were shifted by one-half, direct transport evidence that graphene’s charge carriers behave as massless Dirac fermions with a Berry phase of π. That was not just another quantum Hall measurement: it showed that a table-top electronic material could realize quasiparticles governed by a Dirac-like spectrum, linking geometry, topology, and transport in a way that conventional semiconductor two-dimensional electron gases did not.

The result helped establish graphene as a paradigm system for studying topology in real materials. After this work and the parallel 2005 Manchester measurements, Berry phase, chirality, valley degeneracy, and Landau-level structure became experimentally tunable quantities rather than mostly theoretical descriptors. The paper made it newly plausible to use gate voltage, magnetic field, and sample quality to probe relativistic quantum mechanics, quantum Hall physics, and mesoscopic transport in a single atomically thin material.

Its influence also ran forward into later graphene and two-dimensional-material breakthroughs: bilayer and multilayer graphene quantum Hall effects, fractional quantum Hall states in graphene, high-mobility suspended and encapsulated devices, and the broader search for topological electronic phases in materials. In retrospect, the paper’s paradigm shift was that it made graphene a bridge between quantum Hall metrology, Dirac fermion physics, and the modern topology-centered view of electronic matter.
