# Power-law distributions in empirical data

## Why this mattered

Before this work, empirical claims of “power laws” were often made from straight lines on log-log plots, usually with least-squares fits. Clauset, Shalizi, and Newman changed the standard of evidence: a power law was no longer something to be visually recognized, but a statistical hypothesis to be estimated, tested, and compared against alternatives. Their central contribution was methodological discipline: estimate the scaling exponent by maximum likelihood, choose the lower cutoff using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic, use goodness-of-fit tests to ask whether a power law is plausible at all, and compare it quantitatively with distributions such as log-normal or exponential alternatives.

This mattered because power laws had become a unifying language across network science, physics, biology, economics, linguistics, computer science, and the study of human systems. Claims about scale-free networks, city sizes, wealth, earthquakes, citations, species abundance, and many other heavy-tailed phenomena carried theoretical implications: if a system followed a power law, it suggested mechanisms such as preferential attachment, self-organized criticality, multiplicative growth, or other scale-free generative processes. The paper made it possible to separate those substantive claims from fragile curve-fitting. Its analysis of 24 proposed examples showed that some famous cases were statistically consistent with power-law behavior, while others were not, shifting the burden from pattern recognition to model criticism.

The subsequent impact was less a single downstream discovery than a change in scientific hygiene. Later work on complex networks, heavy-tailed risk, online behavior, epidemic spreading, and inequality could no longer responsibly treat a log-log line as decisive evidence. The paper helped turn “is it a power law?” into a reproducible workflow, with explicit uncertainty, cutoff selection, goodness-of-fit, and comparison to rival explanations. In that sense, its paradigm shift was negative as much as positive: it made some discoveries newly defensible, but it also made many attractive universal-scaling stories harder to sustain.

## Abstract

Power-law distributions occur in many situations of scientific interest and have significant consequences for our understanding of natural and man-made phenomena. Unfortunately, the empirical detection and characterization of power laws is made difficult by the large fluctuations that occur in the tail of the distribution. In particular, standard methods such as least-squares fitting are known to produce systematically biased estimates of parameters for power-law distributions and should not be used in most circumstances. Here we describe statistical techniques for making accurate parameter estimates for power-law data, based on maximum likelihood methods and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic. We also show how to tell whether the data follow a power-law distribution at at all, defining quantitative measures that indicate when the power law is a reasonable fit to the data and when it is not. We demonstrate these methods by applying them to twenty-four real-world data sets from a range of different disciplines. Each of the data sets has been conjectured previously to follow a power-law distribution. In some cases we find these conjectures to be consistent with the data while in others the power law is ruled out.
