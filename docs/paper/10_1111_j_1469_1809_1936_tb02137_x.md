# THE USE OF MULTIPLE MEASUREMENTS IN TAXONOMIC PROBLEMS

## Why this mattered

Fisher’s 1936 paper made a paradigm shift by turning taxonomic classification into a quantitative, multivariate decision problem. Instead of judging species or varieties by one measurement at a time, Fisher showed how several correlated measurements could be combined into a single linear function that maximally separated predefined groups. Using the now-famous iris data, he demonstrated that classification could be grounded in measured variation, covariance, and explicit statistical criteria rather than informal typological judgment.

What became newly possible was systematic discrimination among groups when no single trait was decisive. This was the origin of what is now called linear discriminant analysis: a method for projecting high-dimensional observations onto axes chosen for class separation. It gave biologists, anthropologists, and later many other scientists a practical way to classify specimens, diagnose categories, and assess whether apparent group differences were statistically meaningful. Its importance lies less in the specific taxonomic example than in the general idea that classification can be learned from multivariate data.

The paper became a foundation for modern pattern recognition, multivariate statistics, and supervised machine learning. Later breakthroughs in statistical classification, dimensionality reduction, face recognition, medical diagnosis, and data-driven taxonomy all inherited part of Fisher’s formulation: represent each object by measured features, estimate group structure from data, and assign new observations by a rule derived from those measurements. The venue and surrounding eugenic context are historically significant and ethically troubling, but the statistical method itself became a central tool far beyond its original setting.
