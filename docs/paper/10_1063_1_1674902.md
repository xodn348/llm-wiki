# Self-Consistent Molecular-Orbital Methods. IX. An Extended Gaussian-Type Basis for Molecular-Orbital Studies of Organic Molecules

## Why this mattered

Ditchfield, Hehre, and Pople’s 1971 paper mattered because it helped turn *ab initio* molecular orbital theory from a specialist calculation into a practical tool for organic chemistry. The 4-31G basis was not merely another numerical basis set: it gave chemists a standardized, transferable way to represent first-row atoms with enough valence flexibility to describe bonding, geometry, and molecular charge redistribution at a useful level of accuracy. By splitting the valence orbitals while keeping the core compact, the authors struck a balance between chemical realism and computational feasibility that was crucial for the computers of the period.

The broader paradigm shift was methodological standardization. Earlier molecular orbital calculations often depended heavily on bespoke basis choices, making results harder to compare and reproduce. The 4-31G construction showed that a fixed Gaussian basis, optimized from atomic calculations and then modestly rescaled for molecular use, could be applied across a family of organic molecules and yield reasonable equilibrium geometries. That helped establish the idea that quantum chemistry could provide routine, benchmarkable predictions for real molecules rather than only qualitative models or highly customized demonstrations.

This paper also sits directly in the lineage that led to Pople-style computational chemistry: split-valence Gaussian basis sets, systematic basis-set improvement, and increasingly automated molecular calculations. Later basis sets such as 6-31G and its polarized and diffuse variants extended the same logic, while programs associated with Pople’s group made these methods accessible to a much wider chemical community. In that sense, the importance of the 1971 work was not only the specific 4-31G basis, but the template it provided for making electronic-structure theory modular, transferable, and usable at scale.

## Abstract

An extended basis set of atomic functions expressed as fixed linear combinations of Gaussian functions is presented for hydrogen and the first-row atoms carbon to fluorine. In this set, described as 4–31 G, each inner shell is represented by a single basis function taken as a sum of four Gaussians and each valence orbital is split into inner and outer parts described by three and one Gaussian function, respectively. The expansion coefficients and Gaussian exponents are determined by minimizing the total calculated energy of the atomic ground state. This basis set is then used in single-determinant molecular-orbital studies of a group of small polyatomic molecules. Optimization of valence-shell scaling factors shows that considerable rescaling of atomic functions occurs in molecules, the largest effects being observed for hydrogen and carbon. However, the range of optimum scale factors for each atom is small enough to allow the selection of a standard molecular set. The use of this standard basis gives theoretical equilibrium geometries in reasonable agreement with experiment.
