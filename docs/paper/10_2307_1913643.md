# Regression Quantiles

## Why this mattered

Koenker and Bassett’s 1978 paper shifted regression from a model of the conditional mean to a model of the entire conditional distribution. Ordinary least squares summarized how covariates moved the average outcome; regression quantiles made it possible to estimate how covariates affected the median, the lower tail, or the upper tail without assuming symmetric, homoskedastic errors. The central insight was that sample quantiles could be generalized to linear regression by minimizing an asymmetric absolute-loss function, giving a convex optimization problem with a clear statistical interpretation.

This mattered because many scientific and economic questions are not questions about averages. Wage inequality, financial risk, medical outcomes, educational performance, survival-related measurements, and environmental extremes often depend on whether a treatment or covariate changes the bottom, middle, or top of a distribution differently. After this paper, heterogeneous effects could be studied directly rather than treated as residual noise around a mean model. It gave applied researchers a practical language for distributional regression before “heterogeneity” became a central theme in modern empirical work.

The paper also became a foundation for later breakthroughs in semiparametric econometrics, robust statistics, inequality measurement, risk modeling, and modern causal inference. Quantile treatment effects, distributional policy analysis, censored and panel quantile regression, and high-dimensional quantile methods all build on the same conceptual move: estimate conditional features of the outcome distribution beyond its expectation. In that sense, *Regression Quantiles* did not merely add a robust alternative to least squares; it expanded what regression itself could mean.
