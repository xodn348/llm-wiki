# Embryonic Stem Cell Lines Derived from Human Blastocysts

## Why this mattered

Thomson and colleagues made human pluripotent cells experimentally available as stable, expandable laboratory lines. Before this paper, embryonic stem cells were a powerful concept in mice, but human early development could not be studied with a renewable, genetically normal cell source that retained broad developmental potential after months in culture. The key shift was not merely that human blastocyst-derived cells could differentiate into many tissues; it was that they could be maintained undifferentiated, with normal karyotypes and high telomerase activity, while still producing derivatives of trophoblast and all three embryonic germ layers. That combination turned transient embryological material into a reproducible experimental system.

After this work, researchers could ask human-specific questions about lineage choice, early developmental signaling, toxicology, disease mechanisms, and potential cell replacement using cells that were not mouse proxies and not terminally committed adult cells. It also created the ethical and political controversy that shaped the field for the next decade, because the derivation depended on human blastocysts. That constraint made the paper scientifically enabling and socially catalytic at the same time: it defined both the promise of regenerative medicine and the terms of the debate over embryo-derived research.

Its later importance is clearest in the breakthroughs it made legible. Human embryonic stem cell lines became a benchmark for pluripotency, differentiation protocols, disease modeling, and transplantation-oriented cell manufacturing. They also supplied the conceptual and technical target for induced pluripotent stem cells in 2006–2007: reprogrammed adult cells mattered in part because they aimed to reproduce the developmental plasticity that human embryonic stem cells had demonstrated. In that sense, the 1998 paper did not just add a new cell type to biology; it established human pluripotency as a practical platform.

## Abstract

Human blastocyst-derived, pluripotent cell lines are described that have normal karyotypes, express high levels of telomerase activity, and express cell surface markers that characterize primate embryonic stem cells but do not characterize other early lineages. After undifferentiated proliferation in vitro for 4 to 5 months, these cells still maintained the developmental potential to form trophoblast and derivatives of all three embryonic germ layers, including gut epithelium (endoderm); cartilage, bone, smooth muscle, and striated muscle (mesoderm); and neural epithelium, embryonic ganglia, and stratified squamous epithelium (ectoderm). These cell lines should be useful in human developmental biology, drug discovery, and transplantation medicine.
