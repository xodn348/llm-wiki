# Separation of Ownership and Control

## Why this mattered

Fama and Jensen’s 1983 paper mattered because it gave agency theory a general architecture for explaining why modern organizations can survive the separation of residual risk bearing from day-to-day decision management. Rather than treating dispersed ownership simply as a defect of the public corporation, the paper showed how organizations control agency problems by separating decision initiation and implementation from decision ratification and monitoring. This made the corporation, the mutual, the partnership, the nonprofit, and other forms comparable within one framework: each could be analyzed by how residual claims, decision rights, and monitoring mechanisms were allocated.

The paradigm shift was to move the theory of the firm away from a simple owner-manager model and toward a governance-systems view. After Fama and Jensen, boards of directors, internal hierarchies, voting rules, professional managers, takeovers, and concentrated residual claimants could be understood as alternative mechanisms for controlling decision agents. This helped make “corporate governance” a formal research field rather than a loose collection of legal and managerial concerns, linking finance, law, organizational economics, and accounting around a common question: how do institutions make delegated decision-making credible?

The paper also became a bridge from Jensen and Meckling’s earlier agency-cost theory to later work on boards, executive compensation, ownership structure, venture capital contracting, nonprofit governance, and the market for corporate control. Its influence lay less in a single empirical result than in a vocabulary that made new empirical and theoretical work possible: decision rights could be decomposed, monitoring could be studied as an institutional design problem, and organizational forms could be compared by the way they economize on agency costs under different residual-claim structures.
