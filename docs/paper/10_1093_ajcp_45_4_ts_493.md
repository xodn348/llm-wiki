# Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing by a Standardized Single Disk Method

## Why this mattered

Before Bauer, Kirby, Sherris, and Turck, antibiotic susceptibility testing was technically possible but often locally variable: differences in inoculum size, agar depth, disk potency, incubation, and interpretive thresholds could change results enough to limit comparability. The 1966 single-disk method mattered because it turned susceptibility testing into a standardized clinical laboratory procedure. By specifying a reproducible disk diffusion workflow and relating inhibition-zone diameters to therapeutic categories, the paper helped make antimicrobial choice less dependent on artisanal lab practice and more dependent on shared, auditable measurement.

The paradigm shift was not the invention of diffusion testing itself, but its normalization into a practical standard. After this paper, ordinary hospital laboratories could generate susceptibility results that were fast, inexpensive, and comparable across institutions. That made it newly feasible to guide treatment for individual infections at scale, to detect emerging resistance patterns systematically, and to build cumulative antibiograms that linked microbiology results to local prescribing decisions. In effect, the method helped move antibiotics from a largely empiric therapeutic era toward a data-guided one.

Its later importance is visible in the infrastructure that grew around it. The “Kirby-Bauer” disk diffusion test became a foundation for standardized interpretive criteria maintained by bodies such as NCCLS/CLSI and, later, other standards organizations. That standardization supported antimicrobial stewardship, infection-control surveillance, and resistance epidemiology, including the recognition and tracking of clinically consequential resistance phenotypes. Many later breakthroughs in antimicrobial policy and hospital microbiology depended less on a single new drug than on the ability this paper helped institutionalize: measuring bacterial response to antibiotics in a way that clinicians, laboratories, and public-health systems could trust and compare.
