# A pneumonia outbreak associated with a new coronavirus of probable bat origin

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it turned an unexplained pneumonia cluster into a defined viral disease problem. By sequencing full genomes from early patients, showing near identity among isolates, placing the agent within SARS-related coronaviruses, and linking it most closely to a bat coronavirus, Zhou et al. established that the Wuhan outbreak was caused by a novel betacoronavirus rather than by a known respiratory pathogen. That shifted the scientific response from clinical case description to molecular epidemiology: the outbreak could now be tracked, compared, cultured, and diagnosed with sequence-based tools.

The key paradigm shift was that the new virus was immediately made legible in the framework built after SARS. The paper showed that the virus was related to SARS-CoV, but distinct; that it had a probable bat origin; and that it used ACE2 for cell entry, the same receptor used by SARS-CoV. Those findings did not merely classify the pathogen. They identified the biological interface most relevant to transmission, tissue tropism, neutralization, and countermeasure design. After this, the spike protein and ACE2-binding mechanism became central targets for structural biology, serology, antivirals, monoclonal antibodies, and vaccine development.

Its practical importance was equally large. The availability of full viral genomes and an isolate from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid enabled diagnostic assays, comparative genomics, and laboratory neutralization studies at the very start of the pandemic. Subsequent breakthroughs, including rapid mRNA and viral-vector vaccine design, spike-structure determination, variant surveillance, and antibody escape mapping, all depended on the shift this paper exemplified: an emerging epidemic could be converted within weeks into a globally shared molecular object, with its ancestry, receptor biology, and immune targets defined well enough to guide intervention.

## Abstract

Abstract Since the outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) 18 years ago, a large number of SARS-related coronaviruses (SARSr-CoVs) have been discovered in their natural reservoir host, bats 1–4 . Previous studies have shown that some bat SARSr-CoVs have the potential to infect humans 5–7 . Here we report the identification and characterization of a new coronavirus (2019-nCoV), which caused an epidemic of acute respiratory syndrome in humans in Wuhan, China. The epidemic, which started on 12 December 2019, had caused 2,794 laboratory-confirmed infections including 80 deaths by 26 January 2020. Full-length genome sequences were obtained from five patients at an early stage of the outbreak. The sequences are almost identical and share 79.6% sequence identity to SARS-CoV. Furthermore, we show that 2019-nCoV is 96% identical at the whole-genome level to a bat coronavirus. Pairwise protein sequence analysis of seven conserved non-structural proteins domains show that this virus belongs to the species of . In addition, 2019-nCoV virus isolated from the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of a critically ill patient could be neutralized by sera from several patients. Notably, we confirmed that 2019-nCoV uses the same cell entry receptor—angiotensin converting enzyme II (ACE2)—as SARS-CoV.
