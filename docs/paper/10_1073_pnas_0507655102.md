# An index to quantify an individual's scientific research output

## Why this mattered

Hirsch’s 2005 paper mattered because it gave scientific evaluation a simple, portable number that combined productivity and citation impact into one measure. Before the h-index, researchers were commonly compared by total papers, total citations, citations per paper, or recognition-based signals, each of which could be distorted by a few highly cited papers, many lightly cited papers, career length, or field conventions. The h-index shifted the paradigm by defining influence as a sustained citation profile: a scientist with index *h* has *h* papers cited at least *h* times. That made individual research output newly quantifiable in a way that was easy to compute, explain, and rank.

The paper did not create bibliometrics, but it changed the scale at which bibliometric judgment could be operationalized. Once citation databases such as Web of Science, Scopus, and later Google Scholar made publication and citation records broadly searchable, the h-index became a ready-made evaluative tool for hiring, promotion, grants, awards, departmental comparisons, and public profiles. Its importance lay partly in this institutional uptake: it translated a complex scholarly record into a metric that administrators, committees, and researchers could all use, even when they disagreed about its meaning.

Its influence also exposed the limits of metric-driven evaluation. The h-index helped catalyze later work on field-normalized metrics, author-level indicators, journal and institutional analytics, and critiques of research assessment practices. Subsequent breakthroughs in responsible metrics, altmetrics, citation-context analysis, and evaluation reform can be read partly as responses to the world this paper helped create: one in which scientific impact was no longer only narrated by experts, but continuously measured, compared, and contested.

## Abstract

I propose the index h , defined as the number of papers with citation number ≥ h , as a useful index to characterize the scientific output of a researcher.
