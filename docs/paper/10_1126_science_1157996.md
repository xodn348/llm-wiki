# Measurement of the Elastic Properties and Intrinsic Strength of Monolayer Graphene

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it turned graphene’s extraordinary mechanical properties from a theoretical expectation into a direct experimental fact. Before 2008, graphene was already known as an exceptional electronic material, but Lee, Wei, Kysar, and Hone showed that a single atomic layer could also be treated as a measurable mechanical object: suspended, indented, strained far beyond the linear regime, and broken in a controlled way. The reported stiffness of about 340 N/m, corresponding to a Young’s modulus near 1 TPa, placed monolayer graphene alongside the stiffest known carbon materials, while the intrinsic strength of 42 N/m, or about 130 GPa when expressed as an equivalent bulk stress, established it as the strongest material then measured.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as numerical. The work demonstrated that atomically thin membranes could be mechanically tested without relying only on bulk analogies, simulations, or indirect inference. By combining AFM nanoindentation with a nonlinear elastic model, the authors created a practical route for extracting elastic constants and failure strength from two-dimensional materials. That made it newly possible to ask engineering-style questions about a one-atom-thick crystal: how much strain it can sustain, how defects alter failure, how adhesion and boundary conditions matter, and how continuum mechanics survives or breaks down at atomic thickness.

Its influence extended well beyond graphene. The paper helped define mechanical characterization as a central part of two-dimensional materials science, alongside electronic, optical, and thermal measurement. Subsequent work on graphene resonators, strain engineering, flexible electronics, nanocomposites, and later transition-metal dichalcogenides inherited the premise that monolayer crystals were not just surfaces or films, but freestanding materials with intrinsic elastic and fracture properties. In that sense, the result helped convert graphene from a remarkable laboratory crystal into a platform for testing the limits of materials mechanics at the atomic scale.

## Abstract

We measured the elastic properties and intrinsic breaking strength of free-standing monolayer graphene membranes by nanoindentation in an atomic force microscope. The force-displacement behavior is interpreted within a framework of nonlinear elastic stress-strain response, and yields second- and third-order elastic stiffnesses of 340 newtons per meter (N m(-1)) and -690 Nm(-1), respectively. The breaking strength is 42 N m(-1) and represents the intrinsic strength of a defect-free sheet. These quantities correspond to a Young's modulus of E = 1.0 terapascals, third-order elastic stiffness of D = -2.0 terapascals, and intrinsic strength of sigma(int) = 130 gigapascals for bulk graphite. These experiments establish graphene as the strongest material ever measured, and show that atomically perfect nanoscale materials can be mechanically tested to deformations well beyond the linear regime.
