# Comprehensive molecular characterization of gastric adenocarcinoma

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it reframed gastric adenocarcinoma from a single anatomically defined disease with variable histology into a set of molecularly distinct diseases. Before TCGA’s analysis, gastric cancer classification was dominated by location, morphology, and broad etiologic associations, which made clinical trials and biomarker studies difficult to interpret. By integrating genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and viral-status data across 295 tumors, the study proposed four reproducible subtypes: Epstein-Barr virus positive, microsatellite unstable, genomically stable, and chromosomal instability tumors. That classification made heterogeneity itself the organizing principle rather than a confounding problem.

The practical shift was that each subtype suggested different biological vulnerabilities. EBV-positive tumors were linked to PIK3CA mutation, extreme DNA hypermethylation, and amplification of immune-checkpoint ligand genes such as CD274/PD-L1 and PDCD1LG2/PD-L2; MSI tumors showed high mutation burden and alterations in targetable signaling genes; genomically stable tumors were tied to diffuse-type disease and RHOA/RHO-family pathway alterations; chromosomal-instability tumors were enriched for aneuploidy and receptor tyrosine kinase amplifications. This gave researchers and trial designers a rationale for stratifying patients by mechanism rather than by gastric cancer diagnosis alone.

Its influence was especially important for later precision-oncology and immunotherapy work in gastroesophageal cancers. The paper did not itself prove subtype-specific therapies, but it supplied a widely used molecular map that helped connect EBV positivity, MSI status, checkpoint biology, receptor tyrosine kinase amplification, and diffuse-type biology to testable clinical strategies. In that sense, its paradigm shift was not a single drug target; it was the demonstration that gastric adenocarcinoma could be made clinically and experimentally tractable by molecular taxonomy.

## Abstract

Gastric cancer is a leading cause of cancer deaths, but analysis of its molecular and clinical characteristics has been complicated by histological and aetiological heterogeneity. Here we describe a comprehensive molecular evaluation of 295 primary gastric adenocarcinomas as part of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project. We propose a molecular classification dividing gastric cancer into four subtypes: tumours positive for Epstein–Barr virus, which display recurrent PIK3CA mutations, extreme DNA hypermethylation, and amplification of JAK2, CD274 (also known as PD-L1) and PDCD1LG2 (also known as PD-L2); microsatellite unstable tumours, which show elevated mutation rates, including mutations of genes encoding targetable oncogenic signalling proteins; genomically stable tumours, which are enriched for the diffuse histological variant and mutations of RHOA or fusions involving RHO-family GTPase-activating proteins; and tumours with chromosomal instability, which show marked aneuploidy and focal amplification of receptor tyrosine kinases. Identification of these subtypes provides a roadmap for patient stratification and trials of targeted therapies. The Cancer Genome Atlas reports on molecular evaluation of 295 primary gastric adenocarcinomas and proposes a new classification of gastric cancers into 4 subtypes, which should help with clinical assessment and trials of targeted therapies. This contribution from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project describes the molecular evaluation of 295 primary gastric adenocarcinomas. Based on the results, the authors propose a novel classification separating gastric cancers into four subtypes according to: Epstein–Barr virus positive status, microsatellite instability, chromosomal instability or genomic stability. Given the histologic and etiologic heterogeneity of gastric cancer identification of these subtypes, using a schema that can readily be applied to patient samples should help with patient stratification and trials of targeted therapies.
