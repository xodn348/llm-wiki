# A discrete numerical model for granular assemblies

## Why this mattered

Cundall and Strack’s paper mattered because it made granular matter computationally discrete in a practical, general way. Instead of treating soil, powders, or grains as a continuum with averaged stresses and strains, the distinct element method represented an assembly as individual discs or spheres whose contacts were tracked one by one and whose motions were advanced particle by particle. That shifted the modeling question from “what continuum law should approximate this material?” to “what macroscopic behavior emerges from many simple contact interactions?” For granular media, where force chains, rearrangements, slip, dilation, and history-dependent contact networks are central rather than incidental, this was a change in explanatory unit.

The validation against photoelastic disc experiments was crucial. The paper did not merely propose a numerical scheme; it showed that the computed force-vector patterns from the BALL program closely resembled experimentally observed force transmission in a real granular assembly. That comparison gave researchers a reason to trust simulations as laboratories for phenomena that were difficult to observe directly inside opaque, many-particle systems. After this, it became possible to study granular mechanics from the inside: contact-force distributions, fabric evolution, shear banding, jamming-like transitions, failure localization, and the link between grain-scale frictional rules and bulk strength.

Its later influence came from this portability. DEM became a foundation for computational geomechanics, powder technology, rock mechanics, bulk solids handling, pharmaceutical processing, mining, planetary regolith studies, and coupled methods such as CFD-DEM for particle-fluid flows. Subsequent breakthroughs did not simply cite the 1979 paper as an early simulation; they inherited its central premise: discontinuous, frictional, rearranging materials often need to be modeled as interacting bodies, not as smoothed fields alone. That is why the paper became paradigmatic: it supplied both a method and a new computational ontology for granular matter.

## Abstract

The distinct element method is a numerical model capable of describing the mechanical behaviour of assemblies of discs and spheres. The method is based on the use of an explicit numerical scheme in which the interaction of the particles is monitored contact by contact and the motion of the particles modelled particle by particle. The main features of the distinct element method are described. The method is validated by comparing force vector plots obtained from the computer program BALL with the corresponding plots obtained from a photoelastic analysis. The photoelastic analysis used for the comparison is the one applied to an assembly of discs by De Josselin de Jong and Verruijt (1969). The force vector diagrams obtained numerically closely resemble those obtained photoelastically. It is concluded from this comparison that the distinct element method and the program BALL are valid tools for research into the behaviour of granular assemblies.
