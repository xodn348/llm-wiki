# Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity

## Why this mattered

Strictly, the 1991 *Feminist Review* item is Mary McIntosh’s review of Judith Butler’s 1990 book; the paradigm-shifting work is Butler’s *Gender Trouble*. Its importance lay in moving feminist theory away from treating “women” as a stable, pre-political category and toward asking how such categories are produced, regulated, and made to appear natural. Butler’s argument that sex, gender, and desire are organized through a “heterosexual matrix” challenged the older division in which sex was biological ground and gender was cultural interpretation.

After *Gender Trouble*, it became newly possible to analyze identity not as an essence expressed by bodies, but as an effect of repeated, norm-governed acts. That shift gave scholars a vocabulary for studying femininity, masculinity, heterosexuality, and bodily coherence as maintained through social practice, language, law, medicine, and everyday performance. It also reframed parody, drag, and gender discontinuity not as marginal curiosities, but as cases that expose the imitative structure of gender itself.

The book helped consolidate queer theory in the 1990s and set up later breakthroughs in gender studies, sexuality studies, trans studies, cultural theory, and political philosophy. Butler’s own *Bodies That Matter* developed the account of materiality and normativity that critics demanded after *Gender Trouble*, while later work across the humanities and social sciences used performativity to examine how institutions stabilize identity categories. Its influence was not that it ended debates over sex, gender, or feminist politics, but that it made any adequate theory of them answer a harder question: how the subject who seeks representation is itself historically produced.
