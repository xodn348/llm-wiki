# A Fast Learning Algorithm for Deep Belief Nets

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, deep neural networks were widely viewed as difficult to train: backpropagation through many layers often stalled, and probabilistic belief networks with multiple hidden layers faced hard inference problems because of explaining-away interactions. Hinton, Osindero, and Teh changed the practical picture by showing that deep models could be trained greedily, one layer at a time, using restricted Boltzmann machines and “complementary priors” to make inference tractable. The result was not merely a better handwritten-digit classifier; it was evidence that depth itself could be learned effectively, and that unsupervised pretraining could put a deep network into a parameter region where later fine-tuning worked.

What became newly possible was a credible recipe for building multilayer representations rather than hand-designing features or relying on shallow models. The paper made deep generative modeling operational at a time when labeled data, compute, and optimization methods were more limited than in the 2010s. Its demonstrations on MNIST and digit generation showed that higher layers could capture abstract structure, including class identity and smooth variations along learned manifolds. This helped revive serious interest in neural networks after a period dominated by kernel methods, graphical models, and shallower discriminative techniques.

The specific machinery of deep belief nets was later overtaken in many applications by rectified units, dropout, batch normalization, large labeled datasets, GPUs, and end-to-end supervised training. But the paper’s historical importance lies in reopening the question of whether deep learned representations could scale and outperform feature-engineered systems. It was a direct precursor to the deep learning resurgence that followed, including the layerwise pretraining era of the late 2000s and, more broadly, the representation-learning mindset that underpinned later breakthroughs in vision, speech recognition, and generative modeling.

## Abstract

We show how to use "complementary priors" to eliminate the explaining-away effects that make inference difficult in densely connected belief nets that have many hidden layers. Using complementary priors, we derive a fast, greedy algorithm that can learn deep, directed belief networks one layer at a time, provided the top two layers form an undirected associative memory. The fast, greedy algorithm is used to initialize a slower learning procedure that fine-tunes the weights using a contrastive version of the wake-sleep algorithm. After fine-tuning, a network with three hidden layers forms a very good generative model of the joint distribution of handwritten digit images and their labels. This generative model gives better digit classification than the best discriminative learning algorithms. The low-dimensional manifolds on which the digits lie are modeled by long ravines in the free-energy landscape of the top-level associative memory, and it is easy to explore these ravines by using the directed connections to display what the associative memory has in mind.
