# Measurements of Ω and Λ from 42 High‐Redshift Supernovae

## Why this mattered

This paper was one of the decisive results behind the late-1990s shift from a matter-dominated, decelerating universe to the now-standard picture of cosmic acceleration. By using 42 high-redshift Type Ia supernovae as standardized candles and comparing them with low-redshift supernovae, the Supernova Cosmology Project found that distant supernovae were dimmer than expected in a flat, Λ = 0 cosmology. Interpreted within general relativity and Friedmann-Lemaître cosmology, that dimming pointed to a positive cosmological constant or some equivalent dark-energy component. The reported flat-universe value, ΩM ≈ 0.28 with ΩΛ making up the balance, was close to what became the concordance ΛCDM model.

Its importance was not simply that it measured two cosmological parameters more tightly; it changed which cosmological models were viable. Before these supernova results, Λ was often treated as optional or aesthetically problematic, and inflationary flatness was commonly paired with ΩM = 1. This paper helped show that a flat universe did not require critical-density matter: most of the energy density could instead be in a smooth component with negative pressure. That made it possible to reconcile supernova distances with emerging evidence from galaxy clustering, cluster baryon fractions, cosmic ages, and soon after, the cosmic microwave background.

The work also turned high-redshift Type Ia supernova cosmology into a precision program rather than a speculative test. Its statistical checks on reddening, Malmquist bias, light-curve standardization, and outliers helped define the methodological agenda for later surveys. Subsequent breakthroughs, including CMB acoustic-peak measurements, baryon acoustic oscillations, weak-lensing surveys, and large supernova compilations, did not replace the paper’s core claim so much as embed it in a broader evidence network: the universe is spatially close to flat, matter is subcritical, and late-time expansion is accelerating.

## Abstract

We report measurements of the mass density, ΩM, and cosmological-constant energy density, ΩΛ of the universe based on the analysis of 42 type Ia supernovae discovered by the Supernova Cosmology Project. The magnitude-redshift data for these supernovae, at redshifts between 0.18 and 0.83, are fitted jointly with a set of supernovae from the Calán/Tololo Supernova Survey, at redshifts below 0.1, to yield values for the cosmological parameters. All supernova peak magnitudes are standardized using a SN Ia light-curve width-luminosity relation. The measurement yields a joint probability distribution of the cosmological parameters that is approximated by the relation 0.8ΩM - 0.6ΩΛ ≈ - 0.2 ± 0.1 in the region of interest (ΩM ≲ 1.5). For a flat (ΩM + ΩΛ = 1) cosmology we find ΩflatM = 0.28+0.09-0.08 (1 σ statistical) +0.05-0.04 (identified systematics). The data are strongly inconsistent with a Λ = 0 flat cosmology, the simplest inflationary universe model. An open, Λ = 0 cosmology also does not fit the data well: the data indicate that the cosmological constant is nonzero and positive, with a confidence of P(Λ &gt; 0) = 99%, including the identified systematic uncertainties. The best-fit age of the universe relative to the Hubble time is tflat0 = 14.9+1.4-1.1(0.63/h) Gyr for a flat cosmology. The size of our sample allows us to perform a variety of statistical tests to check for possible systematic errors and biases. We find no significant differences in either the host reddening distribution or Malmquist bias between the low-redshift Calán/Tololo sample and our high-redshift sample. Excluding those few supernovae that are outliers in color excess or fit residual does not significantly change the results. The conclusions are also robust whether or not a width-luminosity relation is used to standardize the supernova peak magnitudes. We discuss and constrain, where possible, hypothetical alternatives to a cosmological constant.
