# Dabigatran versus Warfarin in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation

## Why this mattered

For more than half a century, warfarin had been the standard oral anticoagulant for preventing stroke in atrial fibrillation, but its usefulness was constrained by a narrow therapeutic window, food and drug interactions, and the need for regular INR monitoring. This trial mattered because it showed, in a very large randomized population, that a fixed-dose oral anticoagulant targeting thrombin directly could match or exceed warfarin’s protection against stroke and systemic embolism without routine coagulation monitoring. The result changed anticoagulation from a model centered on individualized dose titration and laboratory surveillance to one in which predictable pharmacology could be used for long-term stroke prevention.

The key paradigm shift was not simply that dabigatran was “another anticoagulant,” but that it provided clinical proof that warfarin was no longer the unavoidable reference therapy for nonvalvular atrial fibrillation. Dabigatran 150 mg twice daily reduced stroke or systemic embolism compared with warfarin, while dabigatran 110 mg twice daily achieved similar efficacy with less major bleeding; both doses sharply reduced hemorrhagic stroke. Those findings made it newly plausible to develop and prescribe oral anticoagulants on the basis of fixed dosing, rapid onset, fewer dietary constraints, and less monitoring burden, while still demanding careful attention to renal function, bleeding risk, adherence, and later concerns about reversibility.

The paper helped open the direct oral anticoagulant era. Subsequent trials of factor Xa inhibitors, including rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban, extended the same broad idea: targeted oral anticoagulation could be at least comparable to warfarin in atrial fibrillation, often with lower intracranial bleeding. In retrospect, the study marked a turning point in cardiovascular therapeutics: it translated decades of coagulation biology into a practical alternative to vitamin K antagonism and reshaped guidelines, prescribing habits, drug development, and the risk-benefit conversation around stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation.

## Abstract

BACKGROUND: Warfarin reduces the risk of stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation but increases the risk of hemorrhage and is difficult to use. Dabigatran is a new oral direct thrombin inhibitor. METHODS: In this noninferiority trial, we randomly assigned 18,113 patients who had atrial fibrillation and a risk of stroke to receive, in a blinded fashion, fixed doses of dabigatran--110 mg or 150 mg twice daily--or, in an unblinded fashion, adjusted-dose warfarin. The median duration of the follow-up period was 2.0 years. The primary outcome was stroke or systemic embolism. RESULTS: Rates of the primary outcome were 1.69% per year in the warfarin group, as compared with 1.53% per year in the group that received 110 mg of dabigatran (relative risk with dabigatran, 0.91; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.74 to 1.11; P<0.001 for noninferiority) and 1.11% per year in the group that received 150 mg of dabigatran (relative risk, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.53 to 0.82; P<0.001 for superiority). The rate of major bleeding was 3.36% per year in the warfarin group, as compared with 2.71% per year in the group receiving 110 mg of dabigatran (P=0.003) and 3.11% per year in the group receiving 150 mg of dabigatran (P=0.31). The rate of hemorrhagic stroke was 0.38% per year in the warfarin group, as compared with 0.12% per year with 110 mg of dabigatran (P<0.001) and 0.10% per year with 150 mg of dabigatran (P<0.001). The mortality rate was 4.13% per year in the warfarin group, as compared with 3.75% per year with 110 mg of dabigatran (P=0.13) and 3.64% per year with 150 mg of dabigatran (P=0.051). CONCLUSIONS: In patients with atrial fibrillation, dabigatran given at a dose of 110 mg was associated with rates of stroke and systemic embolism that were similar to those associated with warfarin, as well as lower rates of major hemorrhage. Dabigatran administered at a dose of 150 mg, as compared with warfarin, was associated with lower rates of stroke and systemic embolism but similar rates of major hemorrhage. (ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT00262600.)
