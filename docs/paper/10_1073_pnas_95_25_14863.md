# Cluster analysis and display of genome-wide expression patterns

## Why this mattered

Before Eisen, Spellman, Brown, and Botstein, genome-wide expression data were becoming technically possible, but they were not yet easy to interpret as a coherent biological object. This paper helped turn microarray measurements from long tables of gene-by-condition values into patterns that biologists could inspect, compare, and reason about. Its key shift was methodological and visual: genes could be grouped by similarity of expression across many conditions, and the clustered data could be displayed in a heat-map-like format that showed both relationships among genes and the underlying measurements at once.

That made a new kind of functional inference practical at genome scale. If genes with known related roles clustered together, then uncharacterized genes appearing in the same expression neighborhood became candidates for related functions. The paper did not claim that coexpression proved function, but it established coexpression as a systematic source of biological hypotheses. This was especially important in the late 1990s, when genome sequencing was rapidly expanding the catalog of predicted genes faster than experimental biology could annotate them one by one.

Its influence extended well beyond yeast microarrays. The paper helped define a template for exploratory genomics: measure many molecular features in parallel, use unsupervised pattern discovery to organize them, and interpret clusters as signatures of cellular processes, states, or perturbations. Later transcriptomics, cancer expression subtyping, gene-expression signatures, regulatory network inference, and single-cell RNA-seq analysis all inherited parts of this paradigm. The specific algorithms were simple by later standards, but the conceptual advance was durable: high-dimensional genomic data could reveal biological structure when arranged by shared patterns rather than examined gene by gene.

## Abstract

A system of cluster analysis for genome-wide expression data from DNA microarray hybridization is described that uses standard statistical algorithms to arrange genes according to similarity in pattern of gene expression. The output is displayed graphically, conveying the clustering and the underlying expression data simultaneously in a form intuitive for biologists. We have found in the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae that clustering gene expression data groups together efficiently genes of known similar function, and we find a similar tendency in human data. Thus patterns seen in genome-wide expression experiments can be interpreted as indications of the status of cellular processes. Also, coexpression of genes of known function with poorly characterized or novel genes may provide a simple means of gaining leads to the functions of many genes for which information is not available currently.
