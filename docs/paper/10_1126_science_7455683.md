# The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, rational choice theory largely treated preferences as stable objects revealed by choice, assuming that equivalent descriptions of the same decision should lead to equivalent decisions. Tversky and Kahneman showed that this invariance principle fails in systematic, predictable ways: people reverse preferences when outcomes are framed as gains rather than losses, or as lives saved rather than lives lost, even when the underlying probabilities and consequences are mathematically identical. The paper made “framing” a central explanatory concept, turning what might have looked like noise or bias in individual judgment into a reproducible feature of human decision-making.

Its importance was not only empirical but conceptual. By comparing frames to perceptual perspectives, the authors argued that decision problems are psychologically constructed rather than merely read off from objective facts. This helped shift the study of choice from an axiomatic model of ideal rationality toward a descriptive science of how people actually represent risk, probability, and value. It gave economists, psychologists, political scientists, physicians, and policy designers a concrete way to study why wording, reference points, and presentation change behavior without changing material incentives.

The paper became a foundation for behavioral economics and for later work on nudges, risk communication, medical decision-making, legal persuasion, and public policy design. It clarified why prospect theory, introduced by Kahneman and Tversky in 1979, mattered beyond laboratory gambles: if choices depend on reference points and loss aversion, then institutions and communicators inevitably shape decisions by shaping frames. Subsequent breakthroughs in behavioral finance, health communication, and choice architecture built on this insight, treating the formulation of options as a causal part of decision environments rather than a neutral wrapper around them.

## Abstract

The psychological principles that govern the perception of decision problems and the evaluation of probabilities and outcomes produce predictable shifts of preference when the same problem is framed in different ways. Reversals of preference are demonstrated in choices regarding monetary outcomes, both hypothetical and real, and in questions pertaining to the loss of human lives. The effects of frames on preferences are compared to the effects of perspectives on perceptual appearance. The dependence of preferences on the formulation of decision problems is a significant concern for the theory of rational choice.
