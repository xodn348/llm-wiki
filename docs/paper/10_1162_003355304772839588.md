# How Much Should We Trust Differences-In-Differences Estimates?

## Why this mattered

Before Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan, differences-in-differences was already a central tool for empirical work in economics, especially in policy settings where treated and untreated jurisdictions could be compared over time. The paper mattered because it showed that a large share of published-style DD designs had a basic inference problem: with many years of data and serially correlated outcomes, conventional OLS standard errors could make noise look like policy effects. The placebo-law exercise was especially influential because it made the failure concrete rather than merely technical: randomly assigned “laws” appeared statistically significant far more often than they should have, implying that the nominal 5 percent test could behave like a much looser standard in common applications.

The paradigm shift was not that DD became unusable, but that credible DD required design-conscious inference. After this paper, applied researchers increasingly treated the unit of policy variation, the number of clusters, serial correlation, and the effective sample size as first-order features of the research design. Cluster-robust inference, block bootstrap methods, collapsing pre/post periods, and later refinements for few clusters and staggered adoption became part of the normal methodological vocabulary. The paper helped move DD from a regression specification one could write down mechanically toward an identification-and-inference framework whose uncertainty had to match the way treatment was assigned and outcomes evolved.

Its influence also set the stage for later breakthroughs in causal inference with panel data. Once researchers took seriously that DD estimates could look precise for the wrong reason, it became natural to scrutinize other hidden assumptions: parallel trends, heterogeneous treatment effects, dynamic effects, and the problems created by two-way fixed effects under staggered treatment timing. Later work on event-study diagnostics, modern staggered-adoption estimators, synthetic control connections, and robust confidence intervals all built on the same lesson in spirit: the credibility of quasi-experimental evidence depends not only on a plausible comparison group, but on inference and estimands that reflect the actual structure of the data.

## Abstract

Most papers that employ Differences-in-Differences estimation (DD) use many years of data and focus on serially correlated outcomes but ignore that the resulting standard errors are inconsistent. To illustrate the severity of this issue, we randomly generate placebo laws in state-level data on female wages from the Current Population Survey. For each law, we use OLS to compute the DD estimate of its “effect” as well as the standard error of this estimate. These conventional DD standard errors severely understate the standard deviation of the estimators: we find an “effect” significant at the 5 percent level for up to 45 percent of the placebo interventions. We use Monte Carlo simulations to investi-gate how well existing methods help solve this problem. Econometric corrections that place a specific parametric form on the time-series process do not perform well. Bootstrap (taking into account the autocorrelation of the data) works well when the number of states is large enough. Two corrections based on asymptotic approximation of the variance-covariance matrix work well for moderate numbers of states and one correction that collapses the time series information into a “pre”-and “post”-period and explicitly takes into account the effective sample size works well even for small numbers of states.
