# On Information and Sufficiency

## Why this mattered

Kullback and Leibler’s paper made a decisive conceptual move: it treated “information” not as an absolute quantity in a distribution, but as a directed measure of how well observations distinguish one statistical hypothesis from another. The quantity now called Kullback-Leibler divergence gave statisticians a general mathematical language for comparing probability laws, independent of any particular test statistic or parametric family. Its link to sufficiency was central: a sufficient statistic preserves all the information, in this discrimination sense, that the full sample contains about the alternative distributions under consideration.

This shifted statistical inference toward an information-theoretic view of evidence. After the paper, likelihood, hypothesis testing, estimation, and model comparison could be discussed through a common measure of distinguishability between distributions. The work helped make possible later developments in asymptotic statistics, information geometry, maximum likelihood theory, minimum discrimination information, and Bayesian and frequentist model selection criteria. It also supplied one of the core mathematical objects used later in machine learning, variational inference, coding theory, signal processing, and statistical physics.

The paper’s importance lies partly in how reusable the idea proved to be. KL divergence became a bridge between Fisherian sufficiency, Shannon’s information theory, and the modern study of probabilistic models. Subsequent breakthroughs such as Akaike’s information criterion, variational Bayes, expectation-maximization interpretations, generative modeling, and cross-entropy-based learning all rely, directly or indirectly, on the same insight: progress in inference often depends on quantifying how one probability distribution departs from another.
