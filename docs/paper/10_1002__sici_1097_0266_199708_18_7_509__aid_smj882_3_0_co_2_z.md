# Dynamic capabilities and strategic management

## Why this mattered

Teece, Pisano, and Shuen shifted strategic management away from treating competitive advantage mainly as a matter of industry position, entry barriers, or possession of valuable resources at a point in time. Their core move was to make adaptation itself the object of analysis: firms in fast-changing technological environments succeed not simply because they own scarce assets, but because they can integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences as conditions change. This reframed strategy around processes, asset positions, and paths, making organizational routines, managerial coordination, learning, and inherited histories central explanations for why some firms repeatedly create and capture value while others fail to adjust.

The paper mattered because it gave scholars a vocabulary for studying advantage under technological turbulence without reducing firm performance either to market structure or to static resource endowments. Concepts such as path dependence, difficult-to-trade knowledge assets, complementary assets, replicability, and imitatability made it possible to ask why capabilities persist, why they are hard to copy, and why prior commitments both enable and constrain future moves. In doing so, the article extended the resource-based view into a more evolutionary and process-oriented theory of strategic change.

Its influence is visible in later work on organizational learning, business model innovation, platform strategy, technology commercialization, and managerial theories of sensing, seizing, and transforming opportunities. Subsequent dynamic-capabilities research refined the framework, debated whether dynamic capabilities directly produce performance or operate through ordinary capabilities, and applied it to settings such as digital transformation, ecosystems, and innovation under uncertainty. The enduring contribution of the 1997 paper was to make strategic renewal a first-class problem: after it, competitive advantage could be studied not only as something firms have, but as something they must continually remake.

## Abstract

The dynamic capabilities framework analyzes the sources and methods of wealth creation and capture by private enterprise firms operating in environments of rapid technological change. The competitive advantage of firms is seen as resting on distinctive processes (ways of coordinating and combining), shaped by the firm’s (specific) asset positions (such as the firm’s portfolio of difficult-to-trade knowledge assets and complementary assets), and the evolution path(s) it has adopted or inherited. The importance of path dependencies is amplified where conditions of increasing returns exist. Whether and how a firm’s competitive advantage is eroded depends on the stability of market demand, and the ease of replicability (expanding internally) and imitatability (replication by competitors). If correct, the framework suggests that private wealth creation in regimes of rapid technological change depends in large measure on honing internal technological, organizational, and managerial processes inside the firm. In short, identifying new opportunities and organizing effectively and efficiently to embrace them are generally more fundamental to private wealth creation than is strategizing, if by strategizing one means engaging in business conduct that keeps competitors off balance, raises rival’s costs, and excludes new entrants. © 1997 by John Wiley & Sons, Ltd.
