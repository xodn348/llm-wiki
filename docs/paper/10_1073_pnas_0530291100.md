# Prospective identification of tumorigenic breast cancer cells

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, the cancer stem cell model had strong roots in hematologic malignancy, but it was not yet established that a solid human carcinoma could be functionally organized around a prospectively identifiable tumor-initiating minority. Al-Hajj and colleagues shifted the question from whether breast tumors looked heterogeneous to whether that heterogeneity had causal structure. By sorting primary human breast cancer cells with surface markers and testing their tumor-forming capacity in immunocompromised mice, they showed that CD44+ CD24−/low Lineage− cells were highly enriched for tumor initiation, while much larger numbers of other tumor cells generally failed to regenerate tumors. The key change was methodological as much as conceptual: tumorigenic cells could now be isolated before the assay, not merely inferred afterward.

That made a new research program possible. Investigators could compare tumor-initiating and non-tumor-initiating populations from the same cancer, ask which signaling pathways sustained self-renewal, and test whether therapies spared or eliminated the cells most capable of regenerating disease. The finding helped move breast cancer biology away from a purely bulk-tumor view and toward models in which recurrence, metastasis, dormancy, and treatment resistance might depend disproportionately on specific cellular states. It also encouraged analogous searches for cancer stem-like cells in other solid tumors, using prospective markers and serial transplantation as functional tests.

Its influence also came with important qualifications that shaped later work. The xenograft assay measured tumor initiation under particular experimental conditions, and later studies showed that tumorigenic capacity can depend on mouse strain, microenvironment, tumor subtype, marker plasticity, and assay sensitivity. Even so, the paper mattered because it gave the solid-tumor cancer stem cell hypothesis a concrete experimental foothold in human breast cancer. Subsequent breakthroughs in lineage tracing, single-cell profiling, organoid culture, and targeted pathway studies refined the model rather than erasing its central lesson: clinically relevant cancers are not just masses of interchangeable malignant cells, and identifying the cells that can rebuild a tumor can change both biological explanation and therapeutic strategy.

## Abstract

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy in United States women, accounting for &gt;40,000 deaths each year. These breast tumors are comprised of phenotypically diverse populations of breast cancer cells. Using a model in which human breast cancer cells were grown in immunocompromised mice, we found that only a minority of breast cancer cells had the ability to form new tumors. We were able to distinguish the tumorigenic (tumor initiating) from the nontumorigenic cancer cells based on cell surface marker expression. We prospectively identified and isolated the tumorigenic cells as CD44 + CD24 −/low Lineage − in eight of nine patients. As few as 100 cells with this phenotype were able to form tumors in mice, whereas tens of thousands of cells with alternate phenotypes failed to form tumors. The tumorigenic subpopulation could be serially passaged: each time cells within this population generated new tumors containing additional CD44 + CD24 −/low Lineage − tumorigenic cells as well as the phenotypically diverse mixed populations of nontumorigenic cells present in the initial tumor. The ability to prospectively identify tumorigenic cancer cells will facilitate the elucidation of pathways that regulate their growth and survival. Furthermore, because these cells drive tumor development, strategies designed to target this population may lead to more effective therapies.
