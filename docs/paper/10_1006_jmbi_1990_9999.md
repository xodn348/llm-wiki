# Basic Local Alignment Search Tool

## Why this mattered

BLAST mattered because it turned sequence comparison from a specialized, computationally expensive analysis into a routine act of biological discovery. Earlier dynamic-programming methods such as Smith-Waterman could find optimal local alignments, but they were too slow for rapidly growing sequence databases. Altschul and colleagues introduced a heuristic strategy that searched for short high-scoring word matches and then extended them, preserving much of the biological usefulness of local alignment while making database-scale searches practical.

That shift changed what molecular biologists could do. A newly sequenced gene or protein could be compared quickly against known sequences, making it possible to infer homology, suggest function, identify conserved domains, and connect new experimental results to existing biological knowledge. BLAST helped make sequence databases active research instruments rather than passive archives: as GenBank and protein databases grew, their value increased because researchers had a fast way to query them.

Its influence carried into the genomics era. Large-scale genome sequencing, gene annotation, comparative genomics, metagenomics, pathogen identification, and evolutionary analysis all depended on fast similarity search as a basic operation. Later tools improved speed, statistics, indexing, sensitivity, or specialization, but BLAST established the practical paradigm: biological meaning could often be discovered by rapid, statistically interpretable comparison against the accumulated record of life’s sequences.
