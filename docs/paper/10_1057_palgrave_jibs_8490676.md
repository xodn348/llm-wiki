# The Internationalization Process of the Firm—A Model of Knowledge Development and Increasing Foreign Market Commitments

## Why this mattered

Johanson and Vahlne’s 1977 paper mattered because it made firm internationalization intelligible as a cumulative learning process rather than a one-time choice of foreign investment location or entry mode. Drawing on Swedish firm evidence and behavioral theories of the firm, it proposed that companies usually expand abroad through an interaction between **market knowledge** and **market commitment**: experience in a foreign market reduces uncertainty, which makes larger commitments more plausible, which in turn generates more experience. This became the core of the “Uppsala model,” giving international business research a process theory for why firms often begin with low-commitment modes, enter psychically closer markets first, and deepen involvement gradually.

The paradigm shift was methodological as well as theoretical. After this paper, researchers could study internationalization as a path-dependent sequence of decisions, not merely as an equilibrium outcome explained by ownership advantages, product cycles, or transaction costs. That opened a large empirical program on entry-mode progression, experiential knowledge, risk, distance, and commitment escalation, while also giving later scholars a clear baseline to test against. Its influence is visible both in extensions by Johanson and Vahlne themselves, especially the 2009 revision from “liability of foreignness” toward network “liability of outsidership,” and in critiques from research on born globals and international entrepreneurship, whose importance partly comes from showing when the gradual Uppsala process does not hold.
