# A Conceptual Model of Service Quality and Its Implications for Future Research

## Why this mattered

Parasuraman, Zeithaml, and Berry’s 1985 paper mattered because it reframed service quality as a distinct marketing and management problem rather than a simple extension of product quality. Earlier quality research had been shaped largely by tangible goods, where defects, specifications, and conformance could be more directly observed. This paper argued that services required a different conceptual apparatus because they are intangible, heterogeneous, produced and consumed simultaneously, and difficult to evaluate before purchase. Its central move was to define service quality through the gap between customers’ expectations and their perceptions of delivered service, making quality a relational and experiential judgment rather than only an operational outcome.

That shift made systematic research on services newly possible. By proposing the “gaps” model, the authors gave scholars and managers a way to diagnose where service failures arise: between customer expectations and managerial perceptions, between managerial perceptions and service specifications, between specifications and delivery, between delivery and external communication, and ultimately between expected and perceived service. This converted a diffuse managerial concern into a researchable framework with identifiable constructs, propositions, and points of intervention. It also helped establish services marketing as a field with its own theoretical foundations, rather than a secondary application of goods-dominant marketing.

The paper’s importance is also visible in what followed. It directly set the stage for the authors’ later SERVQUAL instrument, which operationalized the expectations-perceptions framework and became one of the most influential tools for measuring perceived service quality. Subsequent work debated, refined, criticized, and extended SERVQUAL, but those debates themselves depended on the 1985 paper’s core claim: that service quality could be modeled, measured, and managed through customers’ evaluative experience. In that sense, the paper did not merely add another model to marketing; it opened a durable research program connecting customer satisfaction, service design, relationship marketing, and service management.

## Abstract

The attainment of quality in products and services has become a pivotal concern of the 1980s. While quality in tangible goods has been described and measured by marketers, quality in services is largely undefined and unresearched. The authors attempt to rectify this situation by reporting the insights obtained in an extensive exploratory investigation of quality in four service businesses and by developing a model of service quality. Propositions and recommendations to stimulate future research about service quality are offered.
