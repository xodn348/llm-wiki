# A theory for multiresolution signal decomposition: the wavelet representation

## Why this mattered

Mallat’s paper mattered because it turned wavelets from a promising mathematical construction into a coherent signal-processing paradigm. Before this work, Fourier analysis gave a powerful global frequency description, while image and vision problems often required localized, scale-sensitive descriptions. Mallat formalized multiresolution analysis as a nested sequence of approximations and showed that the missing information between successive resolutions could be represented by an orthonormal wavelet basis. This made “scale” a first-class analytic dimension: signals and images could be decomposed into localized details at multiple resolutions, rather than forced into either purely spatial or purely frequency-domain descriptions.

The practical shift was equally important. The paper connected the theory to a pyramidal algorithm using convolutions with quadrature mirror filters, making wavelet decompositions computationally usable rather than merely abstract. For images, this gave a structured way to separate information by scale and orientation, which directly supported compression, texture analysis, edge-like feature representation, and fractal or self-similar structure analysis. In effect, the paper supplied both the mathematical language and the algorithmic machinery for multiscale image representation.

Its influence is visible in later breakthroughs across compression, denoising, computer vision, numerical analysis, and applied harmonic analysis. Wavelet-based image coding became central to standards such as JPEG 2000, and wavelet shrinkage helped establish modern statistical denoising methods. More broadly, Mallat’s multiresolution framework shaped how researchers thought about hierarchical representations of data: local filters, scale separation, and progressively coarser abstractions became standard tools, later echoed in scattering transforms and in parts of the conceptual lineage behind deep convolutional architectures.

## Abstract

Multiresolution representations are effective for analyzing the information content of images. The properties of the operator which approximates a signal at a given resolution were studied. It is shown that the difference of information between the approximation of a signal at the resolutions 2/sup j+1/ and 2/sup j/ (where j is an integer) can be extracted by decomposing this signal on a wavelet orthonormal basis of L/sup 2/(R/sup n/), the vector space of measurable, square-integrable n-dimensional functions. In L/sup 2/(R), a wavelet orthonormal basis is a family of functions which is built by dilating and translating a unique function psi (x). This decomposition defines an orthogonal multiresolution representation called a wavelet representation. It is computed with a pyramidal algorithm based on convolutions with quadrature mirror filters. Wavelet representation lies between the spatial and Fourier domains. For images, the wavelet representation differentiates several spatial orientations.
