# Classification of subtype of acute ischemic stroke. Definitions for use in a multicenter clinical trial. TOAST. Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment.

## Why this mattered

Before TOAST, acute ischemic stroke was often treated in trials and clinical reporting as a single clinical syndrome, even though its causes, recurrence risks, and likely treatment responses differed substantially. Adams and colleagues made a practical etiologic taxonomy usable at the bedside and across centers: large-artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, small-vessel occlusion, other determined cause, and undetermined cause, with explicit use of clinical findings plus diagnostic tests and a distinction between probable and possible diagnoses. The paradigm shift was not the discovery that stroke had multiple causes, but the conversion of that fact into a reproducible research language.

That common language made later stroke research more interpretable. Trials, registries, and cohort studies could stratify patients by mechanism rather than only by symptoms, severity, or infarct location. This mattered because therapies and prevention strategies increasingly depended on mechanism: anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation-related embolism, carotid intervention and antiplatelet/statin-centered prevention for large-artery disease, risk-factor control for lacunar stroke, and targeted workups for uncommon etiologies. TOAST therefore helped move stroke medicine from syndromic description toward mechanism-based diagnosis, prognosis, and secondary prevention.

Its influence also came from what it made measurable. By defining an “undetermined etiology” category, the system exposed the limits of contemporary diagnostic workups rather than hiding uncertainty. Subsequent advances in vascular imaging, cardiac monitoring, echocardiography, thrombophilia evaluation, and later embolic-stroke concepts could be evaluated against this baseline framework. Even when later classification systems refined or modified TOAST, they did so in a field that TOAST had helped organize: ischemic stroke as a set of mechanistically distinct diseases requiring subtype-aware trials and treatment decisions.

## Abstract

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: The etiology of ischemic stroke affects prognosis, outcome, and management. Trials of therapies for patients with acute stroke should include measurements of responses as influenced by subtype of ischemic stroke. A system for categorization of subtypes of ischemic stroke mainly based on etiology has been developed for the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST). METHODS: A classification of subtypes was prepared using clinical features and the results of ancillary diagnostic studies. "Possible" and "probable" diagnoses can be made based on the physician's certainty of diagnosis. The usefulness and interrater agreement of the classification were tested by two neurologists who had not participated in the writing of the criteria. The neurologists independently used the TOAST classification system in their bedside evaluation of 20 patients, first based only on clinical features and then after reviewing the results of diagnostic tests. RESULTS: The TOAST classification denotes five subtypes of ischemic stroke: 1) large-artery atherosclerosis, 2) cardioembolism, 3) small-vessel occlusion, 4) stroke of other determined etiology, and 5) stroke of undetermined etiology. Using this rating system, interphysician agreement was very high. The two physicians disagreed in only one patient. They were both able to reach a specific etiologic diagnosis in 11 patients, whereas the cause of stroke was not determined in nine. CONCLUSIONS: The TOAST stroke subtype classification system is easy to use and has good interobserver agreement. This system should allow investigators to report responses to treatment among important subgroups of patients with ischemic stroke. Clinical trials testing treatments for acute ischemic stroke should include similar methods to diagnose subtypes of stroke.
