# Neighborhoods and Violent Crime: A Multilevel Study of Collective Efficacy

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, neighborhood violence was often explained either by individual risk factors or by structural neighborhood conditions such as poverty, residential turnover, and family disruption. Sampson, Raudenbush, and Earls shifted the explanatory focus to a measurable social mechanism: “collective efficacy,” the combination of mutual trust and shared willingness to intervene for the common good. The key move was not simply to say that “community matters,” but to show, using multilevel data from 8,782 Chicago residents across 343 neighborhoods, that collective efficacy could be measured reliably at the neighborhood level and was strongly associated with lower violence even after accounting for individual characteristics, measurement error, prior violence, and structural disadvantage.

The paradigm shift was that neighborhood effects became empirically tractable as social processes rather than just demographic aggregates. Concentrated disadvantage and residential instability did not disappear from the explanation; instead, the paper showed that much of their association with violence operated through weakened collective efficacy. That made it possible to study why structurally similar neighborhoods could differ in violence and to connect macro-level inequality with everyday informal social control: neighbors watching public space, correcting youth behavior, and acting collectively when local order was threatened.

The paper helped redirect criminology, urban sociology, and public-health research toward mechanisms linking place to behavior. Subsequent work on neighborhood effects, informal social control, broken-windows policing debates, spatial criminology, and community-based violence prevention built on the possibility that local social organization could be measured independently of individual residents’ traits. Its influence also extended methodologically: it exemplified how survey data, administrative records, and multilevel modeling could be combined to distinguish individual composition from contextual neighborhood processes, making “neighborhood effects” a more rigorous empirical program rather than a loose ecological claim.

## Abstract

It is hypothesized that collective efficacy, defined as social cohesion among neighbors combined with their willingness to intervene on behalf of the common good, is linked to reduced violence. This hypothesis was tested on a 1995 survey of 8782 residents of 343 neighborhoods in Chicago, Illinois. Multilevel analyses showed that a measure of collective efficacy yields a high between-neighborhood reliability and is negatively associated with variations in violence, when individual-level characteristics, measurement error, and prior violence are controlled. Associations of concentrated disadvantage and residential instability with violence are largely mediated by collective efficacy.
