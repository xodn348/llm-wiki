# Use of Ranks in One-Criterion Variance Analysis

## Why this mattered

Kruskal and Wallis made rank-based inference a practical general method for comparing more than two independent samples. The key shift was to replace assumptions about normal measurements and equal-variance parametric analysis with a procedure based on the relative ordering of observations. By extending the logic of rank tests to the one-factor, multi-sample setting, the paper gave statisticians a usable nonparametric analogue of one-way ANOVA: a test that could be applied when measurements were ordinal, skewed, contaminated by outliers, or otherwise poorly described by the Gaussian model.

What became newly possible was routine distribution-free comparison across several groups without committing to a full parametric model for the populations. The statistic \(H\), computed from sums of ranks and approximated by a chi-square distribution with \(C-1\) degrees of freedom, turned a simple ordering operation into an inferential tool suitable for experimental and observational data. This helped normalize the idea that validity could come from invariance under ranks rather than from strong distributional assumptions, a central theme in mid-century nonparametric statistics.

The paper’s influence is visible in the later expansion of robust and rank-based methods across the sciences: nonparametric multiple comparisons, extensions for blocked designs and repeated measures, and modern robust workflows that treat parametric ANOVA as only one option among several. The Kruskal-Wallis test did not replace analysis of variance; instead, it changed the default statistical vocabulary by making it legitimate and convenient to ask ANOVA-like questions when the data scale or distribution made classical ANOVA fragile.

## Abstract

Abstract Given C samples, with n i observations in the ith sample, a test of the hypothesis that the samples are from the same population may be made by ranking the observations from from 1 to Σn i (giving each observation in a group of ties the mean of the ranks tied for), finding the C sums of ranks, and computing a statistic H. Under the stated hypothesis, H is distributed approximately as χ2(C – 1), unless the samples are too small, in which case special approximations or exact tables are provided. One of the most important applications of the test is in detecting differences among the population means.Footnote* * Based in part on research supported by the Office of Naval Research at the Statistical Research Center, University of Chicago.
