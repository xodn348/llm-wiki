# Integrated genomic analyses of ovarian carcinoma

## Why this mattered

This paper helped shift ovarian cancer from a disease classified mainly by histology and clinical stage to one understood through integrated molecular architecture. By analyzing copy number, exome sequence, mRNA, microRNA, and methylation data across hundreds of high-grade serous ovarian carcinomas, TCGA showed that this common and lethal ovarian cancer subtype was not driven by a long list of frequent point mutations. Instead, it was dominated by near-universal TP53 mutation, widespread chromosomal instability, recurrent copy-number alterations, and pathway-level defects, especially in homologous recombination. That reframed the disease as a genomically chaotic cancer in which therapeutic opportunities often lay in DNA repair state, amplification events, and expression programs rather than in single high-frequency oncogenic mutations.

The most consequential result was the estimate that roughly half of tumors had defects in homologous recombination. This gave genomic weight to a clinical idea already emerging from BRCA1/2 biology: that many high-grade serous ovarian cancers might be vulnerable to DNA-damage response targeting. The paper therefore helped provide a population-scale rationale for later PARP inhibitor development, biomarker strategies based on “BRCAness” or homologous recombination deficiency, and more systematic use of germline and somatic BRCA testing in ovarian cancer care. It also clarified that CCNE1-amplified tumors formed a biologically distinct, often mutually exclusive group from BRCA-altered tumors, shaping later efforts to separate ovarian cancer into therapeutically meaningful molecular subsets.

More broadly, the study became a reference model for cancer genomics: not simply sequencing tumors, but integrating multiple molecular layers to define subtypes, pathway dependencies, and survival-associated signatures. Its transcriptional, methylation, microRNA, and copy-number analyses did not immediately yield a simple taxonomy equivalent to hormone-receptor subtyping in breast cancer, but they made clear why ovarian cancer had resisted one-size-fits-all treatment. Subsequent breakthroughs in maintenance therapy, HRD testing, resistance biology, and molecular trial design all built on the central lesson of this paper: high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma is best understood as a pathway- and genome-instability disease, not as a cancer defined by a small set of common actionable mutations.

## Abstract

A catalogue of molecular aberrations that cause ovarian cancer is critical for developing and deploying therapies that will improve patients’ lives. The Cancer Genome Atlas project has analysed messenger RNA expression, microRNA expression, promoter methylation and DNA copy number in 489 high-grade serous ovarian adenocarcinomas and the DNA sequences of exons from coding genes in 316 of these tumours. Here we report that high-grade serous ovarian cancer is characterized by TP53 mutations in almost all tumours (96%); low prevalence but statistically recurrent somatic mutations in nine further genes including NF1, BRCA1, BRCA2, RB1 and CDK12; 113 significant focal DNA copy number aberrations; and promoter methylation events involving 168 genes. Analyses delineated four ovarian cancer transcriptional subtypes, three microRNA subtypes, four promoter methylation subtypes and a transcriptional signature associated with survival duration, and shed new light on the impact that tumours with BRCA1/2 (BRCA1 or BRCA2) and CCNE1 aberrations have on survival. Pathway analyses suggested that homologous recombination is defective in about half of the tumours analysed, and that NOTCH and FOXM1 signalling are involved in serous ovarian cancer pathophysiology. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project reports here its analysis of messenger RNA and microRNA expression, promoter methylation, DNA copy number and exome sequences in 489 high-grade serous ovarian adenocarcinomas. The analyses help establish new tumour subtypes. Among other insights is the finding that while the gene encoding p53 tumour suppressor is mutated in almost all tumours, nine other loci including NF1, BRCA1, BRCA2, RB1 and CDK12 carry recurrent albeit low-prevalence mutations. Homologous recombination is defective in about half of the tumours studied, and Notch and FOXM1 signalling are involved in the pathophysiology.
