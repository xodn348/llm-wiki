# Primer-Directed Enzymatic Amplification of DNA with a Thermostable DNA Polymerase

## Why this mattered

This paper turned PCR from a powerful but cumbersome idea into a practical, general-purpose laboratory method. Earlier primer-directed amplification required repeated manual intervention because the polymerase was destroyed by the high-temperature denaturation step. By introducing a thermostable DNA polymerase from *Thermus aquaticus*, Saiki and colleagues made cyclic DNA amplification automatable, more specific, and much more robust. The shift was not merely technical convenience: it converted selective copying of DNA into a repeatable exponential process that could amplify single-copy genomic sequences by more than ten million-fold while preserving target specificity.

What became newly possible was the reliable detection, analysis, and manipulation of tiny amounts of DNA. The paper showed amplification of fragments up to 2,000 base pairs and detection of a target molecule present only once among DNA from 100,000 cells, making PCR useful for problems where the relevant sequence was rare, degraded, or embedded in overwhelming background DNA. This changed molecular biology’s practical unit of work: researchers no longer needed abundant purified DNA to ask sequence-specific questions. A chosen locus could be made experimentally visible from minute starting material.

That capability underwrote much of modern genomics and molecular diagnostics. Thermostable-polymerase PCR became foundational for genetic disease testing, infectious-disease detection, forensic DNA analysis, ancient DNA studies, cloning, sequencing workflows, and later quantitative and real-time PCR methods. Subsequent breakthroughs such as high-throughput sequencing, clinical viral-load monitoring, and rapid pathogen assays did not replace this paradigm so much as build on its central insight: targeted enzymatic amplification can make otherwise inaccessible genetic information abundant enough to measure, compare, and act on.

## Abstract

A thermostable DNA polymerase was used in an in vitro DNA amplification procedure, the polymerase chain reaction. The enzyme, isolated from Thermus aquaticus, greatly simplifies the procedure and, by enabling the amplification reaction to be performed at higher temperatures, significantly improves the specificity, yield, sensitivity, and length of products that can be amplified. Single-copy genomic sequences were amplified by a factor of more than 10 million with very high specificity, and DNA segments up to 2000 base pairs were readily amplified. In addition, the method was used to amplify and detect a target DNA molecule present only once in a sample of 10(5) cells.
