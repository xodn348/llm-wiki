# Atomic Force Microscope

## Why this mattered

Binnig, Quate, and Gerber’s 1986 paper mattered because it generalized the scanning-probe revolution beyond conducting surfaces. The scanning tunneling microscope had shown that a sharp probe could map surfaces with atomic-scale sensitivity, but its tunneling signal required electrical conductivity. The atomic force microscope replaced tunneling current with force detection, making it possible in principle to image insulators, semiconductors, biological materials, polymers, ceramics, and other systems that could not be studied directly by STM. The key paradigm shift was that atomic-scale microscopy no longer had to depend on electrons transmitted through a specimen or tunneling between conductors; it could be based on local mechanical interaction between a tip and a surface.

The paper also reframed microscopy as a platform for measuring interactions, not only forming images. By proposing force sensitivity down to extremely small scales and demonstrating sub-angstrom vertical resolution in air, it pointed toward instruments that could operate outside high-vacuum electron-microscope conditions and interrogate surfaces under more ordinary environments. That made AFM unusually broad: it could map topography, but later variants also measured adhesion, elasticity, friction, electrostatic forces, magnetic forces, and chemical or molecular recognition. The non-destructive probe emphasized in the paper was especially important for soft matter and biological samples, where conventional high-resolution methods often required harsh preparation.

Subsequent breakthroughs built directly on this change in what counted as a measurable microscopic signal. AFM became central to nanoscience and surface science, enabling atomic and near-atomic characterization of nonconductors, manipulation and lithography at nanometer scales, high-resolution studies of DNA and proteins, and quantitative nanomechanical measurements. Its influence was not just a new instrument, but a new experimental style: scanning a local probe over matter and converting local forces into maps of structure and properties. That style helped make nanoscale materials research a practical laboratory discipline rather than a narrow extension of electron microscopy or tunneling microscopy.

## Abstract

The scanning tunneling microscope is proposed as a method to measure forces as small as ${10}^{\ensuremath{-}18}$ N. As one application for this concept, we introduce a new type of microscope capable of investigating surfaces of insulators on an atomic scale. The atomic force microscope is a combination of the principles of the scanning tunneling microscope and the stylus profilometer. It incorporates a probe that does not damage the surface. Our preliminary results in air demonstrate a lateral resolution of 30 \AA{}A and a vertical resolution less than 1 \AA{}.
