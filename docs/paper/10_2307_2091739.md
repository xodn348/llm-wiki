# The Social Construction of Reality: A Treatise in the Sociology of Knowledge.

## Why this mattered

*The Social Construction of Reality* mattered because it made everyday “common-sense knowledge” a central object of sociological theory. Berger and Luckmann shifted attention from knowledge as mainly formal ideology, science, or elite doctrine to the taken-for-granted categories through which ordinary people experience institutions, roles, norms, and social facts as real. Its paradigm shift was to treat society as a dialectical process: human beings produce social worlds through interaction, those worlds become objectified as institutions, and individuals internalize them through socialization.

That framework made it newly possible to study institutions, professions, identities, deviance, religion, gender, and expertise as historically produced realities rather than merely natural or given structures. It helped open paths later developed in social constructivism, symbolic interactionism, ethnomethodology, discourse analysis, science and technology studies, and constructivist approaches in anthropology, history, and philosophy. Subsequent breakthroughs in the study of categories, classification, institutional legitimacy, and the social production of expertise drew on the book’s core insight: what counts as reality in social life is stabilized through shared meanings, routines, and legitimating narratives, not simply discovered or imposed.

## Abstract

A general and systematic account of the role of knowledge in society aimed to stimulate both critical discussion and empirical investigations. This book is concerned with the sociology of 'everything that passes for knowledge in society'. It focuses particularly on that 'common-sense knowledge' which constitutes the reality of everyday life for the ordinary member of society. The authors are concerned to present an analysis of knowledge in everyday life in the context of a theory of society as a dialectical process between objective and subjective reality. Their development of a theory of institutions, legitimations and socializations has implications beyond the discipline of sociology, and their 'humanistic' approach has considerable relevance for other social scientists, historians, philosophers and anthropologists.
