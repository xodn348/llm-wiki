# Improved patch-clamp techniques for high-resolution current recording from cells and cell-free membrane patches

## Why this mattered

Before this work, membrane currents were usually measured as ensemble behavior across whole cells or tissues. Hamill, Marty, Neher, Sakmann, and Sigworth turned patch clamp from a difficult single-channel demonstration into a general experimental platform: gigaohm seals reduced background noise, improved circuitry increased temporal resolution, and excised or whole-cell configurations made it possible to control membrane voltage and solution composition with unusual precision. The practical consequence was a shift from inferring ion-channel behavior indirectly to recording the opening and closing of individual channels in real time.

That change made ion channels experimentally tractable as discrete molecular machines. Researchers could now measure unitary conductance, gating kinetics, drug effects, ligand activation, and voltage dependence in native membranes and in isolated patches, while whole-cell patch clamp extended high-resolution recording to small cells that had been inaccessible to classical microelectrode methods. The technique helped connect molecular physiology to cellular excitability across neurons, muscle, endocrine cells, and sensory systems.

Its influence also reached beyond electrophysiological method. Patch clamp became a central tool for identifying channel subtypes, linking channel mutations to disease, characterizing synaptic and secretory mechanisms, and validating cloned ion-channel genes as molecular biology advanced in the 1980s and 1990s. The 1991 Nobel Prize to Neher and Sakmann recognized this broader transformation: after this paper, electrical signaling could be studied at the level of single proteins rather than averaged membranes.
