# False-Positive Psychology

## Why this mattered

*False-Positive Psychology* mattered because it turned a diffuse methodological worry into a concrete, testable mechanism for producing unreliable science. Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn showed that ordinary “researcher degrees of freedom” such as optional stopping, trying multiple dependent variables, adding covariates, or selectively reporting studies could make a nominal *p* < .05 standard behave nothing like a 5% false-positive safeguard. Its force came from demonstrating that no fraud or exotic statistical error was required: accepted, routine analytic flexibility could manufacture significant evidence for effects that were not real.

The paper helped shift psychology’s credibility crisis from a problem of individual bad studies to a problem of research systems and incentives. By pairing simulations with deliberately absurd experimental demonstrations, it made clear that publication practices rewarding significant findings could systematically favor false positives. Its proposed disclosure rules also helped make previously hidden analytic choices visible, creating a practical bridge from critique to reform.

After this paper, stronger norms around preregistration, reporting all measures and exclusions, distinguishing confirmatory from exploratory analysis, and scrutinizing researcher flexibility became much harder to dismiss as bureaucratic preferences. It directly anticipated and reinforced later breakthroughs in open science, including registered reports, large-scale replication projects, and routine data/materials transparency. In that sense, the paper was paradigm-shifting not because it introduced a new statistical test, but because it changed what counted as adequate evidence: a significant result alone was no longer enough without knowing how many paths could have led there.

## Abstract

In this article, we accomplish two things. First, we show that despite empirical psychologists' nominal endorsement of a low rate of false-positive findings (≤ .05), flexibility in data collection, analysis, and reporting dramatically increases actual false-positive rates. In many cases, a researcher is more likely to falsely find evidence that an effect exists than to correctly find evidence that it does not. We present computer simulations and a pair of actual experiments that demonstrate how unacceptably easy it is to accumulate (and report) statistically significant evidence for a false hypothesis. Second, we suggest a simple, low-cost, and straightforwardly effective disclosure-based solution to this problem. The solution involves six concrete requirements for authors and four guidelines for reviewers, all of which impose a minimal burden on the publication process.
