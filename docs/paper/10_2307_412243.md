# A simplest systematics for the organization of turn-taking for conversation

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, conversational order could look too fluid and context-bound to admit a formal account. Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson made a paradigm-shifting move: they treated ordinary conversation as an organized system with discoverable rules, not as noise around “real” linguistic structure. Their turn-taking model showed how speakers coordinate with minimal gaps and overlaps through local, participant-managed procedures: recognizing possible turn completions, selecting next speakers, or allowing self-selection. This made the timing and distribution of talk analytically tractable without reducing conversation to scripts, intentions, or external social roles.

What became newly possible was a rigorous study of interaction as its own domain of order. The paper helped establish conversation analysis as a method capable of explaining how social action is built turn by turn, using recordings and transcripts rather than invented examples or broad sociological categories. It also reframed language as fundamentally recipient-designed and sequential: utterances matter not only for what they mean, but for where they occur, what they make relevant next, and how others respond.

Its influence runs through later work on adjacency pairs, repair, preference organization, institutional talk, discourse analysis, pragmatics, and interactional linguistics. More broadly, it anticipated problems that remain central in human-computer interaction and spoken dialogue systems: when to speak, when to yield, how to detect completion, and how to coordinate action in real time. The paper mattered because it showed that one of the most ordinary human activities, conversation, has a machinery precise enough to study systematically and general enough to reshape multiple fields.

## Abstract

The organization of taking turns to talk is fundamental to conversation, as well as to other speech-exchange systems. A model for the turn-taking organization for conversation is proposed, and is examined for its compatibility with a list of grossly observable facts about conversation. The results of the examination suggest that, at least, a model for turn-taking in conversation will be characterized as locally managed, party-administered, interactionally controlled, and sensitive to recipient design. Several general consequences of the model are explicated, and contrasts are sketched with turn-taking organizations for other speech-exchange systems.
