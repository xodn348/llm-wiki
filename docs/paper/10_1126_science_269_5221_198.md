# Observation of Bose-Einstein Condensation in a Dilute Atomic Vapor

## Why this mattered

This paper marked the first clear observation of Bose-Einstein condensation in a dilute atomic gas, turning a long-standing prediction of quantum statistical mechanics into an experimentally accessible system. Bose and Einstein had predicted in the 1920s that bosons could macroscopically occupy a single quantum state at sufficiently low temperature, but for decades the phenomenon remained difficult to realize in weakly interacting atomic vapors. Anderson, Ensher, Matthews, Wieman, and Cornell showed that magnetic trapping plus evaporative cooling could bring rubidium-87 atoms to nanokelvin temperatures where a condensate fraction appeared, with signatures tied directly to momentum distribution, abrupt growth below a critical temperature, and the anisotropic ground-state shape of the trap.

The paradigm shift was not only that Bose-Einstein condensation had been observed, but that it had been observed in a controllable, dilute, weakly interacting atomic system. That made the condensate a new laboratory object: coherent matter waves could be created, imaged, manipulated, split, interfered, and probed with a level of control unavailable in liquid helium or other dense quantum fluids. After this work, ultracold atomic gases became a central platform for studying macroscopic quantum coherence, superfluidity, vortices, collective excitations, atom lasers, low-dimensional gases, and tunable interactions.

The paper helped launch the modern field of ultracold quantum gases. Closely related experiments soon produced condensates in other atomic species and enabled increasingly sophisticated control with optical traps, optical lattices, and Feshbach resonances. Those tools later connected Bose-Einstein condensates to quantum simulation, precision measurement, strongly correlated many-body physics, and studies of the BEC-BCS crossover in fermionic gases. In that sense, the 1995 result did more than confirm a prediction: it opened a practical route to engineering quantum matter from nearly first principles.

## Abstract

A Bose-Einstein condensate was produced in a vapor of rubidium-87 atoms that was confined by magnetic fields and evaporatively cooled. The condensate fraction first appeared near a temperature of 170 nanokelvin and a number density of 2.5 x 10(12) per cubic centimeter and could be preserved for more than 15 seconds. Three primary signatures of Bose-Einstein condensation were seen. (i) On top of a broad thermal velocity distribution, a narrow peak appeared that was centered at zero velocity. (ii) The fraction of the atoms that were in this low-velocity peak increased abruptly as the sample temperature was lowered. (iii) The peak exhibited a nonthermal, anisotropic velocity distribution expected of the minimum-energy quantum state of the magnetic trap in contrast to the isotropic, thermal velocity distribution observed in the broad uncondensed fraction.
