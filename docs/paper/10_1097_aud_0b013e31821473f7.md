# Praat: Doing Phonetics by Computer

## Why this mattered

Praat mattered because it turned acoustic phonetics from a specialist, lab-bound signal-processing practice into a widely reproducible computational workflow. The 2011 *Ear and Hearing* resource review highlighted a single free package that combined recording, signal editing, acoustic analysis, speech synthesis, stimulus presentation, data collection, statistics, and scripting, making it possible for researchers, clinicians, and students to inspect and measure speech with a common toolchain rather than bespoke local software ([Ear and Hearing](https://journals.lww.com/ear-hearing/fulltext/2011/03000/praat__doing_phonetics_by_computer.12.aspx), [Praat](https://praat.org/praat)).

Its paradigm shift was infrastructural: Praat did not propose a new theory of speech, but it changed what counted as practically measurable. Formants, pitch tracks, voice onset time, intensity, segmentation, and perceptual stimuli could be generated and audited at scale, with scripts making analyses repeatable across speakers, languages, and laboratories. That standardization helped make acoustic evidence routine in phonology, sociophonetics, second-language acquisition, hearing research, clinical voice work, and speech perception.

Later breakthroughs in corpus phonetics, forced alignment, and speech-technology evaluation built on this normalization of speech as analyzable data. Tools such as automatic aligners and neural speech models expanded scale and automation, but Praat remained the common measurement, annotation, and validation layer against which many such systems were trained, checked, or interpreted. Its lasting importance is therefore not just citation volume, but the way it made computational phonetics a default method rather than a specialized exception.
