# Robust Locally Weighted Regression and Smoothing Scatterplots

## Why this mattered

Cleveland’s 1979 paper helped turn smoothing from a primarily parametric modeling problem into an exploratory visual instrument. The key shift was local: instead of forcing a single global functional form on a scatterplot, the method estimated the mean response near each target point using nearby observations, distance-based weights, and a low-degree local polynomial. The robust iteration was equally important, because it made the displayed trend less vulnerable to isolated deviant observations. This gave analysts a practical way to see nonlinear structure, curvature, changing slopes, and departures from simple regression assumptions without first committing to a mechanistic model.

What became newly possible was a routine, computationally feasible form of model-free regression for data analysis. The paper framed smoothing as something that could be added directly to scatterplots, making statistical graphics more diagnostic and less merely descriptive. In that sense, LOWESS/LOESS became part of the grammar of exploratory data analysis: a curve could summarize the conditional pattern while preserving the original points and their irregularities. This influenced not just statistical practice but software defaults, since later systems could offer scatterplot smoothers as ordinary interactive or plotting features rather than specialized research tools.

Its later importance is visible in the broader movement toward flexible, data-adaptive modeling. LOESS became a standard nonparametric regression baseline and a building block in statistical graphics, environmental statistics, epidemiology, econometrics, and signal-like observational data analysis. It also anticipated themes that became central in later breakthroughs: local estimation, robustness, bandwidth/span selection, bias-variance tradeoffs, and the use of computation to replace rigid closed-form assumptions. While later methods such as generalized additive models, splines, kernel regression, tree-based models, and modern machine-learning regressors developed different machinery, Cleveland’s paper made the core idea culturally and practically durable: let the data reveal smooth structure locally, visibly, and robustly.

## Abstract

Abstract The visual information on a scatterplot can be greatly enhanced, with little additional cost, by computing and plotting smoothed points. Robust locally weighted regression is a method for smoothing a scatterplot, (x i , y i ), i = 1, …, n, in which the fitted value at z k is the value of a polynomial fit to the data using weighted least squares, where the weight for (x i , y i ) is large if x i is close to x k and small if it is not. A robust fitting procedure is used that guards against deviant points distorting the smoothed points. Visual, computational, and statistical issues of robust locally weighted regression are discussed. Several examples, including data on lead intoxication, are used to illustrate the methodology.
