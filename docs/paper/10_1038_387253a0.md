# The value of the world's ecosystem services and natural capital

## Why this mattered

[Costanza et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/387253a0) mattered because it converted “nature’s services” from a largely qualitative ecological idea into a global accounting problem. By estimating the annual value of 17 ecosystem services across 16 biomes at an average of about US$33 trillion, with a range of US$16-54 trillion, the paper made a deliberately provocative comparison: ecosystems were contributing services on the same order as, and by their estimate larger than, the world’s gross national product at the time. The paradigm shift was not that wetlands, forests, oceans, and soils suddenly had market prices; it was that their life-support functions could be treated as forms of natural capital whose loss should appear in economic reasoning rather than as an invisible externality.

Its influence came partly from its imperfections. The authors used benefit transfer methods, acknowledged large uncertainties, and described the estimate as a minimum rather than a precise price tag. Those limitations made the paper a target for criticism, but also made clear what a new research program would need: better ecological production functions, spatial valuation, uncertainty analysis, and institutions capable of comparing built, human, and natural capital. After 1997, ecosystem-service valuation became a practical bridge between ecology, economics, conservation planning, and public policy.

The paper helped prepare the ground for later syntheses and policy frameworks, including the [Millennium Ecosystem Assessment](https://www.millenniumassessment.org/en/Framework.aspx), TEEB, and national natural-capital accounting efforts. Its line of descent is visible in the 2021 adoption of [SEEA Ecosystem Accounting](https://seea.un.org/ecosystem-accounting) as an international statistical standard: the basic claim that ecosystems provide measurable contributions to human well-being had moved from a controversial global estimate into formal accounting practice. In that sense, the paper’s lasting breakthrough was not the US$33 trillion figure itself, but the reframing of environmental degradation as depreciation of productive capital.
