# Self-organized criticality: An explanation of the 1/<i>f</i>noise

## Why this mattered

Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld’s 1987 paper gave a compact mechanism for why scale-free behavior can arise without external tuning. Before this work, criticality was usually associated with carefully adjusted control parameters, such as temperature at a phase transition. The paper proposed that extended driven dissipative systems could instead evolve *by their own dynamics* toward a critical state, where events occur across many sizes and timescales. This reframed flicker, or `1/f`, noise from a collection of unrelated empirical anomalies into a possible signature of generic nonequilibrium organization.

The paradigm shift was conceptual as much as technical: it made critical phenomena portable beyond equilibrium statistical physics. The sandpile model supplied a simple language for avalanches, thresholds, long-range correlations, and power-law distributions in systems with many interacting parts. After this paper, researchers had a new template for studying earthquakes, forest fires, biological evolution, neural activity, economic fluctuations, and other systems where small local changes can sometimes produce system-wide events. Not all later applications survived scrutiny in their strongest form, but the paper made it newly plausible to ask whether scale invariance in nature reflected internal dynamics rather than fine-tuned external conditions.

Its influence also runs through later work on complex systems, nonequilibrium phase transitions, avalanche dynamics, and networked cascades. The idea of self-organized criticality helped normalize the search for universal behavior in messy, driven systems, and it connected fractal structure, temporal noise spectra, and bursty event statistics within one framework. Even where later breakthroughs refined, limited, or replaced the original sandpile picture, they often did so using questions this paper made central: how do local rules generate scale-free collective behavior, and when does apparent criticality require tuning, feedback, or conservation laws?

## Abstract

We show that dynamical systems with spatial degrees of freedom naturally evolve into a self-organized critical point. Flicker noise, or 1/f noise, can be identified with the dynamics of the critical state. This picture also yields insight into the origin of fractal objects.
