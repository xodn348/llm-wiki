# Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance

## Why this mattered

North’s argument mattered because it made institutions an endogenous object of economic analysis rather than a vague background condition. By defining institutions as “humanly devised constraints” distinct from organizations, and by tying them to transaction costs, uncertainty, incentives, and production possibilities, the work gave economists and political scientists a tractable vocabulary for explaining why the same technologies, resources, or formal market rules could produce sharply different outcomes across societies. The paradigm shift was not simply that institutions mattered; it was that institutional rules, enforcement, belief systems, and organizations could be analyzed as part of the causal machinery of long-run economic performance.

The paper also helped make historical persistence analytically central. Standard neoclassical reasoning implied that inefficient arrangements should be competed away, but North’s framework explained why they often survive: institutional change is incremental, filtered through organizations’ incentives, entrepreneurs’ perceptions, imperfect information, and inherited mental models. This made path dependence a core explanation for divergent development trajectories, allowing scholars to study stagnation, growth, and reform failure without assuming that societies automatically converge toward efficient rules.

After this work, later breakthroughs in new institutional economics, political economy, development economics, and economic history could ask sharper questions: why some states protect property rights while others extract rents; why formal reforms fail when informal constraints remain unchanged; why credible commitment, enforcement capacity, and political order shape markets. Its influence is visible in subsequent work on institutions and growth, colonial origins of development, governance, and state capacity, all of which build on the paper’s central move: economic performance cannot be understood apart from the institutional structure that shapes incentives through time.

## Abstract

Examines the role that institutions, defined as the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction, play in economic performance and how those institutions change and how a model of dynamic institutions explains the differential performance of economies through time. Institutions are separate from organizations, which are assemblages of people directed to strategically operating within institutional constraints. Institutions affect the economy by influencing, together with technology, transaction and production costs. They do this by reducing uncertainty in human interaction, albeit not always efficiently. Entrepreneurs accomplish incremental changes in institutions by perceiving opportunities to do better through altering the institutional framework of political and economic organizations. Importantly, the ability to perceive these opportunities depends on both the completeness of information and the mental constructs used to process that information. Thus, institutions and entrepreneurs stand in a symbiotic relationship where each gives feedback to the other. Neoclassical economics suggests that inefficient institutions ought to be rapidly replaced. This symbiotic relationship helps explain why this theoretical consequence is often not observed: while this relationship allows growth, it also allows inefficient institutions to persist. The author identifies changes in relative prices and prevailing ideas as the source of institutional alterations. Transaction costs, however, may keep relative price changes from being fully exploited. Transaction costs are influenced by institutions and institutional development is accordingly path-dependent.
