# Click Chemistry: Diverse Chemical Function from a Few Good Reactions

## Why this mattered

Kolb, Finn, and Sharpless reframed synthesis around reliability rather than maximal structural novelty. The paper argued that many useful molecules did not need to be assembled through difficult carbon-carbon bond construction; instead, chemists could build complexity quickly by joining modular fragments through robust carbon-heteroatom linkages. Its criteria for “click” reactions: high yielding, selective, broad in scope, tolerant of water and oxygen, simple to perform, and easy to purify, made reaction choice a design principle. That was the paradigm shift: synthesis could be treated less like bespoke craftsmanship and more like a modular engineering platform.

This made it newly practical to generate large libraries, attach probes to biomolecules, functionalize surfaces and polymers, and connect molecular fragments under conditions compatible with complex systems. Although the 2001 paper defined a broader philosophy, the copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition soon became its emblematic reaction because it converted inert functional handles into stable triazoles with exceptional selectivity. That reaction helped make chemical biology, materials functionalization, drug discovery, and bioconjugation more systematic, because researchers could install small “handles” first and decide later what function to attach.

The paper’s influence also lies in how it set the stage for later bioorthogonal chemistry. Click chemistry supplied the conceptual demand for reactions that are fast, selective, and compatible with biological environments; subsequent work, including strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition and other bioorthogonal ligations, extended that demand into living systems without copper toxicity. In that sense, the 2001 article did not merely name a class of reactions. It changed what chemists expected from a useful reaction: not just that it could make a bond, but that it could do so predictably, modularly, and in settings where traditional synthesis had been too fragile or too slow.

## Abstract

Examination of nature's favorite molecules reveals a striking preference for making carbon–heteroatom bonds over carbon–carbon bonds—surely no surprise given that carbon dioxide is nature's starting material and that most reactions are performed in water. Nucleic acids, proteins, and polysaccharides are condensation polymers of small subunits stitched together by carbon–heteroatom bonds. Even the 35 or so building blocks from which these crucial molecules are made each contain, at most, six contiguous C−C bonds, except for the three aromatic amino acids. Taking our cue from nature's approach, we address here the development of a set of powerful, highly reliable, and selective reactions for the rapid synthesis of useful new compounds and combinatorial libraries through heteroatom links (C−X−C), an approach we call "click chemistry". Click chemistry is at once defined, enabled, and constrained by a handful of nearly perfect "spring-loaded" reactions. The stringent criteria for a process to earn click chemistry status are described along with examples of the molecular frameworks that are easily made using this spartan, but powerful, synthetic strategy.
