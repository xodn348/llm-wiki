# A method for registration of 3-D shapes

## Why this mattered

Besl and McKay’s paper mattered because it turned 3-D registration from a collection of representation-specific alignment tricks into a general algorithmic primitive. The key shift was the formulation of registration as repeated closest-point matching followed by rigid-motion least-squares fitting, requiring only the ability to compute closest points on the target shape. That made the same method applicable to point sets, curves, surfaces, and geometric models, and gave practitioners a practical way to align sensed 3-D data with ideal CAD-like models across all six rigid degrees of freedom. Its convergence guarantee was local rather than global, but the paper was unusually clear about that limitation: ICP monotonically descends to the nearest local minimum, and broader search over initial poses is needed for global registration.

What became newly possible was reliable post-acquisition alignment of real 3-D measurements with models or with other scans. This was central for automated shape inspection, but its influence quickly spread to robotics, computer vision, medical imaging, reverse engineering, range scanning, and later 3-D reconstruction pipelines. ICP supplied a simple, reusable registration backbone at a time when 3-D sensing was becoming practical but fragmented by sensor type and shape representation. Because the method was modular, later work could improve individual components: faster nearest-neighbor search, point-to-plane error metrics, robust weighting, outlier rejection, multi-resolution schemes, probabilistic variants, and global initialization methods.

Its downstream importance is that many later breakthroughs in 3-D perception assumed some version of ICP as either a baseline, refinement step, or conceptual template. Dense SLAM, scan matching for mobile robots, surface reconstruction from multiple views, model-based object pose estimation, and modern RGB-D/point-cloud systems all depend on solving variants of the alignment problem that this paper made canonical. Even when newer methods use feature descriptors, learned correspondences, or global optimization, they often still refine with ICP-like local registration. The paper’s paradigm shift was therefore not merely a better algorithm, but the establishment of rigid 3-D shape registration as a standard computational operation.

## Abstract

The authors describe a general-purpose, representation-independent method for the accurate and computationally efficient registration of 3-D shapes including free-form curves and surfaces. The method handles the full six degrees of freedom and is based on the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm, which requires only a procedure to find the closest point on a geometric entity to a given point. The ICP algorithm always converges monotonically to the nearest local minimum of a mean-square distance metric, and the rate of convergence is rapid during the first few iterations. Therefore, given an adequate set of initial rotations and translations for a particular class of objects with a certain level of 'shape complexity', one can globally minimize the mean-square distance metric over all six degrees of freedom by testing each initial registration. One important application of this method is to register sensed data from unfixtured rigid objects with an ideal geometric model, prior to shape inspection.
