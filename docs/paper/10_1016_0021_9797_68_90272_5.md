# Controlled growth of monodisperse silica spheres in the micron size range

## Why this mattered

Before Stöber, Fink, and Bohn, silica particles could be made, but not with this degree of routine control over size, shape, and uniformity. Their 1968 paper introduced a simple solution-phase route: hydrolysis of alkyl silicates followed by condensation of silicic acid in alcoholic solution, with ammonia acting as a morphological catalyst. The key shift was practical reproducibility: spherical silica particles of uniform size could be grown in suspension from below 0.05 μm to about 2 μm, turning monodisperse silica from a difficult specialty material into a standard laboratory platform ([JCIS, 1968](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9797(68)90272-5)).

That control mattered because monodisperse spheres are not just particles; they are calibration objects, model colloids, templates, supports, and building blocks. The “Stöber process” made it possible to study colloidal crystallization, packing, scattering, sedimentation, and interparticle forces with particles whose size distribution was no longer the dominant uncertainty. It also gave materials chemists a clean silica surface that could be functionalized, coated, hollowed, etched, or used as a sacrificial template.

Its influence is visible in later breakthroughs across sol-gel chemistry, core-shell nanoparticles, mesoporous silica, photonic crystals, drug delivery carriers, catalysis supports, and biomedical nanomaterials. Many of those fields depended on the same conceptual advance: particle synthesis became programmable enough that size, morphology, and surface chemistry could be treated as design variables rather than accidents of precipitation. The paper’s more than 14,000 citations reflect that it did not merely improve a preparation; it supplied a general experimental grammar for colloidal materials engineering.
