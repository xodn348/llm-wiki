# New Method for High-Accuracy Determination of the Fine-Structure Constant Based on Quantized Hall Resistance

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it turned the Hall effect from a material- and geometry-dependent transport measurement into evidence for an exact quantum electrical standard. Von Klitzing, Dorda, and Pepper showed that a two-dimensional electron gas could exhibit Hall resistance plateaus with values fixed by fundamental constants, later written as \(R_K = h/e^2\), rather than by sample dimensions, disorder, or microscopic device details. That was the paradigm shift: macroscopic electrical resistance could be reproducibly quantized with extraordinary precision in an ordinary laboratory device.

The immediate consequence was metrological. The quantum Hall effect made it possible to determine the fine-structure constant through resistance measurements and, more importantly, to realize a universal resistance standard far superior to artifact-based standards. The discovery eventually led to the conventional von Klitzing constant \(R_K\), the widespread use of quantum Hall devices in national metrology institutes, and contributed directly to the modern SI framework in which \(h\) and \(e\) have exact defined values.

Scientifically, the result opened a new field in which topology, disorder, and electron interactions became central to condensed-matter physics. The integer quantum Hall effect led to theoretical explanations in terms of Landau levels, edge states, and topological invariants, helping establish the idea that phases of matter can be classified by global, robust quantum properties rather than only by symmetry breaking. It also set the stage for the fractional quantum Hall effect, topological insulators, and later topological phases whose protected transport properties became a defining theme of modern quantum materials research.

## Abstract

Measurements of the Hall voltage of a two-dimensional electron gas, realized with a silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor, show that the Hall resistance at particular, experimentally well-defined surface carrier concentrations has fixed values which depend only on the fine-structure constant and speed of light, and is insensitive to the geometry of the device. Preliminary data are reported.
