# Situated Cognition and the Culture of Learning

## Why this mattered

Brown, Collins, and Duguid’s 1989 article mattered because it helped move learning theory away from the idea that knowledge is a detachable mental substance, acquired first and applied later. In its place, the paper argued that concepts are shaped by the activities, tools, language, and social practices through which people learn to use them. That reframed classroom failure: students were not merely failing to transfer abstract knowledge; schools were often teaching participation in “school” tasks rather than participation in the cultures where mathematical, scientific, or practical reasoning actually has meaning.

The immediate paradigm shift was pedagogical. If knowledge is partly constituted by use, then instruction should make expert practice visible: modeling, coaching, scaffolding, fading, authentic problems, and participation in communities of practice. The paper’s proposal of cognitive apprenticeship gave researchers and designers a vocabulary for building learning environments around activity rather than exposition alone. This made possible a generation of work on anchored instruction, project-based learning, problem-based learning, apprenticeship models, workplace learning, and later networked and digital learning communities.

Its longer influence was to connect cognitive science, anthropology, and educational design into what became central territory for the learning sciences. Subsequent breakthroughs, especially Lave and Wenger’s work on situated learning and communities of practice, extended the same insight from classrooms to identity, membership, and legitimate participation in social worlds. The paper did not by itself solve the hard problem of when abstraction and transfer occur, but it made that problem unavoidable: learning could no longer be adequately explained without asking where knowledge is learned, with whom, through what tools, and for what forms of practice. ## Abstract

Many teaching practices implicitly assume that conceptual knowledge can be abstracted from the situations in which it is learned and used. This article argues that this assumption inevitably limits the effectiveness of such practices. Drawing on recent research into cognition as it is manifest in everyday activity, the authors argue that knowledge is situated, being in part a product of the activity, context, and culture in which it is developed and used. They discuss how this view of knowledge affects our understanding of learning, and they note that conventional schooling too often ignores the influence of school culture on what is learned in school. As an alternative to conventional practices, they propose cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown, &amp; Newman, in press), which honors the situated nature of knowledge. They examine two examples of mathematics instruction that exhibit certain key features of this approach to teaching.
