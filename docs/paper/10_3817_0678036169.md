# Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison

## Why this mattered

D’Amico’s 1978 Telos review mattered chiefly as an early Anglophone marker of the importance of Michel Foucault’s *Discipline and Punish*, a work that helped shift analysis of punishment away from legal doctrine, humanitarian reform, or state repression alone and toward the everyday techniques through which institutions produce disciplined subjects. The central paradigm shift was to treat the prison not as an isolated penal invention, but as part of a broader “carceral” logic linking schools, barracks, factories, hospitals, and administrative knowledge.

After *Discipline and Punish*, scholars could study power as productive, spatial, bureaucratic, and embodied: operating through surveillance, examination, classification, normalization, and routine rather than only through law or violence. This made newly possible a genealogy of modern institutions in which reformist language and expert knowledge were analyzed as components of power, not merely as progress beyond cruelty.

Its influence shaped later breakthroughs across sociology, criminology, history, political theory, anthropology, surveillance studies, and science and technology studies. Work on governmentality, biopolitics, institutional ethnography, risk assessment, policing, schooling, psychiatry, and digital surveillance all drew on the book’s basic move: modern power often works by making people visible, comparable, trainable, and self-monitoring. D’Amico’s review belongs to the early reception of that shift, helping register why Foucault’s prison history became a general theory of modern disciplinary society.
