# La lithosphère et la tectonique des plaques

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it reframed algorithmic bias in health care as a problem that could arise even when a model appeared accurate by conventional predictive metrics. Its central insight was that the target variable itself was biased: health-care cost was being used as a proxy for health need, but unequal access to care meant that Black patients generated lower costs than White patients with comparable illness burden. That showed that bias could be embedded not only in model architecture or training data quality, but in the institutional meaning of the label being predicted.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as moral. After this work, auditing clinical algorithms increasingly meant asking whether the outcome being optimized actually represented the intended clinical construct. The paper made it newly possible to diagnose a major, real-world allocation failure by comparing risk scores against independent measures of illness rather than accepting cost prediction as sufficient. Its proposed remedy also showed that bias mitigation could sometimes come from changing the prediction target, not merely adjusting thresholds or removing protected attributes.

Its influence extends beyond this particular health-management algorithm. The study became a touchstone for later work on proxy labels, label bias, and algorithmic accountability in medicine and public policy. It helped establish that high-performing predictive systems can systematically reproduce inequity when trained on outcomes shaped by unequal access, unequal treatment, or unequal measurement. In that sense, its lasting contribution was to make “what exactly is the ground truth?” a central question for subsequent breakthroughs in fair machine learning and clinical AI governance.

## Abstract

Health systems rely on commercial prediction algorithms to identify and help patients with complex health needs. We show that a widely used algorithm, typical of this industry-wide approach and affecting millions of patients, exhibits significant racial bias: At a given risk score, Black patients are considerably sicker than White patients, as evidenced by signs of uncontrolled illnesses. Remedying this disparity would increase the percentage of Black patients receiving additional help from 17.7 to 46.5%. The bias arises because the algorithm predicts health care costs rather than illness, but unequal access to care means that we spend less money caring for Black patients than for White patients. Thus, despite health care cost appearing to be an effective proxy for health by some measures of predictive accuracy, large racial biases arise. We suggest that the choice of convenient, seemingly effective proxies for ground truth can be an important source of algorithmic bias in many contexts.
