# Induction of Decision Trees

## Why this mattered

Quinlan’s 1986 paper mattered because it helped make symbolic machine learning operational at scale. Earlier expert systems largely depended on hand-coded rules from domain experts; ID3 showed that rule-like structure could be induced automatically from examples. Its central contribution was not merely a particular tree-building heuristic, but a practical paradigm: use information-theoretic criteria to recursively choose attributes, producing decision trees that were both predictive and human-readable.

This made supervised learning newly useful in settings where data existed but explicit theories or expert rules were incomplete. A learned decision tree could classify cases, expose which variables drove decisions, and be translated into if-then rules, giving machine learning a form that was intelligible to scientists, engineers, and domain specialists. That combination of empirical performance and interpretability helped move machine learning away from purely knowledge-engineering approaches toward data-driven model induction.

The paper also established a lineage that shaped later breakthroughs. Quinlan’s ID3 led directly to C4.5 and influenced CART-style tree learning, rule induction, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, and modern ensemble methods that remain central in applied machine learning. Even where later systems abandoned single interpretable trees for higher accuracy, they preserved the paper’s core shift: predictive structure could be learned automatically from data by optimizing a criterion over candidate splits.
