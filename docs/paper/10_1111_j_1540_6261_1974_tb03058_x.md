# ON THE PRICING OF CORPORATE DEBT: THE RISK STRUCTURE OF INTEREST RATES*

## Why this mattered

Merton’s 1974 paper mattered because it recast corporate debt as a contingent claim on the value of the firm. Instead of treating default risk as an external add-on to a bond’s yield, the paper modeled risky debt using the same arbitrage logic that had just transformed option pricing: equity could be viewed as a call option on firm assets, while risky debt could be priced from the distribution of firm value relative to promised repayment. This made the risk structure of interest rates a problem in asset pricing, not merely in credit judgment or institutional description.

The paradigm shift was that defaultable securities became mathematically tractable within a unified valuation framework. After this paper, analysts could derive credit spreads, default probabilities, and the value of corporate liabilities from assumptions about firm value, volatility, leverage, maturity, and the risk-free rate. That opened the way for structural credit-risk modeling: later work by Black and Cox, Geske, Leland, Longstaff and Schwartz, and many others extended Merton’s setup to include safety covenants, multiple debt issues, taxes, bankruptcy costs, stochastic interest rates, and more realistic default timing.

Its influence also reaches into later breakthroughs in derivatives, risk management, and credit markets. The paper showed that option-pricing methods were not limited to traded options but could be applied to the capital structure itself, helping establish the modern view that financial contracts can be decomposed and valued by their state-contingent payoffs. Even where later reduced-form and intensity-based credit models moved away from firm-value mechanics, they inherited the central premise Merton made operational: credit risk can be priced rigorously as part of a general theory of contingent claims.
