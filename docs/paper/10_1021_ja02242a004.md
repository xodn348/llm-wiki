# THE ADSORPTION OF GASES ON PLANE SURFACES OF GLASS, MICA AND PLATINUM.

## Why this mattered

Langmuir’s 1918 paper mattered because it turned adsorption from a largely empirical surface phenomenon into a quantitative molecular theory. By treating a solid surface as a finite array of adsorption sites and deriving the relation between gas pressure and surface coverage, Langmuir introduced what became the Langmuir adsorption isotherm. The key shift was conceptual: adsorption was no longer modeled as indefinite accumulation in a diffuse surface layer, but as formation of a single molecular layer governed by dynamic equilibrium between molecules striking, sticking to, and leaving the surface.

This made surface chemistry mathematically usable. Once monolayer coverage could be inferred from pressure-dependent adsorption, researchers could estimate surface areas, adsorption energies, molecular cross sections, and site densities. That framework became foundational for catalysis, vacuum science, colloid and interface chemistry, thin films, and later materials science. It also helped establish the idea that surfaces have specific, saturable chemical sites, a premise central to heterogeneous catalysis and modern surface reaction kinetics.

The paper’s influence extended well beyond gas adsorption on glass, mica, and platinum. The Langmuir isotherm became the baseline model against which later theories were generalized, including BET multilayer adsorption theory, competitive adsorption models, enzyme- and receptor-binding analogues, and modern descriptions of adsorption in porous materials. Its lasting importance lies in making surfaces experimentally legible: after Langmuir, a measured uptake curve could be interpreted as molecular information about a surface, not merely as a bulk physical effect.
