# Generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity

## Why this mattered

Bollerslev’s 1986 paper mattered because it turned Engle’s ARCH idea from a powerful but often unwieldy model into a practical general framework for time-varying volatility. ARCH had shown that financial and economic time series could have conditional variances that change predictably over time, but high-order ARCH models were often needed to capture the long persistence seen in real data. GARCH introduced a parsimonious autoregressive structure for conditional variance itself, allowing volatility to depend both on recent shocks and on its own past values. This made volatility clustering modelable with relatively few parameters.

The paradigm shift was that volatility became a central object of econometric modeling rather than a nuisance around the conditional mean. After GARCH, researchers and practitioners could estimate, forecast, and test dynamic risk directly in asset returns, exchange rates, interest rates, and macroeconomic series. This helped make conditional variance models standard tools for option pricing, portfolio risk management, value-at-risk, and empirical finance more broadly.

The paper also opened a large research program. Later models such as EGARCH, GJR-GARCH, stochastic volatility models, multivariate GARCH, integrated and fractionally integrated GARCH, and realized-volatility methods can all be understood partly as responses to the possibilities and limitations made clear by Bollerslev’s formulation. Its influence lies not only in one model specification, but in establishing a durable language for thinking about persistence, clustering, asymmetry, and forecastability in uncertainty itself.
