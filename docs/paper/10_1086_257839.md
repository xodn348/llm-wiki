# A Pure Theory of Local Expenditures

## Why this mattered

Tiebout’s paper mattered because it recast a central problem in public finance: how preferences for public goods could be revealed when ordinary markets did not price them. Against Samuelson’s theory of pure public goods, where collective goods created a difficult preference-revelation problem, Tiebout argued that local public goods were different. If households could move among jurisdictions offering different tax-service bundles, mobility could make residents sort themselves into communities that matched their preferences. The famous “voting with one’s feet” mechanism made local government competition analytically comparable to market choice.

The paradigm shift was to treat fragmented local governments not merely as administrative units, but as a quasi-market for public goods. After Tiebout, economists and political scientists could model suburbanization, municipal competition, fiscal federalism, zoning, school finance, and local tax policy as outcomes of household sorting across jurisdictions. The paper’s assumptions were deliberately strong: perfect mobility and information, many communities, no spillovers, and households choosing among fixed public-service packages. But those assumptions made a new research program possible by specifying the conditions under which decentralized local provision might be efficient.

Its influence is visible in later breakthroughs on local public economics and urban economics, including work on capitalization of taxes and amenities into property values, club goods, fiscal competition, and equilibrium sorting models. Much subsequent research relaxed Tiebout’s assumptions and studied where the mechanism breaks down: exclusionary zoning, unequal mobility, congestion, racial and income segregation, interjurisdictional spillovers, and redistribution limits. The paper endured because it gave scholars a simple, powerful benchmark: local public finance could be analyzed as choice among communities, with mobility itself serving as a political and economic signal.
