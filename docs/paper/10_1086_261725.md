# Endogenous Technological Change

## Why this mattered

Romer’s paper shifted growth theory from treating technological progress as an unexplained outside force to modeling it as an economic activity shaped by incentives, market structure, and policy. Earlier neoclassical growth models could explain capital accumulation but had to place long-run productivity growth outside the model. Romer made ideas central: technological knowledge is nonrival, so one firm’s use of an idea does not prevent others from using it, yet it can be made partially excludable through patents, secrecy, or lead time. That combination broke the usual logic of perfect competition and made increasing returns compatible with a formal general-equilibrium growth model.

This mattered because it made sustained growth analyzable as the result of purposeful investment in research, education, and innovation rather than as a residual. The paper gave economists a framework for asking why some economies generate more new ideas than others, why human capital can raise growth rather than merely income levels, and why private markets may underinvest in research when innovators cannot capture all the social returns. It also clarified why scale alone is not enough: a large population does not automatically create growth unless enough human capital is allocated to idea production.

After the paper, endogenous growth became a foundation for modern work on innovation policy, intellectual property, R&D subsidies, human-capital formation, trade, and knowledge spillovers. Its influence is visible in later models of creative destruction, directed technical change, firm-level innovation, and semi-endogenous growth, all of which refined the mechanisms through which ideas are produced and diffused. The paradigm shift was not just a new growth equation; it made technological change a subject for economic explanation, measurement, and policy design.

## Abstract

Growth in this model is driven by technological change that arises from intentional investment decisions made by profit-maximizing agents. The distinguishing feature of the technology as an input is that it is neither a conventional good nor a public good; it is a nonrival, partially excludable good. Because of the nonconvexity introduced by a nonrival good, price-taking competition cannot be supported. Instead, the equilibrium is one with monopolistic competition. The main conclusions are that the stock of human capital determines the rate of growth, that too little human capital is devoted to research in equilibrium, that integration into world markets will increase growth rates, and that having a large population is not sufficient to generate growth.
