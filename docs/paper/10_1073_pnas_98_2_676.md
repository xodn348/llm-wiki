# A default mode of brain function

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, “rest” in functional brain imaging was usually treated as a neutral control condition, a subtraction baseline for finding task-evoked activations. Raichle and colleagues reframed that assumption. By using quantitative PET measures of oxygen extraction fraction, they argued that the awake resting brain has an organized physiological baseline, not merely unconstrained noise. The key conceptual move was to give meaning to the repeated observation that certain regions decreased their activity across many goal-directed tasks: these were not just methodological artifacts or passive absences of activation, but evidence for a coherent “default mode” of brain function.

That shift changed what could be studied. Resting-state activity became a phenomenon in its own right, making it possible to ask what the brain is doing when it is not engaged by an experimenter-defined task. The paper helped legitimize analyses of intrinsic brain organization, large-scale networks, and task-induced deactivations, especially in medial prefrontal, posterior cingulate/precuneus, and lateral parietal regions later associated with the default mode network. It also sharpened the logic of functional imaging: the choice of baseline was no longer a technical convenience but a theoretical commitment about brain function.

Subsequent work built directly on this framing. Resting-state fMRI, functional connectivity mapping, and network neuroscience all depended on the idea that spontaneous or baseline activity contains structured information about brain systems. The default mode network became central to research on self-referential thought, memory, mind-wandering, consciousness, development, aging, and psychiatric and neurological disease. The paper mattered because it turned the brain’s “background” into an object of scientific explanation, opening a path from task-localization studies toward the modern view of the brain as a dynamic, intrinsically organized network.

## Abstract

A baseline or control state is fundamental to the understanding of most complex systems. Defining a baseline state in the human brain, arguably our most complex system, poses a particular challenge. Many suspect that left unconstrained, its activity will vary unpredictably. Despite this prediction we identify a baseline state of the normal adult human brain in terms of the brain oxygen extraction fraction or OEF. The OEF is defined as the ratio of oxygen used by the brain to oxygen delivered by flowing blood and is remarkably uniform in the awake but resting state (e.g., lying quietly with eyes closed). Local deviations in the OEF represent the physiological basis of signals of changes in neuronal activity obtained with functional MRI during a wide variety of human behaviors. We used quantitative metabolic and circulatory measurements from positron-emission tomography to obtain the OEF regionally throughout the brain. Areas of activation were conspicuous by their absence. All significant deviations from the mean hemisphere OEF were increases, signifying deactivations, and resided almost exclusively in the visual system. Defining the baseline state of an area in this manner attaches meaning to a group of areas that consistently exhibit decreases from this baseline, during a wide variety of goal-directed behaviors monitored with positron-emission tomography and functional MRI. These decreases suggest the existence of an organized, baseline default mode of brain function that is suspended during specific goal-directed behaviors.
