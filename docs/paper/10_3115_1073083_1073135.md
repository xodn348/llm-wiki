# BLEU

## Why this mattered

Before BLEU, machine translation progress was constrained by evaluation itself: skilled human judgments were slow, costly, and hard to repeat. Papineni, Roukos, Ward, and Zhu reframed evaluation as a reusable statistical instrument: compare system output against reference translations using modified n-gram precision plus a brevity penalty. That made MT quality measurable quickly and cheaply enough to support frequent experiments, system tuning, and broad comparisons across research groups. The paper’s own framing as an “automated understudy” to human judges captured the shift: BLEU did not replace human evaluation, but it made iterative MT research operational at a new scale ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/P02-1040/), [IBM Research](https://research.ibm.com/publications/bleu-a-method-for-automatic-evaluation-of-machine-translation)).

Its larger consequence was methodological. BLEU helped turn machine translation into a benchmark-driven optimization problem, which suited the statistical MT era and later neural MT: researchers could train, tune, and compare systems against a common automatic target rather than waiting months for human studies. This enabled faster shared-task cycles, larger experimental grids, and clearer claims of incremental improvement. Many later breakthroughs in phrase-based SMT, neural sequence-to-sequence models, attention, and Transformer-based translation were reported through BLEU scores, even when the models themselves no longer resembled the IBM-style statistical systems of the early 2000s.

The same influence also exposed BLEU’s limits. Because it rewards surface n-gram overlap, it can miss valid paraphrases, discourse adequacy, terminology errors, and meaning-preserving rewrites, especially at sentence level or across domains. Later metrics such as METEOR, chrF, learned evaluators, and COMET can be read partly as responses to BLEU’s blind spots. But that criticism underscores the paper’s importance: BLEU established automatic evaluation as a central infrastructure for NLP progress, and subsequent evaluation research has largely been an effort to preserve its scalability while recovering more of what human judges actually assess.

## Abstract

Human evaluations of machine translation are extensive but expensive. Human evaluations can take months to finish and involve human labor that can not be reused. We propose a method of automatic machine translation evaluation that is quick, inexpensive, and language-independent, that correlates highly with human evaluation, and that has little marginal cost per run. We present this method as an automated understudy to skilled human judges which substitutes for them when there is need for quick or frequent evaluations.
