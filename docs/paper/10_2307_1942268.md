# An Ordination of the Upland Forest Communities of Southern Wisconsin

## Why this mattered

Bray and Curtis’s 1957 [ordination study](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942268) mattered because it helped move plant ecology from naming discrete “community types” toward analyzing vegetation as continuous, quantitative structure. Using upland Wisconsin forests, they showed that species composition across stands could be summarized by positions along ordination axes rather than forced into rigid classes. This gave empirical force to the Gleasonian, individualistic view of plant communities: species respond along gradients, and communities can intergrade rather than form sharply bounded units.

The paper also made multivariate community comparison operational. The Bray-Curtis measure of compositional dissimilarity became one of ecology’s durable tools, because it allowed sites to be compared from abundance data without reducing them to a few dominant species or a single environmental variable. After this, ecologists could map whole assemblages, detect gradients, compare disturbance effects, and ask whether environmental variables explained observed community structure.

Its influence runs through later ordination and beta-diversity methods, including polar ordination, non-metric multidimensional scaling, cluster analysis, PERMANOVA-style community testing, and modern microbial and metagenomic community analysis. The breakthrough was not only a statistic, but a change in what counted as analyzable ecological evidence: entire communities became quantitative objects that could be compared, visualized, and modeled.
