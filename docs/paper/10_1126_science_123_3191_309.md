# On the Origin of Cancer Cells

## Why this mattered

Warburg’s paper mattered because it gave cancer a metabolic identity. Its central claim, that malignant cells characteristically rely on fermentation even in the presence of oxygen, reframed cancer from a disorder understood mainly through histology and growth behavior into one that could be studied through cellular energy production. Warburg’s stronger causal thesis, that irreversible respiratory damage was the primary origin of cancer, was not accepted as a general explanation; later genetics showed that mutation, oncogene activation, and tumor-suppressor loss are central to malignancy. But the metabolic phenotype he emphasized, later called the Warburg effect, proved durable.

That shift made tumor metabolism experimentally and clinically tractable. It helped motivate decades of work measuring glucose uptake, lactate production, mitochondrial function, and the coupling between growth signals and biosynthesis. Its influence is visible in later breakthroughs from FDG-PET cancer imaging, which exploits high glucose uptake, to the modern cancer-metabolism field linking pathways such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR, HIF signaling, p53, MYC, and IDH mutations to metabolic rewiring. The lasting paradigm was not that metabolism replaced genetics, but that cancer cells are shaped by an interaction between genetic lesions and altered biochemical state, making metabolism both a diagnostic signal and a therapeutic target.
