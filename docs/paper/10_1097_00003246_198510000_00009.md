# APACHE II

## Why this mattered

APACHE II mattered because it turned intensive care severity from a largely local clinical judgment into a portable, quantitative object. By reducing early ICU physiology, age, and chronic health status into a validated 0–71 score, Knaus, Draper, Wagner, and Zimmerman made it possible to compare very sick patients across diagnoses, units, hospitals, and time. The key shift was not simply prediction of death; it was risk adjustment. Once mortality could be interpreted relative to initial severity, outcomes in critical care could be studied without pretending that all ICU populations were comparable at baseline.

This changed what counted as evidence in intensive care. APACHE II gave investigators a common stratification tool for clinical trials and observational comparisons, helping distinguish whether a therapy, unit, or hospital appeared better because it improved care or because it treated less severely ill patients. It also connected bedside data to systems-level questions: ICU resource use, case mix, quality assessment, and institutional benchmarking. In that sense, the paper helped make critical care a measurable service line as well as a clinical specialty.

Its influence can be seen in later generations of prognostic and risk-adjustment systems, including subsequent APACHE models, SAPS, MPM, and disease-specific ICU scores. Those later systems refined calibration, added variables, expanded populations, and eventually fed into modern electronic-health-record analytics and machine-learning mortality models. But APACHE II established the durable paradigm: routinely collected physiologic data, measured early in critical illness, could support standardized prognosis and fairer comparison of outcomes at scale.

## Abstract

This paper presents the form and validation results of APACHE II, a severity of disease classification system. APACHE II uses a point score based upon initial values of 12 routine physiologic measurements, age, and previous health status to provide a general measure of severity of disease. An increasing score (range 0 to 71) was closely correlated with the subsequent risk of hospital death for 5815 intensive care admissions from 13 hospitals. This relationship was also found for many common diseases.When APACHE II scores are combined with an accurate description of disease, they can prognostically stratify acutely ill patients and assist investigators comparing the success of new or differing forms of therapy. This scoring index can be used to evaluate the use of hospital resources and compare the efficacy of intensive care in different hospitals or over time.
