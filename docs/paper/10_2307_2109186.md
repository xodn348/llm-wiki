# An Economic Theory of Democracy.

## Why this mattered

Downs’s central move was to make democratic participation analyzable with the same formal assumptions used in economics: voters, parties, and candidates could be modeled as strategic actors pursuing expected benefits under constraints. This helped shift political science away from largely sociological or civic-norm explanations of voting behavior toward rational-choice models in which turnout, party competition, information costs, and ideological positioning could be treated as linked problems. Its most disruptive implication was the “paradox of voting”: if an individual vote is vanishingly unlikely to change an election outcome, then the instrumental benefit of voting will usually be smaller than the cost.

That negative result became generative. Later work did not simply accept that people should not vote; it used Downs’s framework to ask what had been left out of the original calculus. Riker and Ordeshook’s 1968 elaboration, especially the addition of expressive or civic-duty benefits, turned the paradox into a research program: scholars could now distinguish instrumental incentives from psychological, social, informational, and institutional mechanisms that make participation rational or predictable in practice.

The paper mattered because it made nonparticipation theoretically expected rather than merely anomalous. After Downs, explaining turnout required more than invoking democratic norms or social background; it required specifying why citizens vote despite tiny pivotal probabilities. That reframing shaped subsequent breakthroughs in rational choice, spatial models of party competition, collective action theory, and empirical turnout research, making the act of voting a test case for how far economic reasoning could travel inside democratic theory.

## Abstract

Downs presents a rational calculus of voting that has inspired much of the later work on voting and turnout. Particularly significant was his conclusion that a rational voter should almost never bother to vote. This conclusion, especially as elaborated on by Riker and Ordeshook (1968) has shifted the attention of modern political scientists from explaining why people don't vote to explaining why they do.
