# Functional connectivity in sympatric spiny rats reflects different dimensions of Amazonian forest‐association

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it challenged a simple, intuitive model of Amazonian connectivity: that várzea floodplain forests should act as linear corridors and terra-firme forests as more resistant matrix. By comparing two sympatric *Proechimys* species with different forest associations using genome-wide SNP data and landscape-resistance models, Dalapicolla and colleagues showed that the same broad forest categories could have opposite or more conditional effects on gene flow depending on species ecology. The key shift was from treating Amazonian forest type as a single explanatory axis to treating connectivity as a functional, taxon-specific property of landscapes.

What became newly possible was a more mechanistic comparative landscape-genomic approach in Amazonian biogeography. Instead of asking only whether rivers, floodplains, or uplands structure populations, researchers could test how particular habitat dimensions promote or inhibit movement for co-distributed species living in the same region. The finding that *P. steerei*, the várzea-associated species, showed stronger genetic structure than expected under the conventional corridor hypothesis was especially important because it separated habitat association from realized gene flow: being associated with floodplain forest does not automatically mean high connectivity along rivers, particularly across large geographic distances.

Its influence lies less in a single sweeping conclusion than in the framework it reinforced for later work: Amazonian diversification cannot be explained adequately by one landscape feature or one historical barrier model. Subsequent landscape-genomic and comparative phylogeographic studies have increasingly treated rivers, flooding regimes, forest heterogeneity, dispersal ecology, and species-specific habitat use as interacting dimensions. In that sense, the paper helped move Amazonian biogeography toward multi-layer, organism-centered models of connectivity, where the same landscape can be a corridor for one lineage, a filter for another, and a barrier only under particular spatial or ecological conditions.

## Abstract

Abstract Aim Understanding how the landscape influences gene flow is important in explaining biodiversity, especially when co‐distributed taxa across heterogeneous landscapes exhibit species‐specific habitat associations. Here, we test predictions about the effects of forest‐type on population connectivity in two sympatric species of spiny rats that differ in their forest associations. Specifically, we evaluate the hypothesis that seasonal floodplain forests ( várzea ) provide linear connectivity, facilitating gene flow among individuals, while non‐flooded forests ( terra‐firme ) may diminish the functional connectivity. Location Western Amazon, South America. Taxon Proechimys simonsi (non‐flooded forests, terra‐firme ) and Proechimys steerei (seasonal floodplain forests, várzea ). Methods We analyse about 13,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms along with characterizations of landscape heterogeneity for two forest types to test for differences in the functional connectivity. Influence of the landscape and environmental variables are quantified using maximum‐likelihood population effect models to identify the relative importance of variables in explaining the gene flow. Results There are significant differences in functional connectivity between species. However, the genomic data does not support the conventional hypotheses of higher connectivity for inhabitants of várzea than those of terra‐firme . Stronger genetic structure in P. steerei than P. simonsi based on isolation by distance models suggests reduced gene flow in species associated with várzea forests. Isolation by resistance reinforces that wetland habitats inhibit and promote the functional connectivity in P. simonsi and P. steerei , respectively, although large distances along the rivers can prevent gene flow in P. steerei . Main conclusion Interpreting differences between connectivity in taxa apparent from genetic analyses through the lens of a single dimension of Amazonian heterogeneity—that is, forest type—may be an oversimplification. Our statistical modelling and fit of the data to different models points to specific environmental and habitat differences between the ecological divergent spiny rat species that may contribute to differences in the genetic structure of these sympatric taxa.
