# Topological domains in mammalian genomes identified by analysis of chromatin interactions

## Why this mattered

Before Dixon et al.’s 2012 [Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11082), genome organization was often described at broad scales: chromosome territories, active and inactive compartments, and individual looping contacts. This work showed that mammalian chromosomes are also partitioned into pervasive megabase-scale “topological domains,” regions within which chromatin contacts are more frequent than contacts across boundaries. That made 3D genome folding a map-like, genome-wide feature rather than a set of isolated anecdotes.

The paradigm shift was that regulatory genome structure became segmental and interpretable. The paper showed that domain boundaries were often stable across cell types, conserved between syntenic human and mouse regions, and enriched for factors such as CTCF, housekeeping genes, tRNAs, and SINE elements. After this, researchers could ask whether enhancers, promoters, chromatin states, and disease-associated variants acted within constrained neighborhoods, rather than assuming the linear genome alone defined regulatory opportunity.

This framework directly set up later breakthroughs in 3D genomics: higher-resolution Hi-C maps, the formalization of topologically associating domains, CTCF/cohesin boundary models, loop-extrusion mechanisms, and studies showing that boundary disruption can rewire enhancer-promoter contacts in development and disease. Its importance was not that it solved chromosome folding, but that it gave the field a durable unit of analysis for connecting nuclear architecture to gene regulation.
