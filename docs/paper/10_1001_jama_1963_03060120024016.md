# Studies of Illness in the Aged

## Why this mattered

Katz and colleagues’ 1963 JAMA paper, *[Studies of Illness in the Aged](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/666768)*, shifted geriatric medicine from describing disease states alone to measuring what illness meant for daily function. Its “Index of ADL” made basic activities such as bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, and feeding into a standardized clinical scale. That was the paradigm change: frailty, recovery, disability, and prognosis could now be compared across patients and institutions using observable function rather than only diagnoses, impressions, or mortality.

After this paper, functional status became a measurable outcome for chronic illness, rehabilitation, nursing-home care, and aging research. It helped make possible later geriatric assessment frameworks, disability epidemiology, long-term-care policy measures, and clinical trials that treated independence itself as a health endpoint. Subsequent tools, including instrumental ADL scales and multidimensional geriatric assessments, extended the same idea: the consequences of disease in older adults are not fully captured by pathology unless medicine also measures the capacity to live.
