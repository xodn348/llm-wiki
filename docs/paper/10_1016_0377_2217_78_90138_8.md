# Measuring the efficiency of decision making units

## Why this mattered

Before Charnes, Cooper, and Rhodes, efficiency analysis was largely tied either to single-output engineering ratios or to econometric production functions that required specifying a functional form. This paper reframed the problem: a “decision making unit” with many inputs and many outputs could be evaluated relative to the best observed performers, with the weights chosen endogenously from the data rather than imposed in advance. That move made efficiency measurement operational for schools, hospitals, government programs, banks, and other organizations where outputs are plural, prices may be missing, and profit is not the natural criterion.

The decisive innovation was the transformation of a nonconvex fractional efficiency model into linear programming problems, making the concept computationally usable and reproducible. The dual formulation also gave the method its geometric interpretation: each unit is compared with an empirical efficient frontier formed from observed peers. After this paper, analysts could identify not only whether an organization was inefficient, but also by how much, relative to which reference units, and with what input reductions or output increases implied by the frontier.

The paper founded what became known as Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA). Its influence was not just methodological but institutional: it supplied a language for benchmarking performance without reducing public or nonprofit activity to a single monetary output. Later breakthroughs extended the CCR model to variable returns to scale, dynamic and network settings, stochastic and bootstrap inference, environmental applications, and productivity-change measures such as Malmquist indexes. Those developments all preserved the paper’s central paradigm shift: efficiency could be estimated nonparametrically from observed multi-input, multi-output practice, rather than derived only from prices, profits, or pre-specified production functions.

## Abstract

A nonlinear (nonconvex) programming model provides a new definition of efficiency for use in evaluating activities of not-for-profit entities participating in public programs. A scalar measure of the efficiency of each participating unit is thereby provided, along with methods for objectively determining weights by reference to the observational data for the multiple outputs and multiple inputs that characterize such programs. Equivalences are established to ordinary linear programming models for effecting computations. The duals to these linear programming models provide a new way for estimating extremal relations from observational data. Connections between engineering and economic approaches to efficiency are delineated along with new interpretations and ways of using them in evaluating and controlling managerial behavior in public programs.
