# A Contribution to the Empirics of Economic Growth

## Why this mattered

Mankiw, Romer, and Weil made the Solow model empirically central again by showing that its apparent failure in cross-country data largely changed once “capital” was expanded to include human capital. Before the paper, large international income gaps were often taken as evidence that the neoclassical growth model was too simple to explain development patterns. Their augmented specification showed that savings, population growth, and schooling-related human-capital accumulation could account for a substantial share of variation in income per worker across countries, while preserving the Solow model’s core logic of diminishing returns and steady-state differences.

The paper also sharpened the empirical meaning of “convergence.” Rather than asking whether poor countries automatically catch up with rich ones, it distinguished unconditional convergence from conditional convergence: countries converge toward their own steady states, given investment rates, population growth, and human-capital accumulation. This became a standard organizing framework for growth empirics, allowing researchers to test whether economies were poor because they were far from their steady states or because their steady states themselves differed.

Its influence was partly methodological. The paper helped make cross-country growth regressions a dominant tool in macroeconomics and development economics, while also defining the benchmark that later work had to move beyond. Subsequent breakthroughs in endogenous growth, institutions, geography, technology diffusion, and development accounting often framed themselves against the Mankiw-Romer-Weil result: either by explaining what the augmented Solow model left in the residual, questioning the causal interpretation of its regressions, or replacing proximate inputs such as physical and human capital with deeper determinants of long-run prosperity.

## Abstract

This paper examines whether the Solow growth model is consistent with the international variation in the standard of living. It shows that an augmented Solow model that includes accumulation of human as well as physical capital provides an excellent description of the cross-country data. The paper also examines the implications of the Solow model for convergence in standards of living, that is, for whether poor countries tend to grow faster than rich countries. The evidence indicates that, holding population growth and capital accumulation constant, countries converge at about the rate the augmented Solow model predicts.
