# Refinement of Macromolecular Structures by the Maximum-Likelihood Method

## Why this mattered

Before this work, crystallographic refinement of macromolecular structures was still dominated by least-squares targets that treated discrepancies between observed and calculated diffraction data too simplistically, especially when phases were uncertain, models were incomplete, and errors varied across a structure. Murshudov, Vagin, and Dodson made maximum-likelihood refinement practical for routine protein crystallography by deriving a target that explicitly modeled uncertainty in the model and data, incorporated prior phase information, and estimated model quality through `sigma(A)` using free reflections. The shift was not merely a better scoring function: it reframed refinement as probabilistic inference under imperfect information rather than direct minimization of residual differences.

This mattered because macromolecular crystallography is often hardest exactly where least-squares assumptions are weakest: early refinement, low-resolution data, partial models, flexible regions, and structures with heterogeneous error. By implementing the method in REFMAC and showing consistent improvement across several protein cases, the paper helped turn maximum-likelihood refinement from a theoretical ideal into a usable standard method. After this, crystallographers could refine more difficult models with reduced overfitting and better use of experimental phase information, making structure determination more reliable at stages where incorrect models could otherwise be reinforced by the refinement target itself.

The broader consequence was that probabilistic refinement became part of the infrastructure of structural biology. REFMAC and related maximum-likelihood approaches were incorporated into widely used crystallographic pipelines, supporting the surge of macromolecular structure determination in the structural genomics era and improving the quality of deposited Protein Data Bank models. Later advances in automated model building, validation, ligand fitting, and low-resolution refinement built on the same conceptual turn: structural models should be optimized against diffraction data while explicitly accounting for uncertainty, bias, and model incompleteness.

## Abstract

This paper reviews the mathematical basis of maximum likelihood. The likelihood function for macromolecular structures is extended to include prior phase information and experimental standard uncertainties. The assumption that different parts of a structure might have different errors is considered. A method for estimating sigma(A) using 'free' reflections is described and its effects analysed. The derived equations have been implemented in the program REFMAC. This has been tested on several proteins at different stages of refinement (bacterial alpha-amylase, cytochrome c', cross-linked insulin and oligopeptide binding protein). The results derived using the maximum-likelihood residual are consistently better than those obtained from least-squares refinement.
