# Isolation of a cDNA cLone Derived from a Blood-Borne Non-A, Non-B Viral Hepatitis Genome

## Why this mattered

Before this [1989 *Science* paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11983439/), “non-A, non-B hepatitis” was largely a disease category defined by exclusion: transmissible through blood, clinically important, but without an identified agent. Choo, Kuo, Weiner, Overby, Bradley, and Houghton changed that by recovering a cDNA clone from infectious plasma and showing that it encoded an antigen specifically associated with NANBH infection. The shift was conceptual as much as technical: an unknown pathogen could be found by molecular cloning and immune recognition, without first growing the virus in culture or visualizing it directly.

That made hepatitis C a molecular object. Once part of the viral genome was in hand, researchers could design probes, sequence related isolates, build serologic and nucleic-acid tests, and begin classifying the agent as the RNA virus now known as hepatitis C virus. The immediate practical consequence was blood screening: what had been an invisible post-transfusion risk became detectable, allowing blood banks to sharply reduce transmission. The paper also supplied a template for identifying elusive infectious agents by genomic fragments rather than classical virology alone.

Its later importance lies in the chain of work it enabled. The clone led to fuller genome characterization, diagnostic assays, viral replication systems, and eventually the molecular targets used for direct-acting antivirals that made hepatitis C a curable chronic viral infection for most treated patients. In that sense, the paper stands between Harvey Alter’s clinical demonstration of transmissible non-A, non-B hepatitis and Charles Rice’s later proof of infectious cloned HCV, the sequence of advances recognized by the [2020 Nobel Prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2020/press-release/) for the discovery of hepatitis C virus.

## Abstract

A random-primed complementary DNA library was constructed from plasma containing the uncharacterized non-A, non-B hepatitis (NANBH) agent and screened with serum from a patient diagnosed with NANBH. A complementary DNA clone was isolated that was shown to encode an antigen associated specifically with NANBH infections. This clone is not derived from host DNA but from an RNA molecule present in NANBH infections that consists of at least 10,000 nucleotides and that is positive-stranded with respect to the encoded NANBH antigen. These data indicate that this clone is derived from the genome of the NANBH agent and are consistent with the agent being similar to the togaviridae or flaviviridae. This molecular approach should be of great value in the isolation and characterization of other unidentified infectious agents.
