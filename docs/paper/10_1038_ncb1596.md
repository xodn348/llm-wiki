# Exosome-mediated transfer of mRNAs and microRNAs is a novel mechanism of genetic exchange between cells

## Why this mattered

Before Valadi et al. (2007), exosomes were often treated chiefly as small secreted vesicles involved in disposal or conventional cell signaling. This paper changed the frame: it showed that mast-cell exosomes contain specific mRNAs and microRNAs, that these RNAs can be taken up by other cells, and that transferred mRNA can be translated into protein in the recipient cell. The central conceptual shift was that extracellular vesicles were not merely carriers of proteins or cellular waste, but vehicles for horizontal transfer of regulatory and coding information between mammalian cells.

That made a new kind of cell-to-cell communication experimentally plausible: cells could influence distant or neighboring cells by exporting RNA packages rather than only by secreting soluble ligands or making direct contact. The paper helped launch the modern extracellular-vesicle and extracellular-RNA fields, including work on exosomal microRNAs in cancer, immunity, development, neurobiology, and inflammation. It also supplied a rationale for later efforts to use vesicle-associated RNAs as disease biomarkers in blood and other fluids, and for engineering exosomes or exosome-like particles as delivery systems for therapeutic RNA. Its lasting importance is less that every early claim about exosomes proved simple, and more that it made RNA-containing vesicles a serious mechanism of intercellular genetic communication.
