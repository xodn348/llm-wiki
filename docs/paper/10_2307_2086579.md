# The Theory of Social and Economic Organization.

## Why this mattered

Weber’s theory mattered because it turned authority from a moral or legal label into an analytic object. By distinguishing charismatic, traditional, and rational-legal legitimacy, it made possible a comparative sociology of rule: states, churches, firms, parties, and bureaucracies could be studied according to the grounds on which obedience was claimed and organized, rather than treated as historically unique institutions or reduced to coercion alone.

Its paradigm shift was especially important for understanding modernity. Weber’s account of rational-legal authority and bureaucracy gave social science a language for explaining why modern capitalist economies and administrative states depend on impersonal rules, offices, credentials, files, and predictable procedures. That framework helped move sociology beyond grand evolutionary narratives and simple economic determinism toward historically grounded, ideal-typical comparison.

The English publication also helped transmit Weber into postwar Anglophone sociology, where Parsons’s editorial and translational role connected Weberian concepts to emerging structural-functional theory, organization studies, political sociology, and economic sociology. Later work on bureaucracy, legitimacy, institutional authority, state capacity, professions, and organizational rationalization repeatedly returned to Weber’s categories, often revising or criticizing them, but rarely escaping the problem-space they defined.

## Abstract

The Theory of Social and Economic Organization grew out of Weber’s philosophical inquiries into the nature of authority and how it is transmitted. He identified three types of authority: the charismatic, based on the individual qualities of a leader and reverence for them among his or her followers; the traditional, based on custom and usage; and the rational-legal, based on the rule of objective law.
