# Numerical integration of the cartesian equations of motion of a system with constraints: molecular dynamics of n-alkanes

## Why this mattered

Ryckaert, Ciccotti, and Berendsen’s 1977 paper introduced what became known as the **SHAKE algorithm**, a practical method for enforcing holonomic constraints during molecular dynamics. Its immediate importance was computational: by constraining fast internal motions such as covalent bond stretching, especially C-H bonds in n-alkanes, simulations could use larger time steps while preserving the physically relevant slower conformational dynamics. This changed constrained molecular dynamics from a formal mechanical problem into a robust numerical technique usable for realistic molecular systems.

The paradigm shift was that molecular dynamics no longer had to spend most of its computational effort resolving the highest-frequency vibrations when those motions were not the scientific target. SHAKE made it possible to simulate larger molecules and longer trajectories with the hardware of the time, helping move molecular dynamics from small, idealized models toward chemically detailed simulations of liquids, polymers, biomolecules, and membranes. The paper’s focus on n-alkanes was therefore not incidental: flexible chain molecules exposed exactly the timescale problem that would limit molecular simulation without constraints.

Its influence runs through much of later molecular simulation. Constraint algorithms became standard components of MD engines, enabling routine use of fixed bond lengths, rigid water models, and all-atom biomolecular simulations at femtosecond time steps. Later methods such as RATTLE, LINCS, SETTLE, and modern multiple-time-step schemes refined or specialized the same basic idea: separate numerically troublesome fast degrees of freedom from the slower motions that determine structure, thermodynamics, and function. In that sense, the paper helped establish one of the core algorithmic compromises that made molecular dynamics a practical tool for computational chemistry, biophysics, and materials science.
