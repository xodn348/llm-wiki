# The Measurement of Productive Efficiency

## Why this mattered

[M. J. Farrell’s 1957 paper](https://academic.oup.com/jrsssa/article/120/3/253/7101561) changed efficiency from a largely qualitative or accounting comparison into an estimable frontier concept. Its central move was to define a producer’s inefficiency relative to the best observed production possibilities, separating **technical efficiency** from **allocative/price efficiency** and combining them into an overall measure. That made it possible to ask not just whether one firm, farm, or industry produced more than another, but how far it was from an attainable frontier using comparable inputs and outputs.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as conceptual: productive efficiency became a measurable distance to a frontier rather than a residual impression from productivity ratios. After Farrell, researchers could compare heterogeneous producers, estimate avoidable waste, and distinguish gains from better technique from gains due to better input proportions. This gave economists, statisticians, operations researchers, and policy analysts a common quantitative language for evaluating farms, firms, hospitals, schools, banks, utilities, and public agencies.

Its most direct legacy was frontier efficiency analysis. [Charnes, Cooper, and Rhodes’s 1978 data envelopment analysis paper](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v2y1978i6p429-444.html) explicitly built on Farrell’s ideas by turning the frontier comparison into a linear-programming framework for multi-input, multi-output “decision-making units.” In parallel, stochastic frontier analysis extended the same frontier logic while allowing statistical noise. Much of modern productivity and performance measurement still rests on Farrell’s basic insight: inefficiency can be treated as an empirically measurable gap between observed performance and the best feasible practice.
