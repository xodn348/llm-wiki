# A general method applicable to the search for similarities in the amino acid sequence of two proteins

## Why this mattered

Needleman and Wunsch’s 1970 paper made sequence comparison a rigorous computational problem rather than an informal visual exercise. Its central contribution was a dynamic programming method for finding an optimal global alignment between two protein sequences, including insertions and deletions, under an explicit scoring scheme. This reframed biological similarity as something that could be systematically optimized, reproduced, and evaluated, which was a major shift as protein sequences were beginning to accumulate faster than manual comparison methods could handle.

What became newly possible was large-scale, algorithmic inference of evolutionary and functional relationships from sequence data. By aligning full sequences end to end and allowing gaps, the method could detect homology even when mutations, insertions, or deletions had obscured simple residue-by-residue correspondence. The paper therefore helped establish the conceptual foundation for computational molecular biology: biological history could be read from sequences using formal algorithms, not only from morphology, biochemistry, or expert judgment.

The later history of bioinformatics rests heavily on this move. Smith-Waterman local alignment, BLAST and FASTA database search, multiple sequence alignment, comparative genomics, phylogenetic reconstruction, protein domain analysis, and modern annotation pipelines all descend from the same basic insight: sequence similarity can be made into a tractable optimization problem. Needleman-Wunsch did not by itself solve every practical problem of scale or statistics, but it supplied the paradigm that made those later breakthroughs coherent.
