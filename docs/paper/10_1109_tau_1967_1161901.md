# The use of fast Fourier transform for the estimation of power spectra: A method based on time averaging over short, modified periodograms

## Why this mattered

Welch’s paper mattered because it turned spectral estimation from a theoretically simple but computationally expensive procedure into a practical routine for digital signal analysis. Earlier periodogram methods could estimate power spectra from sampled data, but long records demanded substantial computation and storage, and single periodograms had high variance. Welch combined the newly important fast Fourier transform with a pragmatic statistical idea: split a record into shorter segments, taper each segment, compute “modified periodograms,” and average them. The result traded some frequency resolution for much lower variance and far better computational feasibility.

That tradeoff became a paradigm for applied spectral analysis. After Welch, estimating a power spectral density was no longer mainly a bespoke calculation for small or carefully prepared datasets; it became a standard, repeatable workflow that could be applied to long signals, streaming measurements, and systems whose behavior might vary over time. The method’s segmenting and averaging also made nonstationarity easier to inspect, because spectra could be computed over local portions of a record rather than only over the full observation.

Its influence is visible across later signal-processing practice: vibration analysis, acoustics, communications, biomedical signal analysis, geophysics, radar, and control systems all adopted variants of averaged, windowed FFT-based spectral estimation. Welch’s method did not solve every spectral-estimation problem, and later approaches such as multitaper methods addressed different bias-variance limitations. But the paper helped establish the computational and methodological template behind much of modern empirical frequency-domain analysis: use the FFT, window finite data carefully, average to stabilize estimates, and make spectral estimation practical at scale.

## Abstract

The use of the fast Fourier transform in power spectrum analysis is described. Principal advantages of this method are a reduction in the number of computations and in required core storage, and convenient application in nonstationarity tests. The method involves sectioning the record and averaging modified periodograms of the sections.
