# Job Market Signaling

## Why this mattered

Spence’s paper mattered because it gave economists a tractable way to analyze markets in which one side knows something important that the other side cannot directly observe. In the job-market setting, education was modeled not simply as human-capital formation, but as a possible signal: a costly action that can separate workers of different productivity if the cost of acquiring it differs across types. This shifted attention from prices and quantities alone to the informational content of observable choices, making asymmetric information a central object of equilibrium analysis rather than a peripheral imperfection.

The paper helped establish signaling as a general mechanism for understanding how markets operate under uncertainty. After Spence, economists could formally study why apparently wasteful expenditures might persist, how equilibria can depend on beliefs and feedback, and why markets may sort people or firms through costly signals rather than direct measurement. This made possible a broad research program in education, labor markets, finance, insurance, industrial organization, law and economics, and biology, where signals such as schooling, warranties, dividends, advertising, credentials, and displays can shape behavior even when they do not directly change underlying quality.

Its importance also lies in how it completed a larger transformation in microeconomics alongside Akerlof’s “Market for Lemons” and later work by Stiglitz and others. Akerlof showed how hidden information could cause markets to unravel; Spence showed how informed agents might respond by taking actions that reveal information; Stiglitz’s work on screening showed how uninformed agents could design contracts to elicit it. Together these ideas became the foundation of modern information economics, helping explain why real markets often depend on institutions, credentials, contracts, and reputational devices rather than perfect information.
