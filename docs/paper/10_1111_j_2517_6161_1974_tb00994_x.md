# Cross-Validatory Choice and Assessment of Statistical Predictions

## Why this mattered

Stone’s 1974 paper helped turn cross-validation from a practical data-splitting trick into a general statistical principle for choosing and assessing predictive procedures. Its key shift was to frame model choice around estimated out-of-sample predictive performance rather than only in-sample fit, formal optimality criteria, or analyst preference. By treating a data-analytic “prescription” as the object to be evaluated, Stone made it possible to compare whole procedures, including estimation rules, regression specifications, and analysis-of-variance choices, by asking how well they would predict data not used in fitting.

This mattered because it gave statisticians a broadly applicable way to discipline flexibility. As statistical modeling moved toward richer model classes, cross-validation supplied a criterion that could penalize overfitting empirically without requiring a fully specified parametric truth. The paper’s examples were classical, but the principle anticipated the central problem of modern statistical learning: choosing among competing procedures by their generalization behavior. In that sense, it helped shift attention from explaining the observed sample to estimating performance on future samples.

The later influence is visible across model selection, nonparametric regression, regularization, and machine learning. Cross-validation became a standard tool for choosing smoothing parameters, selecting variables, tuning penalties such as ridge and lasso regularization, and comparing predictive algorithms. Stone’s work also sits near the historical bridge between classical statistics and the later learning-theoretic vocabulary of training error, test error, and generalization. Its lasting importance is that it made predictive validation a reusable statistical method rather than an ad hoc safeguard.

## Abstract

Summary A generalized form of the cross-validation criterion is applied to the choice and assessment of prediction using the data-analytic concept of a prescription. The examples used to illustrate the application are drawn from the problem areas of univariate estimation, linear regression and analysis of variance.
