# The Hungarian method for the assignment problem

## Why this mattered

Kuhn’s paper mattered because it turned the assignment problem from a combinatorial search over `n!` matchings into a tractable optimization problem with a constructive, finite algorithm. The key shift was not merely a faster trick for one scheduling problem, but a demonstration that the structure of a linear assignment instance could be exploited through dual variables, equality subgraphs, and augmenting operations. By drawing out ideas from Dénes Kőnig and Jenő Egerváry, Kuhn made explicit a bridge between matching theory, matrix reductions, and linear programming duality.

After this paper, assignment problems became practically solvable at scales relevant to personnel allocation, logistics, transportation, economics, and later computer science. The Hungarian method gave researchers and practitioners a canonical polynomial-time algorithm for a central discrete optimization problem, helping establish that some apparently explosive combinatorial tasks had hidden structure that could be algorithmically controlled. Its importance was amplified by later refinements, especially James Munkres’s 1957 proof and analysis showing the method could be implemented in strongly polynomial time, which helped fix the algorithm’s place in the foundations of combinatorial optimization.

The paper also influenced the broader development of algorithmic graph theory and network optimization. The assignment problem is a special case of bipartite matching and minimum-cost flow, and the Hungarian method anticipated themes that became central in later breakthroughs: primal-dual algorithms, complementary slackness as an algorithmic guide, and augmenting-path methods. In that sense, Kuhn’s contribution was paradigmatic: it helped recast optimization over discrete structures as a domain where mathematical certificates and efficient algorithms could develop together.

## Abstract

Abstract Assuming that numerical scores are available for the performance of each of n persons on each of n jobs, the “assignment problem” is the quest for an assignment of persons to jobs so that the sum of the n scores so obtained is as large as possible. It is shown that ideas latent in the work of two Hungarian mathematicians may be exploited to yield a new method of solving this problem.
