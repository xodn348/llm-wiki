# Experimental and computational approaches to estimate solubility and permeability in drug discovery and development settings

## Why this mattered

Lipinski and colleagues turned a recurring medicinal-chemistry failure mode into a simple, operational rule. Before this paper, potency against a biological target could dominate early discovery even when compounds were becoming too large, too lipophilic, or too hydrogen-bond-rich to dissolve and cross membranes well. The paper’s “Rule of Five” made poor oral absorption and permeability partly predictable from properties available at design time: molecular weight, calculated lipophilicity, hydrogen-bond donors, and hydrogen-bond acceptors. Its importance was not that these variables were unknown, but that it compressed them into a practical screening heuristic usable by chemists before costly development work.

The paradigm shift was to treat “drug-likeness” and developability as early design constraints, not late-stage pharmacokinetic surprises. This helped move drug discovery toward integrated ADME thinking, where solubility, permeability, and absorption risk were considered alongside target affinity. It also fit the rise of combinatorial chemistry and high-throughput screening in the 1990s: as libraries grew larger, researchers needed fast computational filters to prioritize compounds likely to become orally active drugs rather than merely active assay hits.

The rule later became a foundation for cheminformatics, virtual screening, library design, and multi-parameter optimization. Subsequent work refined or challenged it, especially for natural products, macrocycles, transporters, prodrugs, and “beyond Rule of Five” chemical space, but those exceptions reinforced the paper’s broader legacy: drug discovery needed explicit, quantitative links between molecular structure and human-use feasibility. In that sense, Lipinski et al. did not solve oral bioavailability; they made it a routine design problem.
