# Ordered mesoporous molecular sieves synthesized by a liquid-crystal template mechanism

## Why this mattered

This paper changed porous-materials chemistry by showing that ordered pores did not have to be limited to the micropore dimensions of zeolites. Kresge and coauthors reported silica-based molecular sieves with regular mesopores formed through a liquid-crystal templating mechanism, using surfactant assemblies to organize inorganic walls. That shifted the design principle from relying mainly on crystallographic frameworks to using soft-matter self-assembly as a scaffold for inorganic structure.

The immediate consequence was a new size regime for “molecular sieves”: large, uniform, high-surface-area channels capable of admitting molecules too bulky for conventional zeolites. This made ordered porous solids relevant to heavier hydrocarbons, large organic molecules, supported catalysts, separations, adsorption, and host-guest chemistry at length scales that had previously been difficult to control with comparable regularity.

Its influence is visible in the later explosion of mesoporous materials chemistry, including MCM-type silicas, SBA-15, ordered mesoporous organosilicas, metal oxides, carbons, and nanocasting strategies. The deeper breakthrough was conceptual: pore architecture could be programmed by coupling molecular self-assembly with sol-gel inorganic chemistry. That idea became a foundation for later work in catalysis, environmental adsorption, sensing, energy materials, and biomedical delivery systems.
