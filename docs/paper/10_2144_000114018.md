# Chelex 100 as a Medium for Simple Extraction of DNA for PCR-Based Typing from Forensic Material

## Why this mattered

Before this work, forensic DNA typing often depended on extraction methods such as proteinase K digestion followed by phenol-chloroform purification: effective, but slow, chemically hazardous, transfer-heavy, and poorly matched to the tiny, degraded, or inhibitor-rich samples common in casework. Walsh, Metzger, and Higuchi showed that Chelex 100 could turn DNA preparation for PCR into a simple boil-and-use procedure for bloodstains, semen stains, buccal swabs, hair, and post-coital material. The crucial shift was not that Chelex produced pristine genomic DNA; it produced DNA that was sufficiently amplifiable, with fewer practical barriers and, for bloodstains, apparently fewer PCR inhibitors.

That mattered because PCR-based forensic typing was becoming a practical alternative to older, larger-sample DNA methods. By removing organic solvents, reducing tube transfers, and preserving concordant HLA DQ alpha genotypes across 84 paired extractions, the paper helped make PCR typing more routine, scalable, and compatible with low-template forensic evidence. The method lowered the technical threshold for recovering useful genetic information from stains and traces that would previously have been difficult, slow, or risky to process.

Its influence extended beyond the specific HLA DQ alpha assays used in the study. Chelex extraction became a standard preparatory method in forensic genetics and helped support the broader transition toward PCR-centered human identification, including later STR typing workflows. In that sense, the paper’s paradigm shift was methodological: it made sample preparation fast and robust enough that the power of PCR could be applied widely to ordinary forensic material, helping move DNA typing from specialized laboratory procedure toward routine investigative infrastructure.

## Abstract

Procedures utilizing Chelex 100 chelating resin have been developed for extracting DNA from forensic-type samples for use with the PCR. The procedures are simple, rapid, involve no organic solvents and do not require multiple tube transfers for most types of samples. The extraction of DNA from semen and very small bloodstains using Chelex 100 is as efficient or more efficient than using proteinase K and phenol-chloroform extraction. DNA extracted from bloodstains seems less prone to contain PCR inhibitors when prepared by this method. The Chelex method has been used with amplification and typing at the HLA DQ alpha locus to obtain the DQ alpha genotypes of many different types of samples, including whole blood, bloodstains, seminal stains, buccal swabs, hair and post-coital samples. The results of a concordance study are presented in which the DQ alpha genotypes of 84 samples prepared using Chelex or using conventional phenol-chloroform extraction are compared. The genotypes obtained using the two different extraction methods were identical for all samples tested.
