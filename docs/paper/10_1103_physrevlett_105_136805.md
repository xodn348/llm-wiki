# Atomically Thin <mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"> <mml:msub> <mml:mi>MoS</mml:mi> <mml:mn>2</mml:mn> </mml:msub> </mml:math> : A New Direct-Gap Semiconductor

## Why this mattered

Before this work, atomically thin crystals were already important because graphene had shown that stable two-dimensional materials could exist, but graphene’s lack of a band gap limited its direct use in conventional optoelectronics and transistor logic. Mak, Lee, Hone, Shan, and Heinz showed that reducing MoS₂ to a single S-Mo-S layer did not merely make a thinner version of the bulk semiconductor: it changed the band structure itself. The key result was the thickness-driven crossover from an indirect-gap semiconductor in bulk and few-layer MoS₂ to a direct-gap semiconductor in the monolayer, accompanied by a more than \(10^4\)-fold increase in photoluminescence quantum efficiency relative to the bulk. That made monolayer MoS₂ one of the first clear examples of a two-dimensional material whose optical and electronic properties were not just preserved at atomic thickness, but fundamentally improved by reaching the monolayer limit.

The paradigm shift was that two-dimensional materials could now be understood as a broader electronic-materials platform, not only as graphene-like conductors. A single atomic layer could be semiconducting, optically bright, mechanically flexible, and compatible with electrostatic gating and van der Waals assembly. This opened a practical route to atomically thin light emitters, photodetectors, field-effect transistors, and later heterostructures in which different monolayers could be stacked without the lattice-matching constraints of traditional epitaxy. The paper helped redirect the field toward transition-metal dichalcogenides as a family of materials with band gaps, excitons, spin-orbit coupling, and layer-dependent electronic structure.

Subsequent breakthroughs built directly on this recognition. Monolayer MoS₂ and related dichalcogenides became central systems for studying tightly bound excitons, valley-selective optical excitation, spin-valley coupling, and moiré physics in stacked two-dimensional semiconductors. The result also supplied the materials basis for later demonstrations of ultrathin transistors, flexible optoelectronics, valleytronic devices, and artificial van der Waals heterostructures. In retrospect, the paper mattered because it turned the search for post-graphene two-dimensional materials from a structural question into a functional one: atomic thickness could be used as a design principle for creating electronic and optical behavior absent in the parent bulk crystal.

## Abstract

The electronic properties of ultrathin crystals of molybdenum disulfide consisting of N=1,2,…,6 S-Mo-S monolayers have been investigated by optical spectroscopy. Through characterization by absorption, photoluminescence, and photoconductivity spectroscopy, we trace the effect of quantum confinement on the material's electronic structure. With decreasing thickness, the indirect band gap, which lies below the direct gap in the bulk material, shifts upwards in energy by more than 0.6 eV. This leads to a crossover to a direct-gap material in the limit of the single monolayer. Unlike the bulk material, the MoS₂ monolayer emits light strongly. The freestanding monolayer exhibits an increase in luminescence quantum efficiency by more than a factor of 10⁴ compared with the bulk material.
