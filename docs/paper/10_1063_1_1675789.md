# Reptation of a Polymer Chain in the Presence of Fixed Obstacles

## Why this mattered

De Gennes’s 1971 paper made polymer entanglement into a tractable dynamical problem. Instead of treating a chain in a dense gel as merely slowed by friction, it imposed the topological constraint that the chain cannot cross fixed obstacles and showed that this forces motion into an effectively one-dimensional “tube” pathway. The resulting reptation picture converted an otherwise forbiddingly many-body problem into a scaling theory with sharp predictions: relaxation time growing as \(M^3\), diffusion and mobility falling as \(M^{-2}\), and anomalous monomer motion scaling as \(t^{1/4}\) before full renewal of the chain conformation.

The paradigm shift was that entanglements became physical constraints with calculable consequences, not phenomenological corrections. After this paper, polymer rheology could be organized around tube survival, contour renewal, and the separation of local equilibration from global relaxation. This made it possible to connect microscopic chain motion to macroscopic viscoelasticity in melts, gels, and concentrated solutions, and it gave experimentalists concrete exponents to test by diffusion, scattering, and mechanical relaxation measurements.

Its broader importance lies in how it seeded the tube-model program that dominated later polymer dynamics. The paper explicitly pointed from fixed obstacles to the harder case of unlinked polymer melts, where the obstacles are themselves moving chains. That extension was developed in subsequent work, especially the Doi-Edwards theory of entangled polymer dynamics, which turned reptation into a central framework for understanding stress relaxation, molecular-weight dependence of viscosity, and the nonlinear rheology of polymeric liquids.

## Abstract

We discuss possible motions for one polymer molecule P (of mass M) performing wormlike displacements inside a strongly cross-linked polymeric gel G. The topological requirement that P cannot intersect any of the chains of G is taken into account by a rigorous procedure: The only motions allowed for the chain are associated with the displacement of certain “defects” along the chain. The main conclusions derived from this model are the following: (a) There are two characteristic times for the chain motion: One of them (Td) is the equilibration time for the defect concentration, and is proportional to M2. The other time (Tr) is the time required for complete renewal of the chain conformation, and is proportional to M3. (b) The over-all mobility and diffusion coefficients of the chain P are proportional to M−2. (c) At times t &amp;lt; Tr the mean square displacement of one monomer of P increases only like 〈(rt − r0)2〉 = const t1/4. These results may also turn out to be useful for the (more difficult) problem of entanglement effects in unlinked molten polymers.
