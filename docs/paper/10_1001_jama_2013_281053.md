# World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki

## Why this mattered

The 2013 JAMA publication of the *Declaration of Helsinki* mattered because it restated, in a concise and globally citable form, the ethical architecture that made modern human-subjects research legitimate: scientific value was not enough; research also had to protect dignity, autonomy, health, privacy, informed consent, and the interests of vulnerable groups. Its paradigm shift was not a laboratory technique but a governance one: it treated clinical research as a moral enterprise requiring independent review, risk-benefit assessment, protocol transparency, and obligations to participants before, during, and after a study.

That framework helped make large-scale, international clinical research more possible by giving investigators, ethics committees, journals, regulators, and sponsors a shared reference point for judging trials across borders. In the decades after the original 1964 declaration, this became essential for randomized controlled trials, vaccine studies, genomics, biobanking, data-intensive epidemiology, and global health research, where participants, samples, data, funders, and benefits often cross national and institutional boundaries.

Its influence also lies in what later breakthroughs had to build around: new therapies, precision medicine, pandemic trials, and learning health systems all depend on public trust that human beings are not merely instruments of discovery. The 2013 version’s emphasis on identifiable human material and data anticipated the growing importance of privacy, consent, and accountability in biomedical data science, making the Declaration a continuing foundation for ethically scalable medical innovation rather than a historical code alone.

## Abstract

1. The World Medical Association (WMA) has developed the Declaration of Helsinki as a statement of ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects, including research on identifiable human material and data.
