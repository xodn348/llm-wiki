# Institutionalized Organizations: Formal Structure as Myth and Ceremony

## Why this mattered

Meyer and Rowan’s 1977 paper helped shift organizational theory away from treating formal structure mainly as an efficient response to technical tasks. Its central move was to argue that many organizational forms are adopted because they embody socially authorized “rational” myths: rules, offices, credentials, procedures, and classifications that signal legitimacy in a wider institutional environment. This made it possible to explain why organizations often look formally rational even when their official structures are only loosely connected to everyday work. Bureaucratic complexity, in this view, was not simply evidence of better coordination; it could also be ceremonial conformity to expectations carried by professions, states, laws, and public belief systems.

The paper opened a new analytic space between technical efficiency and symbolic legitimacy. By introducing decoupling as a normal organizational response, Meyer and Rowan gave scholars a vocabulary for studying why organizations can adopt formal policies, evaluation systems, compliance offices, or professional standards while insulating core activity from those structures. That claim changed what counted as an organizational explanation: researchers could now ask how legitimacy, survival, and resource access are produced through conformity to institutionalized rules, not only how internal arrangements improve production or exchange.

Its influence is visible in later neo-institutional theory, especially work on organizational fields, isomorphism, professions, and diffusion. DiMaggio and Powell’s account of coercive, mimetic, and normative isomorphism developed one major consequence of Meyer and Rowan’s argument: organizations become similar because they face shared institutional pressures, not merely because they discover the same efficient solutions. Subsequent studies of schools, hospitals, firms, nonprofits, states, and global policy diffusion drew on this framework to explain the spread of formal structures whose practical effects are ambiguous but whose legitimacy effects are powerful.

## Abstract

Many formal organizational structures arise as reflections of rationalized institutional rules. The elaboration of such rules in modern states and societies accounts in part for the expansion and increased complexity of formal organizational structures. Institutional rules function as myths which organizations incorporate, gaining legitimacy, resources, stability, and enhanced survival prospects. Organizations whose structures become isomorphic with the myths of the institutional environment-in contrast with those primarily structured by the demands of technical production and exchange-decrease internal coordination and control in order to maintain legitimacy. Structures are decoupled from each other and from ongoing activities. In place of coordination, inspection, and evaluation, a logic of confidence and good faith is employed.
