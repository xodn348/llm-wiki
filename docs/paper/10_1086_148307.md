# A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc/s.

## Why this mattered

Penzias and Wilson’s report transformed an instrumental anomaly into evidence for a cosmic relic. The paper was deliberately narrow: it established that the excess 4080 Mc/s antenna temperature was about 3.5 K, isotropic within observational limits, unpolarized, and not seasonal. Those properties made ordinary local explanations increasingly implausible and aligned with the companion interpretation by Dicke, Peebles, Roll, and Wilkinson: the excess noise was the remnant thermal radiation expected from an early hot, dense universe. In that sense, the paper helped shift cosmology from a field dominated by competing large-scale models toward one in which the hot Big Bang had a direct observable signature.

What became newly possible was precision observational cosmology. The cosmic microwave background gave cosmologists a universal background field whose spectrum, isotropy, and later tiny anisotropies could be measured rather than merely inferred from galaxy motions or element abundances. The Penzias-Wilson detection did not by itself map the early universe, but it made the CMB a central empirical object: subsequent experiments could test whether the radiation had a blackbody spectrum, whether it was nearly uniform across the sky, and whether small departures from uniformity preserved information about primordial density fluctuations.

The later breakthroughs followed directly from that opening. COBE confirmed the CMB’s near-perfect blackbody spectrum and detected anisotropies; WMAP and Planck turned those anisotropies into precise estimates of cosmic age, geometry, composition, and fluctuation statistics. The 1965 measurement therefore mattered not only because it supplied decisive evidence against steady-state cosmology, but because it identified the observational fossil record from which modern cosmology would reconstruct the early universe.

## Abstract

Measurements of the effective zenith noise temperature of the 20-foot horn-reflector antenna (Crawford, Hogg, and Hunt 1961) at the Crawford Hill Laboratory, Holmdel, New Jersey, at 4080 Mc/s have yielded a value of about 3.5 K higher than expected. This excess temperature is, within the limits of our observations, isotropic, unpolarized, and free from seasonal variations (July, 1964 - April, 1965). A possible explanation for the observed excess noise temperature is the one given by Dicke, Peebles, Roll, and Wilkinson (1965) in a companion letter in this issue.
