# Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger

## Why this mattered

This paper turned gravitational waves from a century-old prediction of general relativity into an observational tool. Before GW150914, gravitational waves had only been inferred indirectly, most famously from orbital decay in binary pulsars. Abbott et al. reported a direct, high-significance detection in two LIGO interferometers, with a waveform matching the inspiral, merger, and ringdown of two black holes. That made the result more than a confirmation of Einstein’s theory: it showed that spacetime itself could be used as a detector of violent astrophysical events invisible to conventional telescopes.

The detection also created a new empirical population. Stellar-mass black holes in the tens-of-solar-masses range had been known or suspected individually, but this event demonstrated that binary stellar-mass black hole systems exist, merge within the age of the universe, and radiate several solar masses of energy as gravitational waves. It therefore opened a direct way to measure compact-object masses, spins, merger rates, distances, and strong-field relativistic dynamics without relying on electromagnetic emission.

Its larger consequence was the beginning of gravitational-wave astronomy. After this paper, LIGO and Virgo detections rapidly became a catalog rather than a singular event, enabling population studies of black holes and tests of general relativity in the dynamical strong-field regime. The same observational framework soon led to multimessenger astronomy with the 2017 binary neutron-star merger GW170817, where gravitational waves were combined with gamma-ray, optical, and other electromagnetic observations. GW150914 thus marks the point at which compact-object mergers became a routine observational domain rather than a theoretical endpoint.

## Abstract

On September 14, 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC the two detectors of the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory simultaneously observed a transient gravitational-wave signal. The signal sweeps upwards in frequency from 35 to 250 Hz with a peak gravitational-wave strain of 1.0×10(-21). It matches the waveform predicted by general relativity for the inspiral and merger of a pair of black holes and the ringdown of the resulting single black hole. The signal was observed with a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 24 and a false alarm rate estimated to be less than 1 event per 203,000 years, equivalent to a significance greater than 5.1σ. The source lies at a luminosity distance of 410(-180)(+160) Mpc corresponding to a redshift z=0.09(-0.04)(+0.03). In the source frame, the initial black hole masses are 36(-4)(+5)M⊙ and 29(-4)(+4)M⊙, and the final black hole mass is 62(-4)(+4)M⊙, with 3.0(-0.5)(+0.5)M⊙c(2) radiated in gravitational waves. All uncertainties define 90% credible intervals. These observations demonstrate the existence of binary stellar-mass black hole systems. This is the first direct detection of gravitational waves and the first observation of a binary black hole merger.
