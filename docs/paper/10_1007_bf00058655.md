# Bagging predictors

## Why this mattered

Breiman’s *Bagging predictors* mattered because it reframed instability as a resource rather than merely a defect. The paper showed that for learning algorithms whose predictions change substantially under small perturbations of the training set, repeatedly fitting the predictor on bootstrap resamples and aggregating the results could sharply improve accuracy. This made variance reduction a practical, model-agnostic procedure: instead of designing one “best” tree, neural net, or subset-selection model, one could generate many plausible versions and average or vote across them.

The paradigm shift was especially important for decision trees. Single trees were interpretable but notoriously high-variance; bagging made tree-based prediction far more reliable without changing the underlying tree-growing algorithm. That helped establish ensemble learning as a central machine-learning strategy: predictive power could come not from a single refined model, but from the collective behavior of many deliberately varied models.

Bagging also set the stage for later ensemble breakthroughs, most directly random forests, which added randomized feature selection to bagged trees. More broadly, it helped normalize the idea that injecting randomness into training could improve generalization, a principle that reappears across modern machine learning in methods ranging from boosted and randomized ensembles to stochastic training practices in large-scale models. Its lasting importance is that it turned resampling from an inferential tool into a core predictive technology.
