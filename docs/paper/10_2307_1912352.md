# Sample Selection Bias as a Specification Error

## Why this mattered

Heckman’s paper reframed sample selection bias from a nuisance caused by “bad data” into a formal specification problem. The key insight was that when observations enter a sample through a non-random process, the observed outcome equation is missing a term: the conditional expectation of the unobserved determinants of selection. This made selection bias analytically tractable. Instead of treating censored or self-selected samples as inherently unusable, researchers could model the selection rule and correct the outcome equation using a derived control term, later widely known as the inverse Mills ratio.

What became newly possible was credible estimation of behavioral relationships in settings where the outcome is observed only for selected units: wages only for workers, earnings only for program participants, prices only for traded goods, schooling returns only among those choosing education, and so on. The two-step estimator was especially influential because it translated a structural econometric problem into a procedure compatible with ordinary least squares after a first-stage selection model. That practical accessibility helped move empirical economics toward explicit modeling of the data-generating and observation-generating processes, rather than assuming that the available sample was representative by default.

The paper also helped establish a broader paradigm in applied microeconometrics: identification depends not only on the outcome equation but on why some outcomes are observed and others are not. Its logic connects directly to later work on treatment effects, program evaluation, labor supply, missing data, and causal inference under endogenous participation. Subsequent breakthroughs refined, generalized, and sometimes challenged the parametric assumptions of the original Heckman correction, but the central lesson endured: selection is part of the model, and ignoring it can turn empirical regularities into artifacts of who appears in the data.

## Abstract

Sample selection bias as a specification error This paper discusses the bias that results from using non-randomly selected samples to estimate behavioral relationships as an ordinary specification error or «omitted variables» bias. A simple consistent two stage estimator is considered that enables analysts to utilize simple regression methods to estimate behavioral functions by least squares methods. The asymptotic distribution of the estimator is derived.
