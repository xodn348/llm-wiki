# Human Papillomavirus and Survival of Patients with Oropharyngeal Cancer

## Why this mattered

This paper helped turn HPV-positive oropharyngeal squamous-cell carcinoma from a biologic observation into a clinically decisive disease category. Before it, HPV-associated tumors were known to behave differently, but Ang et al. showed in a rigorously analyzed cohort from a randomized treatment trial that tumor HPV status was an independent prognostic factor, not just a proxy for younger age, less smoking, tumor stage, or treatment differences. The survival separation was large: 3-year overall survival was 82.4% for HPV-positive disease versus 57.1% for HPV-negative disease, with a 58% lower adjusted risk of death. That magnitude made it difficult to keep treating “oropharyngeal cancer” as a single prognostic entity.

The immediate paradigm shift was risk stratification. The paper combined HPV status with tobacco exposure, tumor stage, and nodal stage to define low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, making clear that viral etiology and smoking history could organize prognosis more meaningfully than anatomic staging alone. This changed how trials were designed: HPV-positive patients, especially those with limited smoking exposure, became candidates for de-intensification studies aimed at preserving high cure rates while reducing the long-term toxicity of chemoradiotherapy. At the same time, HPV-negative and heavy-smoking-associated disease remained a distinct high-risk population needing different therapeutic strategies.

Its broader importance was that it helped establish HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer as a model of molecularly defined oncology in head and neck cancer. Subsequent staging systems, biomarker-driven trial eligibility, survivorship research, and treatment-intensity debates all built on the premise that cause and tumor biology could redefine prognosis. The paper did not prove that less intensive therapy was safe, and later trials showed that some de-escalation approaches could compromise outcomes, but it made the central question unavoidable: once a cancer subgroup has a markedly different natural history and treatment response, clinical practice and research must stop pretending it is the same disease.

## Abstract

BACKGROUND: Oropharyngeal squamous-cell carcinomas caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) are associated with favorable survival, but the independent prognostic significance of tumor HPV status remains unknown. METHODS: We performed a retrospective analysis of the association between tumor HPV status and survival among patients with stage III or IV oropharyngeal squamous-cell carcinoma who were enrolled in a randomized trial comparing accelerated-fractionation radiotherapy (with acceleration by means of concomitant boost radiotherapy) with standard-fractionation radiotherapy, each combined with cisplatin therapy, in patients with squamous-cell carcinoma of the head and neck. Proportional-hazards models were used to compare the risk of death among patients with HPV-positive cancer and those with HPV-negative cancer. RESULTS: The median follow-up period was 4.8 years. The 3-year rate of overall survival was similar in the group receiving accelerated-fractionation radiotherapy and the group receiving standard-fractionation radiotherapy (70.3% vs. 64.3%; P=0.18; hazard ratio for death with accelerated-fractionation radiotherapy, 0.90; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.72 to 1.13), as were the rates of high-grade acute and late toxic events. A total of 63.8% of patients with oropharyngeal cancer (206 of 323) had HPV-positive tumors; these patients had better 3-year rates of overall survival (82.4%, vs. 57.1% among patients with HPV-negative tumors; P<0.001 by the log-rank test) and, after adjustment for age, race, tumor and nodal stage, tobacco exposure, and treatment assignment, had a 58% reduction in the risk of death (hazard ratio, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.27 to 0.66). The risk of death significantly increased with each additional pack-year of tobacco smoking. Using recursive-partitioning analysis, we classified our patients as having a low, intermediate, or high risk of death on the basis of four factors: HPV status, pack-years of tobacco smoking, tumor stage, and nodal stage. CONCLUSIONS: Tumor HPV status is a strong and independent prognostic factor for survival among patients with oropharyngeal cancer. (ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT00047008.)
