# Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity.

## Why this mattered

Although often indexed through a 1964 *American Sociological Review* review record, the paradigm-shifting work is Goffman’s 1963 monograph *Stigma*. Its importance was to recast stigma from a fixed personal defect into a relational social process: an attribute becomes “spoiling” only within expectations, encounters, and systems of classification. That move made stigma analyzable as interactional work, not merely as prejudice, pathology, or moral failure.

After Goffman, researchers could study how people manage discrediting information: passing, covering, disclosure, secrecy, affiliation with others, and negotiation between “virtual” and “actual” social identity. This gave sociology, social psychology, disability studies, medical sociology, and later sexuality and race scholarship a portable vocabulary for explaining how institutions and everyday encounters produce unequal burdens of self-presentation.

The book’s later influence lies less in a single testable model than in a durable analytic frame. Subsequent work on labeling theory, mental illness, HIV/AIDS, disability rights, queer identity, and “stigma management” extended Goffman’s insight that social exclusion operates through ordinary categories and face-to-face judgments. Later scholars also criticized the framework for underplaying power, history, and collective resistance, but those critiques largely presupposed the conceptual shift Goffman made possible.
