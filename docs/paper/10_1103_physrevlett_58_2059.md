# Inhibited Spontaneous Emission in Solid-State Physics and Electronics

## Why this mattered

Yablonovitch’s paper reframed spontaneous emission from an intrinsic material property into an environmental one. The key shift was to treat the vacuum modes available to an emitter as something that could be engineered in a solid, not merely accepted as part of nature. By proposing a three-dimensional periodic dielectric with a complete electromagnetic band gap, the paper made a strong claim: if no optical states exist at the relevant transition frequency, spontaneous emission can be forbidden. That connected atomic radiative physics to band-structure thinking familiar from solid-state physics.

This mattered because it turned “controlling light” into a materials-design problem. The paper immediately suggested new limits and possibilities for semiconductor lasers, transistors, and solar cells, where unwanted radiative recombination or emission losses constrain performance. More broadly, it helped found the modern field of photonic crystals: artificial structures in which photons, like electrons in a crystal, can have allowed and forbidden energy bands. After this, researchers could design cavities, waveguides, mirrors, and defect states by sculpting the electromagnetic density of states rather than relying only on conventional reflection, absorption, or refraction.

The later breakthroughs followed directly from that change in viewpoint. Photonic band-gap materials enabled high-Q microcavities, inhibited and enhanced emission via local density-of-states control, low-threshold nanolasers, integrated photonic-crystal waveguides, and strong light-matter coupling platforms used in quantum optics and nanophotonics. The paper’s importance is not only that it proposed a specific structure, but that it made spontaneous emission a controllable variable in solid-state devices, opening a route from passive optical materials to engineered photonic environments.

## Abstract

It has been recognized for some time that the spontaneous emission by atoms is not necessarily a fixed and immutable property of the coupling between matter and space, but that it can be controlled by modification of the properties of the radiation field. This is equally true in the solid state, where spontaneous emission plays a fundamental role in limiting the performance of semiconductor lasers, heterojunction bipolar transistors, and solar cells. If a three-dimensionally periodic dielectric structure has an electromagnetic band gap which overlaps the electronic band edge, then spontaneous emission can be rigorously forbidden.
