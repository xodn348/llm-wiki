# A simple method for estimating evolutionary rates of base substitutions through comparative studies of nucleotide sequences

## Why this mattered

Kimura’s 1980 paper mattered because it turned comparative nucleotide sequences into a practical quantitative instrument. Earlier sequence comparisons could count observed differences, but those counts underestimated true evolutionary change because the same site can mutate more than once. Kimura supplied a simple correction that distinguished transitions from transversions, recognizing that these two classes of substitutions often occur at different rates. This made it possible to estimate evolutionary distance and substitution rates from DNA sequences with a model that was still simple enough for broad use.

The shift was methodological and conceptual: molecular evolution could now be studied through explicit stochastic models of sequence change rather than through raw similarity alone. The paper helped make DNA sequence data usable for testing molecular clocks, comparing evolutionary rates across lineages, and building distance-based phylogenies. It also fit naturally with Kimura’s broader neutral theory program, in which many molecular substitutions are treated as probabilistic events accumulating over time.

Its influence is visible in nearly every later generation of molecular phylogenetics and comparative genomics. More elaborate substitution models, maximum-likelihood phylogenetics, Bayesian dating, genome-scale molecular clocks, and modern tests of selection all build on the same premise: observed sequence differences are filtered through an explicit model to infer hidden evolutionary history. Kimura’s two-parameter model was not the final model, but it was one of the clearest steps that made model-based molecular evolution a standard language of biology.
