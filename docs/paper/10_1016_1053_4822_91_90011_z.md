# A three-component conceptualization of organizational commitment

## Why this mattered

Meyer and Allen’s 1991 paper mattered because it changed organizational commitment from a relatively unitary idea into a differentiated psychological construct. Instead of treating commitment mainly as either an attitude toward the organization or a behavioral tendency to remain, they argued that employees may stay because they *want* to stay, *need* to stay, or feel they *ought* to stay. That three-part distinction, later named affective, continuance, and normative commitment, made commitment less like a single score of attachment and more like a map of distinct motivational states.

The shift made new kinds of research possible. Scholars could now ask whether the same management practice strengthened emotional attachment, raised perceived costs of leaving, or created felt obligation, and whether those forms predicted different outcomes such as turnover, performance, absenteeism, citizenship behavior, or well-being. This also gave human resource management a more precise vocabulary: retention was no longer automatically evidence of healthy commitment, because an employee remaining from necessity could mean something quite different from one remaining from identification or loyalty.

Its influence is visible in the later consolidation of commitment research, especially meta-analytic work comparing the antecedents, correlates, and consequences of affective, continuance, and normative commitment. The model became a standard framework for studies of employee retention, psychological contracts, leadership, organizational change, and cross-cultural employment relations. Its lasting contribution was not simply adding two more labels to the field, but making organizational commitment empirically decomposable: researchers and practitioners could distinguish forms of attachment that had previously been blurred together.
