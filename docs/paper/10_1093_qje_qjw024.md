# Measuring Economic Policy Uncertainty*

## Why this mattered

Baker, Bloom, and Davis made economic policy uncertainty measurable at scale. Before this paper, “uncertainty” was widely invoked as a channel through which politics, wars, fiscal conflict, or regulatory change could affect the economy, but it was difficult to track consistently across time and countries. Their newspaper-based EPU index turned a latent concept into a reproducible empirical object, validated against human article readings and shown to spike around identifiable policy-relevant events such as contested elections, wars, 9/11, Lehman’s failure, and the 2011 debt ceiling crisis. That shifted the field from mostly narrative claims about uncertainty to time-series, firm-level, and cross-country tests.

The paper also clarified why policy uncertainty mattered economically: it was not just political noise, but a measurable force associated with higher firm-level stock volatility and weaker investment and employment, especially in sectors exposed to government decisions such as defense, health care, finance, and infrastructure construction. At the macro level, innovations in EPU preceded declines in investment, output, and employment in the United States and in a panel of major economies. This gave researchers, central banks, and policy analysts a practical way to separate policy-related uncertainty from broader financial stress or business-cycle weakness.

Its broader importance was methodological as much as substantive. The paper helped establish text-as-data measurement as a central tool in empirical macroeconomics and political economy: newspapers, later joined by earnings calls, regulatory filings, speeches, and online text, could be used to construct real-time indicators of otherwise unobservable economic forces. Subsequent work on geopolitical risk, trade policy uncertainty, climate policy uncertainty, monetary policy communication, and pandemic-related uncertainty all drew on the same basic breakthrough: systematic textual evidence could make institutional and political ambiguity visible in economic data.

## Abstract

Abstract We develop a new index of economic policy uncertainty (EPU) based on newspaper coverage frequency. Several types of evidence—including human readings of 12,000 newspaper articles—indicate that our index proxies for movements in policy-related economic uncertainty. Our U.S. index spikes near tight presidential elections, Gulf Wars I and II, the 9/11 attacks, the failure of Lehman Brothers, the 2011 debt ceiling dispute, and other major battles over fiscal policy. Using firm-level data, we find that policy uncertainty is associated with greater stock price volatility and reduced investment and employment in policy-sensitive sectors like defense, health care, finance, and infrastructure construction. At the macro level, innovations in policy uncertainty foreshadow declines in investment, output, and employment in the United States and, in a panel vector autoregressive setting, for 12 major economies. Extending our U.S. index back to 1900, EPU rose dramatically in the 1930s (from late 1931) and has drifted upward since the 1960s.
