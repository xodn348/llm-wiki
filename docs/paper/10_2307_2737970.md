# Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison.

## Why this mattered

The 1978 *Eighteenth-Century Studies* item is best understood as a review record for Michel Foucault’s *Discipline and Punish*, translated by Alan Sheridan, rather than the original source of the paradigm shift itself ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2737970)). The work mattered because it changed the study of punishment from a story about legal reform and humanitarian progress into a genealogy of power: the modern prison appeared not simply as a replacement for torture, but as part of a wider “disciplinary” regime that trained bodies, organized time, classified individuals, and made conduct visible.

After Foucault, punishment could be studied as a technology of knowledge and social ordering, not only as law or state violence. The prison became a model for analyzing schools, barracks, hospitals, factories, and bureaucracies, where surveillance and normalization operated without needing spectacular force. This made newly possible a vocabulary for studying power as productive and dispersed: power produced categories, records, habits, and subjects, rather than merely repressing preexisting freedoms.

Its later influence is visible across critical criminology, sociology of punishment, surveillance studies, postcolonial theory, gender studies, and analyses of the carceral state. Subsequent work on governmentality, risk management, biometric identification, algorithmic surveillance, and institutional discipline repeatedly returned to Foucault’s central move: modern control often works by making people legible, comparable, correctable, and self-monitoring.
