# The Scree Test For The Number Of Factors

## Why this mattered

Cattell’s paper mattered because it turned a recurring judgment problem in factor analysis into a practical diagnostic procedure. Before the scree test, researchers had formal criteria for extracting factors, but these often produced either too many factors or depended heavily on arbitrary thresholds. Cattell reframed the task visually: plot eigenvalues in descending order and identify where the curve changes from substantive decline to the shallow “scree” of residual, minor factors. The shift was not a new model of latent structure, but a new way to make factor retention empirically inspectable.

That mattered especially for psychology, education, and the behavioral sciences, where factor analysis was used to infer latent traits from large batteries of tests, ratings, and observations. The scree test made it newly feasible to treat factor retention as a replicable part of exploratory analysis rather than as a hidden technical choice. It helped researchers distinguish broad, interpretable dimensions from statistical noise, supporting more stable work on personality structure, intelligence testing, psychopathology, and survey measurement.

Its influence also extended beyond classical factor analysis. The same intuition behind the scree plot became central to principal component analysis, dimensionality reduction, exploratory data analysis, and later machine-learning workflows: inspect the spectrum, look for an elbow, and decide how much latent structure is worth keeping. Subsequent methods such as parallel analysis, information criteria, cross-validation, and random-matrix approaches made factor selection more formal, but they did not displace Cattell’s core contribution. The scree test gave scientists a simple visual language for deciding when complexity had stopped explaining structure and started fitting residue.
