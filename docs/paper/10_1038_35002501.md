# Biodiversity hotspots for conservation priorities

## Why this mattered

The paper crystallized a practical paradigm for global conservation: instead of treating biodiversity loss as an evenly distributed problem, it argued that conservation priorities should focus on a small set of regions combining exceptional endemism with severe habitat loss. Myers and colleagues identified 25 hotspots that contained a large share of the world’s endemic plant and vertebrate species while retaining only a fraction of their original natural vegetation. This made conservation triage explicit: limited resources could be directed toward places where protecting relatively small areas might prevent disproportionately large losses of evolutionary history.

What changed was not simply the map, but the decision framework. The paper helped make biodiversity prioritization quantitative, spatial, and policy-relevant at a global scale. It gave governments, NGOs, and funders a common language for comparing regions, justifying investment, and building protected-area strategies around measurable conservation return. Subsequent conservation planning, including systematic reserve design, gap analysis, and global biodiversity funding portfolios, built on this shift toward data-driven prioritization.

Its influence also shaped later breakthroughs in conservation science by making clear that biodiversity value, threat, and feasibility had to be analyzed together. Later frameworks refined or challenged hotspot thinking by adding dimensions such as ecosystem services, phylogenetic diversity, climate-change vulnerability, intactness, and social governance, but they did so in relation to the benchmark this paper helped establish. The central legacy is that global conservation became less about cataloging where biodiversity exists and more about deciding, under scarcity and urgency, where action could matter most.
