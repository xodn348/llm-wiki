# Indexing by latent semantic analysis

## Why this mattered

Before this paper, automatic retrieval largely treated words as surface tokens: a document matched a query when they shared enough explicit terms. Deerwester, Dumais, Furnas, Landauer, and Harshman reframed indexing as a problem of recovering hidden structure from the whole term-document matrix. By applying singular-value decomposition and keeping a reduced set of latent dimensions, they showed that retrieval systems could use patterns of word co-occurrence to infer relationships not visible in exact lexical overlap. This made synonymy and relatedness computationally tractable at scale: a query and a document could be close in the latent space even when they did not use the same words.

The paradigm shift was that meaning became something that could be approximated geometrically from distributional evidence. LSA did not “understand” language in a human sense, and its linear algebraic representation was limited by corpus choice, preprocessing, and the fixed low-rank space. But it established a durable template: represent linguistic items as vectors learned from large text collections, compare them by distance or angle, and exploit latent structure rather than hand-built vocabularies alone. That made fully automatic semantic indexing, query expansion, document clustering, and conceptual similarity measurement newly practical.

Its influence runs through later information retrieval and natural language processing. Probabilistic topic models, matrix factorization methods, word embeddings, neural retrieval, and modern dense-vector search all share part of LSA’s core premise: useful semantic structure can be learned from distributional patterns and used for retrieval beyond keyword matching. The 1990 paper therefore marks an early, decisive move from symbolic or lexical indexing toward representation learning for text, a line of work that eventually became central to search engines, recommender systems, and large-scale language models.

## Abstract

A new method for automatic indexing and retrieval is described. The approach is to take advantage of implicit higher-order structure in the association of terms with documents (“semantic structure”) in order to improve the detection of relevant documents on the basis of terms found in queries. The particular technique used is singular-value decomposition, in which a large term by document matrix is decomposed into a set of ca. 100 orthogonal factors from which the original matrix can be approximated by linear combination. Documents are represented by ca. 100 item vectors of factor weights. Queries are represented as pseudo-document vectors formed from weighted combinations of terms, and documents with supra-threshold cosine values are returned. Initial tests find this completely automatic method for retrieval to be promising. © 1990 John Wiley & Sons, Inc.
