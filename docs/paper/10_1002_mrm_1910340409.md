# An investigation of problem factors in ERP selection in KwaZulu-Natal organizations.

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it helped turn “resting” MRI signal from presumed background noise into a measurable source of brain organization. The key move was to show that low-frequency signal fluctuations in an inactive, resting brain were not random: regions of sensorimotor cortex identified during hand-movement fMRI showed temporally correlated fluctuations even when the subject was not performing the task. That made it plausible to infer functional connectivity from spontaneous BOLD-related activity, rather than only from experimentally evoked responses.

After this, fMRI was no longer limited to mapping which regions respond to a stimulus or task. Researchers could ask how distributed brain systems are intrinsically coupled, including in participants who cannot easily perform tasks, such as infants, sedated patients, or clinical populations. This opened the way for resting-state fMRI as a major methodological paradigm: mapping networks, comparing connectivity across individuals and disorders, and studying large-scale brain organization without requiring tightly controlled behavioral paradigms.

The paper’s importance is best understood in relation to later breakthroughs in intrinsic connectivity networks, including the characterization of motor, visual, attention, salience, and default-mode networks. Subsequent work greatly expanded the statistical methods, datasets, and biological interpretation, but the central shift was already present here: spontaneous low-frequency MRI fluctuations could be treated as meaningful evidence of functional brain architecture.

## Abstract

An MRI time course of 512 echo-planar images (EPI) in resting human brain obtained every 250 ms reveals fluctuations in signal intensity in each pixel that have a physiologic origin. Regions of the sensorimotor cortex that were activated secondary to hand movement were identified using functional MRI methodology (FMRI). Time courses of low frequency (< 0.1 Hz) fluctuations in resting brain were observed to have a high degree of temporal correlation (P < 10(-3)) within these regions and also with time courses in several other regions that can be associated with motor function. It is concluded that correlation of low frequency fluctuations, which may arise from fluctuations in blood oxygenation or flow, is a manifestation of functional connectivity of the brain.
