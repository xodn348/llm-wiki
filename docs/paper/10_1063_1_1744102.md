# Free Energy of a Nonuniform System. I. Interfacial Free Energy

## Why this mattered

Cahn and Hilliard’s 1958 paper mattered because it turned an interface from a geometric boundary into a thermodynamic object with internal structure. By adding a composition-gradient penalty, $\kappa(\nabla c)^2$, to the homogeneous free energy density, it gave a continuum free-energy functional for nonuniform systems: one could now calculate interface profiles, interface thicknesses, and interfacial free energies from the same thermodynamic framework used for bulk phases. This was a paradigm shift from treating phase boundaries as sharp dividing surfaces toward treating them as diffuse, spatially resolved regions governed by variational principles.

The paper also made critical behavior of interfaces quantitatively accessible. Its predictions that interface thickness diverges as the critical temperature is approached, and that the interfacial free energy scales as $(T_c - T)^{3/2}$ near $T_c$, linked measurable interfacial properties to the underlying free-energy landscape. That connection made optical measurements, phase-equilibrium data, and continuum thermodynamics part of a single explanatory scheme for nonuniform fluids and mixtures.

Its longer-term importance lies in providing the conceptual foundation for phase-field and diffuse-interface models. The Cahn-Hilliard free energy became the static core behind later dynamical theories of spinodal decomposition, coarsening, pattern formation, and microstructure evolution in alloys, polymers, fluids, and soft matter. Subsequent breakthroughs in computational materials science and mesoscale modeling depended on exactly this move: replacing moving sharp boundaries with fields whose evolution follows from a free-energy functional.

## Abstract

It is shown that the free energy of a volume V of an isotropic system of nonuniform composition or density is given by : NV∫V [f0(c)+κ(▿c)2]dV, where NV is the number of molecules per unit volume, ▿c the composition or density gradient, f0 the free energy per molecule of a homogeneous system, and κ a parameter which, in general, may be dependent on c and temperature, but for a regular solution is a constant which can be evaluated. This expression is used to determine the properties of a flat interface between two coexisting phases. In particular, we find that the thickness of the interface increases with increasing temperature and becomes infinite at the critical temperature Tc, and that at a temperature T just below Tc the interfacial free energy σ is proportional to (Tc−T)32. The predicted interfacial free energy and its temperature dependence are found to be in agreement with existing experimental data. The possibility of using optical measurements of the interface thickness to provide an additional check of our treatment is briefly discussed.
