# Information Systems Success: The Quest for the Dependent Variable

## Why this mattered

DeLone and McLean’s paper mattered because it turned “information systems success” from a loose evaluative phrase into a research construct that could be decomposed, compared, and tested. Before 1992, studies of IS effectiveness often treated success through isolated proxies such as system use, user satisfaction, decision quality, or organizational payoff, making findings difficult to accumulate. By synthesizing prior work into six linked dimensions, the paper reframed success as multidimensional rather than reducible to any single dependent variable.

The paradigm shift was methodological as much as conceptual. The model gave IS researchers a shared vocabulary for asking what kind of success was being measured, at what level of analysis, and through what causal path. This made it possible to compare empirical studies that had previously appeared incommensurable, to design more complete measurement instruments, and to test relationships among technical quality, information quality, user response, and downstream impacts. In that sense, the paper helped move IS evaluation from scattered outcome reporting toward cumulative theory-building.

Its influence is visible in later work on enterprise systems, e-commerce, knowledge management systems, digital platforms, and public-sector IT evaluation, where researchers repeatedly adapted or extended the DeLone-McLean framework to new technologies. The later 2003 model update, which added service quality and consolidated impacts into net benefits, shows why the 1992 paper became foundational: it was not a final metric, but a durable architecture for revising IS success measurement as systems changed. That architecture made subsequent breakthroughs in IS evaluation more comparable, more cumulative, and more explicit about what “success” meant.

## Abstract

A large number of studies have been conducted during the last decade and a half attempting to identify those factors that contribute to information systems success. However, the dependent variable in these studies—I/S success—has been an elusive one to define. Different researchers have addressed different aspects of success, making comparisons difficult and the prospect of building a cumulative tradition for I/S research similarly elusive. To organize this diverse research, as well as to present a more integrated view of the concept of I/S success, a comprehensive taxonomy is introduced. This taxonomy posits six major dimensions or categories of I/S success—SYSTEM QUALITY, INFORMATION QUALITY, USE, USER SATISFACTION, INDIVIDUAL IMPACT, and ORGANIZATIONAL IMPACT. Using these dimensions, both conceptual and empirical studies are then reviewed (a total of 180 articles are cited) and organized according to the dimensions of the taxonomy. Finally, the many aspects of I/S success are drawn together into a descriptive model and its implications for future I/S research are discussed.
