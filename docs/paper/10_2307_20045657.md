# Making Democracy Work: Civic Traditions in Modern Italy

## Why this mattered

The work usually cited as *Making Democracy Work* is Putnam, Leonardi, and Nanetti’s 1993 book; the *Foreign Affairs* item was Fritz Stern’s review of it. Its importance lay in turning “civic culture” from a broad Tocquevillian intuition into a comparative empirical program. By exploiting Italy’s post-1970 regional governments as a quasi-natural comparison, Putnam argued that formally similar democratic institutions performed very differently depending on inherited patterns of civic association, horizontal trust, and reciprocity.

That reframed democratic performance as more than constitutional design or economic modernization. After this work, scholars and policymakers could ask not only whether institutions were well designed, but whether societies possessed the social infrastructure needed to make them function. The book helped make “social capital” a central concept in political science, development studies, and public policy, shaping later debates on civil society, institutional quality, decentralization, and democratic consolidation.

Its influence also provoked the next generation of breakthroughs and critiques. Later research tested whether social capital causes good institutions or is itself produced by them, whether civic associations can have exclusionary or anti-democratic effects, and how historical persistence operates through measurable mechanisms. In that sense, the book mattered not because its causal claims ended the debate, but because it made civic life, institutional performance, and long-run historical legacies analytically inseparable.
