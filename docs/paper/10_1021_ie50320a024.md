# RESISTANCE OF SOLID SURFACES TO WETTING BY WATER

## Why this mattered

Wenzel’s 1936 paper mattered because it changed wetting from a property of an ideal, smooth chemical surface into a problem of real surface geometry. The key move was to recognize that roughness changes the effective solid-liquid contact area and therefore amplifies the intrinsic wetting tendency of a material: hydrophilic surfaces become more wettable, and hydrophobic surfaces become more water-repellent when roughened. This idea, later formalized as the Wenzel relation, gave researchers a quantitative way to connect microscopic texture with macroscopic contact angle, making surface roughness an active design variable rather than an experimental nuisance.

That shift made it possible to engineer wetting behavior by patterning, etching, coating, or otherwise texturing surfaces, rather than relying only on chemical composition. It became foundational for later work on water-repellent fabrics, waterproof coatings, corrosion resistance, adhesion control, printing, detergency, and, much later, micro- and nanoscale surface engineering. The paper’s influence is also visible in the way it set up the central distinction between homogeneous wetting on rough surfaces, described by Wenzel, and composite wetting states involving trapped air, developed soon afterward in the Cassie-Baxter framework.

In retrospect, Wenzel’s contribution was paradigm-shifting because it supplied one of the first durable bridges between surface thermodynamics and surface morphology. Modern superhydrophobicity, self-cleaning materials, anti-icing surfaces, lab-on-chip devices, and biomimetic studies of lotus leaves and insect cuticles all depend on the premise that topography can control liquid behavior. Even when later research showed that real wetting states can be metastable, heterogeneous, or dynamically complex, Wenzel’s paper remained the reference point from which those refinements departed.
