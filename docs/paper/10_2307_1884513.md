# A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth

## Why this mattered

Solow’s 1956 paper mattered because it turned economic growth from a largely unstable, knife-edge problem into a tractable model of long-run equilibrium dynamics. Earlier Harrod-Domar growth theory emphasized the difficulty of maintaining balanced growth when saving, capital requirements, and labor-force growth failed to align. Solow’s central move was to introduce substitutable capital and labor in an aggregate production function, allowing factor proportions to adjust. That change made it possible to show how an economy could converge toward a steady-state growth path without requiring exact parameter coincidences.

The paper also separated the sources of growth in a way that became foundational: capital accumulation could explain movements toward a steady state, but sustained growth in output per worker required technological progress. This distinction created the conceptual basis for growth accounting, especially the later “Solow residual,” where unexplained productivity growth became measurable rather than merely assumed. After Solow, economists could ask quantitatively how much growth came from capital deepening, labor-force growth, and productivity change, making long-run macroeconomics more empirical and more directly connected to national income data.

Its influence extended through later breakthroughs by defining the benchmark that subsequent theories had to explain or overturn. Endogenous growth theory, human-capital models, innovation-based growth, and institutions-oriented development research all began from problems exposed by the Solow framework: why technological progress differs across economies, why convergence is incomplete, and what determines the productivity term treated as external in the original model. In that sense, the paper’s paradigm shift was not only a new model of growth, but a durable research program for explaining what the model deliberately left outside.
