# Comprehensive molecular characterization of human colon and rectal cancer

## Why this mattered

This paper mattered because it turned colorectal cancer from an organ-defined disease into a molecularly stratified disease at genome scale. By integrating exome sequencing, copy number, methylation, mRNA, microRNA, and low-coverage whole-genome data across a large cohort, TCGA showed that colon and rectal cancers were genomically more similar than their anatomical distinction had implied, while also separating out a biologically distinct hypermutated subgroup. That distinction clarified that microsatellite instability and MLH1 silencing explained many, but not all, hypermutated tumors, with mismatch-repair and **POLE** alterations defining additional routes to extreme mutation burden.

The study also expanded the colorectal cancer map beyond the canonical **APC–KRAS–TP53–SMAD4** model. It identified significantly mutated genes such as **ARID1A**, **SOX9**, and **FAM123B**, recurrent amplifications including **ERBB2** and **IGF2**, and pathway-level patterns implicating WNT signaling, PI3K signaling, TGF-β signaling, and MYC-directed transcriptional programs. The paradigm shift was not simply that more mutations were catalogued; it was that colorectal carcinoma could be read as an interconnected set of altered pathways, some prognostic, some mechanistic, and some therapeutically actionable.

After this paper, large-scale multi-omic tumor atlases became a practical foundation for colorectal cancer classification, biomarker discovery, and trial design. Its recognition of hypermutation and mismatch-repair biology helped frame later clinical breakthroughs in immune checkpoint therapy for MSI-high and mismatch-repair-deficient cancers, while its identification of targetable amplifications supported precision-oncology approaches such as HER2-directed strategies in selected colorectal tumors. More broadly, it showed that cancer genomics could move from descriptive mutation lists to clinically meaningful molecular taxonomies, helping set the template for subsequent pan-cancer analyses and biomarker-driven oncology.

## Abstract

To characterize somatic alterations in colorectal carcinoma, we conducted a genome-scale analysis of 276 samples, analysing exome sequence, DNA copy number, promoter methylation and messenger RNA and microRNA expression. A subset of these samples (97) underwent low-depth-of-coverage whole-genome sequencing. In total, 16% of colorectal carcinomas were found to be hypermutated: three-quarters of these had the expected high microsatellite instability, usually with hypermethylation and MLH1 silencing, and one-quarter had somatic mismatch-repair gene and polymerase ε (POLE) mutations. Excluding the hypermutated cancers, colon and rectum cancers were found to have considerably similar patterns of genomic alteration. Twenty-four genes were significantly mutated, and in addition to the expected APC, TP53, SMAD4, PIK3CA and KRAS mutations, we found frequent mutations in ARID1A, SOX9 and FAM123B. Recurrent copy-number alterations include potentially drug-targetable amplifications of ERBB2 and newly discovered amplification of IGF2. Recurrent chromosomal translocations include the fusion of NAV2 and WNT pathway member TCF7L1. Integrative analyses suggest new markers for aggressive colorectal carcinoma and an important role for MYC-directed transcriptional activation and repression. The Cancer Genome Atlas consortium reports on their genome-wide characterization of somatic alterations in colorectal cancer; in addition to revealing a remarkably consistent pattern of genomic alteration, with 24 genes being significantly mutated, the study identifies new targets for therapeutic intervention and suggests an important role for MYC-directed transcriptional activation and repression.
