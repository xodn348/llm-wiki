# The Commitment-Trust Theory of Relationship Marketing

## Why this mattered

Morgan and Hunt’s paper mattered because it gave relationship marketing a general theoretical core at a moment when marketing was moving beyond discrete, transaction-centered exchange. Rather than treating long-term buyer-seller ties, channel partnerships, service relationships, and internal marketing as separate phenomena, the paper proposed that successful relational exchange depends centrally on two mediating constructs: commitment and trust. This reframed relationship marketing from a loose managerial slogan into a testable theory of how cooperation, acquiescence, reduced propensity to leave, functional conflict, and uncertainty reduction arise in exchange networks.

The paper’s paradigm shift was methodological as well as conceptual. By modeling commitment and trust as key mediating variables, Morgan and Hunt made it possible to compare relationship-marketing explanations against rival models that omitted those mediators. Their empirical test among automobile tire retailers helped establish that relationship quality could be studied through latent constructs and causal pathways, not merely through satisfaction, loyalty, or repeated purchase behavior. This gave later researchers a reusable architecture for studying business-to-business marketing, channels, services, sales relationships, supply-chain partnerships, and customer relationship management.

Subsequent work on customer loyalty, service-dominant logic, relationship quality, brand communities, and CRM systems repeatedly drew on the idea that durable market performance depends on relational states rather than isolated transactions. The paper did not create every later concept in relationship marketing, but it helped consolidate the field around a vocabulary and model that could travel across contexts. Its lasting influence lies in making trust and commitment measurable strategic assets, thereby connecting interpersonal and interorganizational exchange theory to modern research on retention, cooperation, customer lifetime value, and networked marketing ecosystems.

## Abstract

Relationship marketing—establishing, developing, and maintaining successful relational exchanges—constitutes a major shift in marketing theory and practice. After conceptualizing relationship marketing and discussing its ten forms, the authors (1) theorize that successful relationship marketing requires relationship commitment and trust, (2) model relationship commitment and trust as key mediating variables, (3) test this key mediating variable model using data from automobile tire retailers, and (4) compare their model with a rival that does not allow relationship commitment and trust to function as mediating variables. Given the favorable test results for the key mediating variable model, suggestions for further explicating and testing it are offered.
